Narrator: Huang: the three new facts
Narrator: Jensen Huang resigned on November 11 after 33 years, and the full record—the resignation accepted that day, the Form 8-K, Colette Kress as interim chief executive, Harvey C. Jones as first chair, the Megaspeed allegation held open as unproven—stands in my report on the fall of Jensen Huang and the repricing of Nvidia. Three facts have landed since that report published, and they are the whole update.
Narrator: The earnings call happened. Nvidia reported its third-quarter fiscal 2027 results on Tuesday, November 17, on the schedule set before the resignation, with Kress running the call and still holding the finance job. The first public test the market had circled is a delivered fact now. The board's search for a permanent chief executive runs on with the interim holding both jobs.
Narrator: The price of the fall depends on the window. The Wall Street Journal and CNN measured a drop of more than 10 percent at the open on November 12. The Associated Press put the opening-bell fall at 14 percent before the shares stabilized. Le Monde priced the after-hours stock at about $159, roughly a third below its early-October peak, with more than two trillion dollars of market value gone. The range is the honest figure: every measure anyone published was violent.
Narrator: Google moved into the vacuum two days before it opened. On November 9 Google expanded its eighth-generation TPU line with two systems. TPU 8tx pairs TPU 8t silicon with fifth-generation AMD EPYC host processors and hardware confidential computing, aimed at governments and regulated industries whose data must stay sealed while a model processes it. TPU 8i Max packs two TPU 8i chips into one package and doubles the compute, the on-chip memory, and the memory bandwidth, built for agents reasoning across massive context windows. That makes four new TPU systems in a single year. Barron's framed the launch against an Nvidia "grappling with an investigation" into the foreign-intelligence reporting around its chief executive. The timing needs no embellishment. The challenger shipped its broadest hardware line in the same week the champion lost its founder.
Narrator: Two proofs in one day
Narrator: This story breaks the terms of Chris' question and belongs in the record anyway. It is an Anthropic story: the mathematician works there and the proof came out of Claude. Excluded by the rules of the question, unavoidable in an honest account of the week. On Friday, November 20, Levent Alpöge, a number theorist at Anthropic, posted a four-page note on his personal website: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis." The hypothesis dates to 1859, when Bernhard Riemann proposed that the nontrivial zeros of the zeta function all lie on a single line, a claim about the hidden order of the prime numbers that has resisted proof for 167 years and carries a million-dollar Clay Millennium Prize. Alpöge says the same method settles the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, the wider statement covering a family of related functions.
Narrator: The proof arrived in an unusual vessel. There is no conventional paper. Alpöge released the argument as a formalization in Lean, a proof-checking language, split into a fourteen-part archive. He says a full written version would run extremely long, and he states plainly that he has not mastered all the territory the argument crosses. He has worked with Claude on the problem since October 6, with several key steps dated November 15. He consulted Dario Amodei before publishing and acknowledged that the timing invites cynicism: Anthropic's shares began trading on Nasdaq the day before, November 19.
Narrator: Lean changes what a claim means. A formal proof either compiles under the checker's kernel or it fails, and any expert can run the same files. Andrew Sutherland of MIT compiled Alpöge's archive himself and said: "The Lean proof has been compiled and independently rechecked by the Lean kernel with only the three standard axioms... Honestly the chance that anything is fishy here is approximately zero." One check remains: the comparator run, which verifies that the formal statement in the code is the Riemann Hypothesis itself, with no symbol quietly redefined along the way. Scientific American puts that check at a matter of hours. The Clay prize moves at institutional speed and requires formal publication, so the million dollars will wait regardless of what the kernel says this weekend.
Narrator: Then the day doubled. Hours after Alpöge's note, Ravi Vakil, president of the American Mathematical Society, released a second claimed proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, this one written entirely by a human: Alessandro Masullo, an independent mathematician whose draft had circulated under private vetting for months, with Stanford's Brian Conrad among those reading it. Vakil's circle had planned a year of quiet polishing at Stanford. They rushed the draft into daylight so the human result would stand beside the machine's announcement on the same day. Masullo's proof travels the old road: no Lean archive, no kernel verdict, months of peer review ahead. By Friday night the oldest open problem in mathematics had two claimants, one whose argument a computer had already checked line by line, and one whose argument the profession has barely begun to read.
Narrator: The house gets hands
Narrator: The third story is the oldest one in the pile. The automated house is fifty years old, and every era of it stopped at the same wall. X10, from 1975, sent signals over a home's power lines to flip relays blind: the module clicked at seven in the evening whether the lamp worked or not, and nothing reported back. The ambient era gave the house senses. Ring put eyes on the door, Alexa and Google Home put ears in every room, and the house learned to announce that the front door stood open and to reorder detergent. It could not close the door. The industry calls that gap the last-inch problem: perfect information, no hands. The luxury tier wired around it. Crestron and Lutron systems put a nervous system in the walls at fifty thousand to a quarter million dollars, programmed by dealers, and a house so equipped could dim every light to a chosen warmth and still leave a wet towel lying on the bathroom floor.
Narrator: Physical AI abandons the house as the unit of automation. The machine walks in through the front door and uses the home as it stands: the ordinary coffee maker, the standard deadbolt, the broom in the closet. The brains are vision-language-action models paired with world models—NVIDIA's Cosmos and GR00T line is the stack named most often in the reporting—which split the work between a slow reasoning layer that plans and a fast reflex layer that moves, trained in simulation and on first-person video of humans doing chores. NVIDIA shipped new physical-AI models in January, Cosmos Transfer 2.5 and Predict 2.5, and advanced the Isaac platform in October. The field's direction sits in that architecture: intelligence that predicts the next physical moment and acts on it.
Narrator: The 2026 ledger, kept honest, is short. Exactly one humanoid has a consumer order page. 1X's NEO costs $20,000 to own or $499 a month, held with a $200 refundable deposit, with US deliveries starting in 2026. Preorders opened on October 28, 2025, and 1X says its first year of production capacity—ten thousand units and more, from a Hayward factory rated at ten thousand a year—sold out within five days. The caveat belongs in the same breath: NEO is not autonomous in 2026. Scheduled sessions put a remote human operator in the loop for tasks the AI has not mastered, gated by owner approval, with designated no-go zones and faces blurred before video leaves the house. The early buyer purchases a machine and enrolls in its education at the same time.

Tesla's Optimus is not for sale at any price: early production began at Fremont in October on a line designed for a million units a year, more than a thousand Gen 3 machines work inside Tesla's own factories, and deliveries to outside customers are targeted around the end of 2027, industrial buyers first.
