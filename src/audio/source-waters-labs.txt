Narrator: The first domino: one lab, one walkout
Narrator: In 1947, at Bell Labs, John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley invented the transistor. The next year Claude Shannon published information theory and Richard Hamming his error-correcting codes. Ken Thompson built Unix there in 1969, Dennis Ritchie wrote C in 1973, and Bjarne Stroustrup started C++ in 1983, the lab's own computing timeline records. That's the hardware and the software of the modern world from one building's research staff.
Narrator: Shockley took the transistor west and opened his own shop in Mountain View. On September 18, 1957, eight of his researchers resigned on the same day: Julius Blank, Victor Grinich, Jean Hoerni, Eugene Kleiner, Jay Last, Gordon Moore, Robert Noyce, and Sheldon Roberts. Sherman Fairchild lent them $1.38 million, a banker named Arthur Rock helped arrange it, and Fairchild Semiconductor was formed that day, the record of the "traitorous eight" shows. Moore and Noyce left Fairchild to found Intel in 1968, with Rock leading the money, as he later did for Apple.
Narrator: Then the dominoes ran on their own. The Computer History Museum's history of the "Fairchildren" notes that a 1986 family tree traced 126 semiconductor companies directly to Fairchild, and that a 2014 count by Endeavor found 92 public companies descended from it, worth about $2.1 trillion. That's the clearest example anywhere of what Chris means by source waters. Eight engineers walking out of one man's lab built the valley's chip industry, its venture capital, and its habit of leaving to start the next thing.
Narrator: PARC invented the office. Other people shipped it.
Narrator: Xerox opened the Palo Alto Research Center in 1970. Its researchers brought the Alto personal computer to life on April 1, 1973; built Ethernet, whose basic patents belonged to Bob Metcalfe and David Boggs; and printed through Ethernet on EARS, the first laser printer, the same year, IEEE Spectrum's oral history records. Metcalfe went on to found 3Com. In December 1979 Steve Jobs and a group of Apple engineers visited to see the Alto's graphical interface, after Xerox had bought a small stake in Apple, the Computer History Museum notes. The lesson PARC teaches is the one every corporate lab still lives with: the lab is the spring, and the river often runs through somebody else's company.
Narrator: Skunk Works and JPL: small teams, hard deadlines, very loud tests
Narrator: In 1943 Lockheed's chief research engineer, Kelly Johnson, pulled together a small team of engineers and mechanics to build a jet fighter. The XP-80 rolled out 143 days later, 37 days ahead of schedule, and the team became Skunk Works, which went on to the U-2, the SR-71, and the F-117 stealth fighter. Johnson's motto was "be quick, be quiet and be on time." Every company that sets up a secret team in a corner of the building and calls it a skunkworks is copying a Burbank shop from the war.
Narrator: The Jet Propulsion Laboratory began on Halloween morning in 1936, when Caltech graduate student Frank Malina, Jack Parsons, Ed Forman, and a few friends fired a rocket motor in the dry Arroyo Seco above Pasadena, JPL's own history recounts. The Army sponsored the work, JPL built Explorer 1, America's first satellite, launched in January 1958, and the lab moved to NASA that year. Caltech still manages it, with about 5,500 people. A graduate student and a few amateur rocketeers testing an engine in a dry gully is the purest form of a source.
Narrator: The government's springs: DARPA, the national labs, and the CIA's venture fund
Narrator: The Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency mostly funds other people's labs, which makes it easy to miss. Its 2007 Urban Challenge, covered in Part 1, staffed a decade of self-driving companies from one Pittsburgh team. Its Personalized Assistant that Learns program paid for CALO, an SRI International project with researchers from several universities, Carnegie Mellon among them. SRI spun off Siri in 2007 with $24 million in funding, and Apple bought it in April 2010 and put it on the iPhone 4S in October 2011, SRI's own case study says. The voice in your phone started as a defense research contract.
Narrator: The Department of Energy runs 17 national laboratories with about 40,000 scientists and staff. In November 2025 an executive order launched the Genesis Mission to turn them into a single AI platform for science, with the stated goal of doubling the productivity of American science and engineering within a decade. That makes the national labs the spring to watch for AI built for physics, chemistry, and materials.
Narrator: Chris asked about the NSA, the NRO, the CIA, and the DIA. None of them publish where their alumni go, and I won't guess. The one public window is In-Q-Tel, the nonprofit venture firm the CIA set up in 1999. In 2003 it invested in Keyhole, whose 3D globe became Google Earth in 2004. It backed Palantir and the weapons maker Anduril early, invests roughly $100 million in taxpayer money a year, and has helped launch more than 800 companies, Fortune reported. In-Q-Tel's portfolio page is the closest thing to a published map of what the intelligence community wants built next.
Narrator: Tel Aviv: the army as a founder school
Narrator: Israel runs the most deliberate founder pipeline in the world, and it runs through the military. The four founders of the cloud security company Wiz, Assaf Rappaport, Ami Luttwak, Yinon Costica, and Roy Reznik, met while serving in Israel's elite intelligence Unit 8200. Google closed its $32 billion all-cash purchase of Wiz in March 2026, the largest in Google's history, Dealroom reported, two years after Wiz turned down $23 billion.
Narrator: Beside 8200 sits Talpiot, an elite military technology program started in 1979, whose classes began at 25 cadets and later grew to 50 or 60. A study of about 50 years of Talpiot graduates by Omer Doron and Stanford's Ilya Strebulaev found that about a quarter became entrepreneurs, about 3 percent founded a company worth more than $1 billion, and Talpiot alumni founded about 10 percent of Israel's unicorns and about 30 percent of its decacorns, Globes reported. For comparison, six in every thousand Stanford MBAs founded a unicorn. Wiz's Rappaport, Luttwak, and Costica are Talpiot alumni. Chris asked how much of tech is Tel Aviv tech. By founders per head, more than anywhere else, and by design.
Narrator: Ukraine and China: wartime springs
Narrator: Ukraine has turned a war into an accelerator. The government set up Brave1 in 2023 as a defense technology cluster. By November 2026 it counted about 2,500 companies and more than 5,000 defense products, had awarded more than 1,000 grants, and its Brave1 Market had taken orders for more than 903,000 items worth $1.39 billion from 955 companies. Its Dataroom, built with the Ministry of Defence, the armed forces, military intelligence researchers, and Palantir, now lets more than 100 Ukrainian companies train AI models on battlefield data, starting with spotting and intercepting Shahed drones. No university has a dataset like that, and no peacetime lab gets feedback that fast.
Narrator: China builds the pipeline on purpose too. Its "Seven Sons of National Defence," universities run directly by the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, supply about three quarters of the graduates its state-owned defense companies hire, Georgetown's Center for Security and Emerging Technology found in a study of 2019 hiring data. A later CSET review of 2,857 AI-related defense contract notices from 2023 and 2024 found the old defense giants leading, with a newer set of private companies and research institutes playing a consequential role. Russia publishes nothing comparable that I could verify, so I'm leaving it out rather than guessing.
Narrator: Companies are springs too: the mafias
Narrator: Some companies produce founders the way Fairchild did. In 2007 Fortune photographed the "PayPal Mafia" in gangster costume. Its members founded or built Palantir, SpaceX, YouTube, Yelp, Yammer, Affirm, and Founders Fund, among others, and Elon Musk bought control of Tesla, the record shows.
Narrator: The current version is OpenAI. TechCrunch's February 2026 count of startups founded by OpenAI alumni lists 18, including Anthropic, which Dario and Daniela Amodei started in 2021; Ilya Sutskever's Safe Superintelligence, which has raised $2 billion; Mira Murati's Thinking Machines Lab; Aravind Srinivas' Perplexity; Andrej Karpathy's Eureka Labs; and Liam Fedus' Periodic Labs, with a $300 million seed round. If you want to know where the next wave of model companies comes from, watch who leaves the current ones.
Narrator: Where the founders and the CEOs come from
Narrator: Chris drew the line between the people who build things and the people who run them, and the data agrees there are two different springs.
Narrator: For founders, PitchBook's 2026 count of undergraduate alumni who founded venture-backed companies between 2015 and August 2026, as Poets&Quants published it, puts UC Berkeley first with 2,380 founders, then Stanford, Harvard, Cornell, MIT, Penn, Waterloo, Michigan, Tel Aviv University, and UT Austin. Stanford's founders have raised $252.7 billion, and Penn's, sixth by count, top the whole list at $309.5 billion. Waterloo jumped 11 places to seventh, the first Canadian school in the top ten. Of the top 100 schools, 62 are American, eight Canadian, eight Indian, and six Israeli; Israel's Technion is twelfth.
Narrator: The CEOs who run the biggest companies come from somewhere much plainer. Among the 20 largest Fortune 500 companies in 2023, only one chief executive, Amazon's Andy Jassy, went to an Ivy League college as an undergraduate, 14 went to public universities, and three quarters held an MBA or another graduate degree, Fortune reported, drawing on a researcher who has tracked their schools since 1999. Big tech adds an Indian spring: Sundar Pichai studied at IIT Kharagpur, and Anil Chakravarthy, who becomes Adobe's chief executive on December 1, studied at IIT (BHU) Varanasi before a doctorate at MIT, Business Today notes. Satya Nadella came through Manipal and the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee. Builders come out of Berkeley and Stanford and Unit 8200. Runners come out of state schools, engineering institutes, and business school.
Narrator: How to watch these springs
Narrator: Watch the walkouts. The traitorous eight, the PayPal alumni, and the OpenAI alumni all announced themselves by leaving. When a group leaves one lab together, the next company is usually already incorporated.
Narrator: Read In-Q-Tel's portfolio and Brave1 Market. Both are public lists of what the most demanding customers in the world are buying before anyone else does.
Narrator: Follow the DOE labs' Genesis Mission partnerships. The companies that get access to national lab data and machines will train the scientific models nobody else can.
Narrator: Track the demo days with a military customer. Ukraine's battlefield tests and DARPA's challenges compress years of feedback into months.
Narrator: Count founders per head, not founders. Berkeley produces the most founders. Talpiot produces the most per graduate. The second number tells you where the water is cleanest.
