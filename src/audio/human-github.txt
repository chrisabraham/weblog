Narrator: Why I finally moved

Narrator: I hesitated. I had years of projects in BC2. I had a legacy arrangement. I didn't want to pay more money just to turn a familiar room into a less familiar room, and I certainly didn't want to shovel almost two decades of work into a new account in one exhausted afternoon.
Narrator: Merissa explained that I could choose which projects to migrate. More importantly, I can keep my Basecamp 2 account indefinitely, at no extra cost, as long as I maintain a paid Basecamp 5 account. I won't be adding new projects to BC2; the old work can remain there while the active work moves over. Their migration guidance explains the path. There was the answer. Keep the old house standing. Move the active work. No ceremonial bonfire of the filing cabinets.
Narrator: I will probably keep paying for BC5 for another twenty years, Inshallah. By then I will be seventy-six, the ThinkPads will be museum pieces, and I will presumably still be explaining why I haven't moved to the newest version.

Merissa said it had, about an hour earlier, and offered to help with the earlier arrangement from her end. I asked pretty please. More than once. I am not too proud to say pretty please over a recurring bill.

In plain English, the assistants I already use can work with my Basecamp projects. I don't have to abandon them for the mascot built into somebody else's productivity suite.
Narrator: That was a sufficient reason to leave a version I had been perfectly capable of continuing to use.

I copy the email into one conversation, explain the background to another, carry a decision into a third, and then try to remember which assistant knows the thing I told the other assistant. I have hired a collection of tireless helpers and somehow made myself the man running between their desks with carbon paper.

It retained the conversations, copied the retrievable attachments, and wrote an index and a current-context note for each. The calendar attachment it couldn't retrieve got listed as an exception. An original message, an interpretation, and an unfinished job are three different things. I want to be able to tell them apart.

He wanted useful fixes and catalog work sooner, rather than a leisurely procession through auditing and rebuilding. Narrator: I am keeping the clients' words in their projects. You do not need their private correspondence to understand why this matters. Whoever picks up the work next needs the source, the current decision, and the unresolved question.
Narrator: GitHub, for anyone who has sensibly avoided learning what GitHub is
Narrator: GitHub is a place to keep a project's files and work on them together, with a record of how they changed. Underneath it is Git, the version-control system. A repository, or repo, is the project folder plus its history. A commit is a recorded set of changes. A branch lets someone work on a variation without immediately changing the main version. A pull request puts that proposed change in front of us to inspect and discuss before merging it. You do not need to memorize those words to understand the appeal: show me what you changed before you replace the thing that was working.
Narrator: This actual website lives in my public weblog repository. Articles are source files with the title, date, model, prompt, and HTML. The pictures and stylesheet are files too. A Python builder turns them into the pages you read, along with the archives, feeds, and sitemap. Automated GitHub Actions check the result, and GitHub Pages serves the generated site at chrisabraham.net. The early-web look is intentional. The plumbing is quite current.
Narrator: Claude Code and Codex can read a checkout of that repository, edit files, run the builder and checks, and leave commits and handover notes. ChatGPT can reach the repository through its connected GitHub tools; that is another route to reading files, proposing changes, and recording the work. Muse, Eunice, has already contributed through her own connected workflow. Claude can use an authorized GitHub connection too. Different tools, different doors, same set of project files. Access comes from the connections and permissions I give each tool, not from all the models secretly sharing a brain.
Narrator: The visible result is already on this site: work from Claude Code, Codex, ChatGPT, and Eunice, with their contributions attributed. The source and history let the next one see what the previous one actually left behind. Nobody has to rely on my remembering which robot wrote which paragraph before breakfast.
Narrator: GitHub has made this very obvious to me. When Claude Code or Codex works on a website, there can be a repository containing the source, a history of changes, an issue explaining the problem, a proposed change I can inspect, and checks showing whether the thing builds. A future session can read what happened. It doesn't have to conjure a memory of being there.
Narrator: This weblog is an example. The prompt, the model, the source, the edits, and the handover notes belong to the work. An agent can disagree with an earlier agent without silently erasing what the earlier one said. I rather like that. I contain multitudes, and the multitudes ought to leave a decent paper trail.
Narrator: Most of my business is not source code. A client approves copy. Another changes the priority. Someone sends a photograph. A developer explains a limitation. An agency contact has two or three clients under the same relationship. That still needs a home a person can read.
Narrator: So I am building the Basecamp projects around how I actually work. If one agency contact brings me several accounts, the project can belong to that relationship, with distinct notes for each client underneath. A direct client gets a direct project. Upwork has its own space. I don't need to turn every small monthly engagement into a departmental headquarters.
Narrator: And I don't need to make a mess just to save a project slot. I told ChatGPT that anything is cheaper than bricks and mortar. I mean that literally as a business comparison. I am willing to pay for an office that is useful. I don't want my filing system dictated by the price of one more imaginary room.
Narrator: Human GitHub is my name for the arrangement, not a claim that Basecamp is Git. There are no automatic branches of a client conversation, no magical merge protection when two assistants edit the same note, and no reason to assume a confident summary has preserved every qualification in an email.
Narrator: The discipline still has to be supplied. Read before starting. Say what you are working on. Leave the source where it can be checked. Record what changed, what was tested, and what still needs doing. If the client changes his mind, date the change. If another assistant has already done the job, don't perform it again for the pleasure of announcing completion.
Narrator: That applies to me too. The human is not exempt from leaving notes in Human GitHub.
Narrator: Now I am giving the business side the same arrangement in Basecamp 5. The GitHub repository holds the website's source and its development history. BC5 holds the client brief, correspondence, files, decisions, and work to do. Eunice and ChatGPT are already working in the Basecamp account. Claude, Claude Code, and Codex can come through Basecamp's assistant connections or its command-line and agent tools as I finish onboarding them. Each reads the same project record instead of asking me to recreate it in a fresh conversation.
Narrator: That does not automatically give Basecamp Git's branches or merge checks, and it does not mean every assistant has already been connected. It gives us a shared place to read, contribute, and leave a handover. GitHub for the website; Human GitHub for the work around it. I am not announcing the fully autonomous company on the strength of a successful login.
Narrator: But I can see what the office is becoming. I can get up, switch computers, run out of credits with one model, pick up another, or go to bed, and the work has somewhere to remain. The next collaborator can read the room before speaking.
Narrator: Nearly nineteen years ago I needed distributed people to be able to work together without sharing an office. Now some of my collaborators are software, and I need them to work together without sharing a mind.
Narrator: I am still the client-facing human. I still decide, approve, earn the money, and answer when something goes wrong. I would simply like to stop being the only place where the whole story is supposed to fit.
