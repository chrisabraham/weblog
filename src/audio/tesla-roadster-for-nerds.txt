Narrator: On October 15, 2026, at SpaceX's rocket test site in McGregor, Texas, outside Waco, Tesla finally showed the second-generation Roadster, nearly nine years after it rolled out of a Semi trailer to the Beastie Boys' "Sabotage" on November 16, 2017. The reveal had already slipped from April 1 and from an October 1 date that rain washed out. What came out of the dark this time was a car that drove up an inverted ramp, spun, landed, and then hopped over a Cybercab, a Cybertruck, a Semi, a Model Y, and a Model 3, according to TechCrunch. Nobody was in it, as Electrek points out. Elon Musk called it "the best of the last of the human-driven cars," and he also said "safety is not the main goal" and that Tesla would "aspire not to kill anyone in this car."

Narrator: Three trims, three ways to cheat traction
Narrator: Every fast car hits the same wall: you can only accelerate as hard as the tires can push on the road, which for street tires is somewhere around 1 to 1.5 g. Tesla's answer is three trims that each add a way around that wall.

Narrator: The thrusters: SpaceX's cold gas, at sea level
Narrator: A cold-gas thruster is the simplest rocket there is: a tank of gas at enormous pressure and a nozzle. Nothing burns. Tesla's version stores air at 10,000 psi, about 690 bar, in carbon-fiber overwrapped pressure vessels, the COPVs SpaceX flies on Falcon 9. Reports differ on the gas: Electrek says compressed air, and MotorTrend's explainer says mostly nitrogen. The Thrust trim carries 200 liters behind the seats and fires four rear ports that together make about 80 kilonewtons. The Rocket adds 400 liters, which is why it gives up the passenger seat, plus a downward thruster at each corner and two up front for yaw, ten in all. The car can top up its own tanks with an onboard compressor, but Electrek reports a full refill takes six to seven hours today, against Tesla's hope of thirty minutes. Musk first promised a SpaceX option package with small thrusters in 2018, and later described the tank taking the place of the rear seats.

Narrator: The rocket math
Narrator: Here is where it gets fun. The car weighs a claimed 1,700 kg, so its weight is about 16,700 newtons. Eighty kilonewtons of thrust is nearly five times that, a thrust-to-weight ratio fighter jets would envy. Now check it against the claims: going from 0 to 60 mph, which is 26.8 meters per second, in 0.55 seconds means averaging about 49 m/s², or 5 g. In 0.81 seconds, the live-demo time, it's 3.4 g. Tires alone can't do either, so the thrusters have to carry most of the launch.
Narrator: And they can't carry it for long. This part is my own back-of-envelope estimate, not a Tesla number. Cold air at sea level has a specific impulse of roughly 60 seconds, a measure of how much push you get per kilogram of gas. At 80 kN that burns about 136 kilograms of air every second. Air at 690 bar weighs roughly 0.4 kilograms per liter, so the Thrust's 200 liters hold about 80 kg and run dry in a little over half a second at full blast, about the length of a launch. The Rocket's 600 liters, about 250 kg, last around two seconds. Electrek reached the same conclusion from the video: a few seconds at full power at most. These are flight-and-launch systems measured in heartbeats.
Narrator: The fans: borrowing from the fan cars
Narrator: Every trim has four suction fans under the floor, about 20 kW each, roughly 80 kW or 108 horsepower in all, with skirts that seal the underbody and lift away at high speed. The fans pull air out from under the car so the atmosphere presses it into the road, at a standstill as well as at speed. Tesla's on-screen display claimed about 4,700 kg of downforce, which Electrek calls "potentially unreal." The benchmark is the McMurtry Spéirling, a 900 kg single-seat fan car that makes 2,000 kg of downforce standing still and in April 2025 set an outright Top Gear test track record of 55.9 seconds, 3.1 seconds quicker than a 2004 Formula 1 car. Downforce is also what let the Roadster climb that inverted ramp: enough suction, and the road can be above you.

Narrator: The Roadster lands in a strange moment. Tesla's own future is the Cybercab, a car with no steering wheel, and here is Musk selling the last great car you drive yourself, built with parts from his rocket company while investors speculate about a Tesla and SpaceX merger. He said himself that he's "not sure it moves the needle massively from a revenue standpoint." Electrek calls the jumps and flips "just a party trick" with no performance benefit, notes that a thruster plume throws debris tens of meters, and doubts the flying version will ever be practical. The 2017 reservation holders have been waiting almost nine years. And the first Roadster, the one Musk launched on a Falcon Heavy in February 2018 with a mannequin named Starman at the wheel, is still out there circling the Sun, which makes it the only Tesla Roadster so far to have actually flown farther than twenty feet.
