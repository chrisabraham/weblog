Narrator: AI Slop Is Good Enough. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: The book became a monitor, and the curve never moved. Doodle by Eunice.
Narrator: A friend shared a collage this week, a grid of AI-generated flyers for church socials and car shows and family markets and DJ nights, printed over with the words It All Looks The Same. Her own line on top of it: "AI is the ultimate turn off."
Narrator: The sameness is real. I grant the whole collage. What the collage leaves out is who the flyers were made for, and it was never the people with the taste to be turned off.
Narrator: Having taste is a rarity. Extreme taste—a real hunger for beauty, a unique and elegant palette, an eye that walks into a room and feels the wrongness of a chair—is maybe five percent of the human race, and that five percent sorts into two piles: people who buy very expensive bespoke things, one at a time, from people they know by name, and people who have no money at all. The first pile commissions. The second pile complains beautifully and buys nothing. Neither pile has ever been a market you could build a business on, and being turned off is itself a luxury good: you have to be able to afford the bespoke alternative, or afford to go without entirely, before a free flyer can offend you.
Narrator: The money is at the top of the bell curve, in the fat middle where most of humanity lives, and what AI did for that middle is simple: it took the friction out of illustrating a thing. A flyer used to be a decision—hire somebody, beg somebody, do without. Now it is an afternoon. The largest buying set on earth just got pictures, and AI slop is perfectly good for that set in the same way clip art was perfectly good for it, because the set was never shopping for art. It was shopping for done. The realtor with an open house on Saturday, the food truck that needs a menu board, the woman selling candles on Etsy, the Little League team that needs a sponsor banner by Friday—none of them experience the sameness in that collage as a failure, because none of them ever see the collage. Each of them sees one flyer, theirs, and theirs has a picture on it where last year there was a blank space and a deadline.
Narrator: Even the top of the pyramid is leaning in. The houses that can afford to cast and populate a Tiffany's ad or a Dior campaign are using AI-generated animation and AI-generated scenarios, because the people who run those brands live in the same world as everybody else: deadlines, budgets, a board that wants the campaign on Thursday.
Narrator: My father had the books. Bob Abraham ran a production company in Hawaii in the seventies and eighties, Abraham and Dunn, and his studio had giant books of clip art—whole bound volumes of public, open, anyone-can-use-them graphics: borders, spot illustrations, little drawings of turkeys and Christmas trees and men shaking hands. Clip art is not a metaphor. It is a description of the workflow. You took an X-Acto knife and cut the picture out of the book. Or you photographed it, or you ran it through the Xerox machine, or you laid tracing paper over it and drew it yourself. Then it went into the layout and the job went out the door. The books lived next to the layout table and the light box, and they were a working tool the way a wrench is a working tool—nobody in that studio thought of them as art and nobody thought of them as cheating. They were inventory.
Narrator: Every design studio in America had those books, as I recall, and they were the first ruin: a small job that once hired a drawing hand for an afternoon started reaching for the shelf instead. Then Microsoft Word shipped with a folder of cheap clipart inside it and the books became a directory on a hard drive, and nobody wept. That clip art illustrated church publications and newsletters and school bulletins for decades. It was good enough for church. Good enough is a real standard, and most of the printed world runs on it: most printed matter exists to announce a thing, and an announcement with a picture beats an announcement without one, and that is the whole contest. The church secretary did not have an art budget. She had a mimeograph, then a copier, then a PC with Word on it, and a bulletin due every single week of the year, and the clip art turkey at Thanksgiving and the clip art lilies at Easter were the difference between a page that looked cared for and a page that looked typed in a hurry. Multiply her by every church, school, VFW hall, and small business in America and you have the actual illustration market of the twentieth century.
Narrator: Stock photography was going to ruin photography, and it ruined the commodity end of it, the same end clip art took from illustration. My buddy is seventy years old and still gets hired to photograph malls, because he can demand a premium, because people like working with him, because his clients carry a line item with his name effectively on it and a relationship that renews itself. The ruin and the career happened at the same time, to different photographers. A stock library cannot photograph your mall. It cannot be there on Tuesday when the light comes through the atrium the way the leasing office likes, and it cannot know the manager's name. The commodity photograph—the generic skyline, the handshake, the smiling team around a conference table—went to the library, and it deserved to: it was already a commodity the day it was shot, priced like one, treated like one.
Narrator: That is the sorting AI is doing now. It destroys the crappy photographer and the crappy artist, the commodity pair of hands who could never demand a premium and never gave a client a reason beyond price. The man with the line item and the relationship stands exactly where he stood, because what he sells was never the picture alone.
Narrator: Underneath the sorting sits the choice nobody in the collage wants to name. For most people, in most weeks, the choice was never between a good illustration and a slop illustration. The choice was an illustration or no illustration at all—a newsletter with no pictures, a Christmas announcement that is just type on a page, a parish secretary laying out the bulletin at 4pm on a Friday with whatever she has. The friction of commissioning an actual work of art for a potluck announcement was never, ever going to be paid. Everybody just wants a Christmas graphic. Everybody just wants a little Thanksgiving thing to sit next to the newsletter column. I know the lineage because it is mine: I used to go to Fiverr for my holiday illustrations and pay a stranger a few dollars to draw them, and before Fiverr I went to Pixabay for free clipart, and now I have the machine make them, and the machine is the third stop on a road I have been walking for twenty years. The Fiverr years are the tell. I am a man with a lifetime in media and a photographer for a father, and for a holiday graphic I did not commission art and never considered commissioning art. I paid a stranger a few dollars on the internet, the same as everyone, because the graphic was a task with a deadline attached, and tasks get cleared.
Narrator: Slop is a property of the prompter. Prompt toward exactly what you see in your head, learn what the tools can actually do, refuse the first thing the machine hands you, and the work gets as bespoke as you have the patience to make it—the first month your prompts are wishes, and by the sixth month they are instructions. Watch two people use the same tool for an hour. One takes the first image the machine offers and is done in four minutes. The other spends the hour pushing—wrong hands, fix the hands, wrong light, fix the light, tighter crop, less noise—and walks away with something that has a point of view. Same tool. The difference was never in the software. The ceiling on AI art is the imagination, vision, and taste of the person at the keyboard. Most people have very little of any of the three, or they have them and spend them elsewhere: beauty, balance, negative space are priorities, and for most people they are low ones. What most people need is the barrier of good enough, cleared. Then they hit publish and check the item off the list.
