Narrator: OpenAI published its perplexity curve and proposed a shared speed limit
Narrator: Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist, published “Perplexity and Intelligence” on Sunday. A frontier model is a predictor trained on a very large corpus, and log-perplexity on a fixed held-out set is the cleanest single number for how good that predictor has become. OpenAI printed its own history, measured on an internal codebase snapshot. GPT-3.5 sat at about 0.99. GPT-4 reached about 0.91, where productive chain-of-thought began. Current models run below 0.8, the band where Pachocki says transformative capabilities appeared in cybersecurity and mathematics. At about 0.76 the models started producing groundbreaking results on open math problems. He expects another change around 0.68 to 0.70 and says plainly that he does not know what it will be. The proposal asks frontier labs to agree on common limits, measured by perplexity and other metrics, and to hold below them until the ability to understand and monitor model behavior reaches a concrete milestone. No first limit is named, and no other lab has signed anything.
Narrator: “Once it moved to a model scoring about 0.76, it started producing groundbreaking results.”
Narrator: The board gives this the lead because a lab published the ruler it uses on itself. Leaderboards measure what a model does on tasks somebody chose; perplexity measures the training process underneath every task, which is why publishing the curve costs OpenAI something real. Microsoft moved first among the rivals: Mustafa Suleyman endorsed the transparency push the same day, and Monday recaps report Microsoft disclosing its MAI-2 Base at 0.819 on OpenAI's own holdout, close to GPT-5.6 Sol, with OpenAI's permission. A shared ruler needs a second lab willing to be measured on it, and that happened within a day. The Lessig chair notes the architecture: a voluntary limit written in a metric is code as law for an industry with no legislature, and its strength depends on whether the numbers stay public. What arrives at 0.68 stays open in this paper. Pachocki left it open too.
Narrator: AI Mode citations per answer nearly doubled in one week of measurement
Narrator: Vilnux Research measures the same buying questions on the same engines every week, and its week-of-November-23 panel, 773 Google AI Mode answers, shows a jump larger than its usual movement on every citation measure at once. Citations per answer went from 4.77 to 9.41, up 97.3 percent. Cited domains per answer went from 4.18 to 7.81. Answers got longer, from 4,000 to 5,026 characters, and industry brands named per answer rose from 0.70 to 1.20, with the tracked brands present in 42.8 percent of answers, up from 29.8 percent. Perplexity's panel presence eased from 17.3 to 16.0 percent. Vilnux prints its own caveat, and this paper prints it with the numbers: its provider received shortened AI Mode answers from August 22 to November 17, and since November 18 has received the full page a real user sees. Some of the jump is the full answer finally being measured in full.
Narrator: The home galaxy reads even the corrected version as the week's most useful item for working businesses. AI Mode answers are getting longer, naming more brands, and citing more sources per answer, which means more slots on the screen a buyer actually reads and more weight on each one. A citation count that can double in a week is a surface that moves, and surfaces that move reward the businesses whose names, prices, and profiles are clean enough to be quoted on any given morning. Sunday's paper covered the coupon code arriving inside the answer. Monday's measurement says the answer itself is widening. The storefront is getting bigger shelves.
Narrator: The Lean community certified the formal Riemann proof
Narrator: Monday's research wires carry the line the last three days have been building toward: the Lean community has issued a verification statement on the formal proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. The claim underneath it belongs to Anthropic's Levent Alpöge, working with Claude: a four-page note and a fourteen-part Lean archive posted November 20, claiming the hypothesis and its generalization. Saturday's scan recorded the machinery mid-run, 175,187 modules processed with the Lean kernel and independent checkers passing on the three standard axioms. Formal verification means the proof as written in Lean checks, every step following by machine from the axioms. It leaves open whether the formal statement matches the hypothesis Riemann stated in 1859, the point Kevin Buzzard has been pressing, and the Clay Millennium Prize runs on a slower clock regardless, requiring publication and two years of community acceptance.
Narrator: The labels stay on. Claim: a proof exists. Exhibit: a machine-checked formalization, now with a community verification statement behind it. Still open: the statement-match question and the judgment of the mathematicians who work the problem. That is a stronger position than any math claim has stood in during this paper's short life, and it arrived the same weekend OpenAI's chief scientist explained, with numbers, why his models started producing groundbreaking results on open problems once the loss fell far enough. Two stories, one mechanism. The board prints them side by side and lets the reader feel the floor move.
Narrator: Claude designed quantum circuits up to 39 times leaner
Narrator: Harvard, Anthropic, and QuEra highlighted a preprint in which Claude, paired with an independent automated evaluator that checked every design for correctness, redesigned eight building blocks used across larger quantum algorithms. In seven of the eight, the AI designs cut both gate counts and qubit counts against the human baselines. The combined resource metric, Toffoli gates multiplied by peak logical qubits, fell by up to 39 times: a fixed-point Gaussian went from about 24,000 Toffoli gates to 1,583 and from 362 qubits to 139. The Reed-Solomon decoder at the heart of a promising optimization algorithm dropped from 5.1 million Toffoli gates to about 440,000. The work was led by Andi Gu under Anthropic's STEM Fellows Program, with Harvard's Susanne Yelin and Mikhail Lukin. These are resource estimates for building blocks, checked by an evaluator, rather than runs on quantum hardware, and the preprint says so.
Narrator: The same week brought the institutional version of the same idea: Harvard and Cornell announced a $12 million Energy Department award under the Genesis Mission, the Application-Aware Error Correction Codesign consortium, joining MIT, Los Alamos, Berkeley Lab, and Sandia with QuEra, NVIDIA, and Anthropic to design hardware, error-correcting codes, AI decoders, and algorithms as one system. Sunday's paper covered AI decoders attacking quantum errors at SC26; Monday's adds AI designers attacking quantum cost itself. Useful quantum computing has two walls, the error rate and the resource bill, and the labs are now aiming the classical AI stack at both, with the receipts published as preprints and awards. The source doctrine of this paper says to watch the labs. The labs are the story again.
Narrator: Torvalds on 7.4-rc4: “It's huge. What a surprise.”
Narrator: Linux 7.4-rc4 arrived Sunday, midway through the release candidates for the last major kernel of 2026, stable expected late December. Linus Torvalds announced it in his standing Sunday form: “It's Sunday afternoon, and -rc4 is out. It's huge. What a surprise. Lots of little fixes all over, with drivers being pretty exactly half of it.” GPU and networking lead the driver half, with USB, IIO, NVMe, and sound behind them. The week's concrete items include removing NVMe namespaces in parallel for better performance and new CPU family and model IDs for Intel's Razor Lake and Coral Rapids processors. Phoronix attributes the continued size to the stream of AI and LLM-generated bug reports and patches still flowing into 7.4.
Narrator: “Would I've loved to see a smaller rc? Sure. But those days are behind us, particularly with this very large 7.4 release.”
Narrator: The standing watch exists for mornings like this. Torvalds spent the early 7.4 cycle saying the healthy pattern is AI-triggered work landing in the merge window as normal development, and rc4 is that pattern at scale: a huge mid-cycle candidate, mostly little fixes, arriving on schedule from a project that has absorbed the AI patch flood into its ordinary week. The kernel is the largest open engineering project on earth, and its maintainer's verdict on the AI era, delivered in the driest possible register, is that the flood is now just the workflow. The tools are in the process. The process still ships on Sundays.
