Narrator: AI Search Roundup: The Week the Citation Became the Ranking. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: This roundup is new. My daily AI-search watch started this week, so week one is short: two stories I published here and one my Feed caught that the daily log missed. Short, but the three rhyme, and the rhyme is the story. Search’s contest has moved inside the answer, and this week it moved in three directions at once: where a citation sits, who writes the words on it, and what kinds of pages get cited at all. Placement, authorship, diet. A business can lose on all three without its rankings changing by a single position, which is exactly why rankings alone no longer tell you what the machine is doing with your name.
Narrator: Google: AI Mode and AI Overviews updates to explore the web
Narrator: The scale first. Search Engine Land, reporting Semrush tracking of 400 U.S. desktop commercial keywords, said Google AI Overviews appeared for 88% of those keywords in August, nearly double the 45.75% recorded in September 2025—and weighted by search volume, coverage reached 97%. Review searches went from roughly 30% to nearly 90%. The answer on top is no longer an experiment; for commercial questions it is simply the results page.
Narrator: Against that backdrop, Google’s October 15 announcement is the operational news. Google is moving inline source links higher up inside AI Mode and AI Overviews responses, and testing a dedicated section at the top of some responses for the one or two official places where a task actually gets done. In Google’s words:
Narrator: “We’ve made continued and substantial increases to the presence of inline links in our AI experiences. We’re also launching updates to show inline links higher up in responses more frequently.”
Narrator: A link buried under a long answer is a footnote. A link attached to the sentence a customer is reading arrives at the moment of decision. And the official-site test asks a harder question than “which pages discuss this?”—it asks “where does this get done?” A business that wants that slot needs a canonical page that performs the job and states the facts, not a brochure. I wrote this up here as The Citation Is the New Ranking, and I stand by the formulation: the citation is the new ranking.
Narrator: Search Engine Roundtable: Google AI Overview Citation Cards With AI Summary Descriptions
Narrator: Then the authorship moved. Search Engine Roundtable reported this morning that the citation cards beside an AI Overview—the cards carrying a site’s name and link—have begun showing AI-generated summary snippets instead of text drawn from the cited page itself. The spotter did the obvious check, comparing the card’s words against the page’s words:
Narrator: “The text doesn’t appear exactly like this on the page – it is being summarised based on the information that Google thinks is most relevant to the query.”
Narrator: The report’s author reproduced it on some test queries but not all, so call it a rollout in progress, not a finished switch. Even so, the direction is unmistakable. The snippet was the last corner of the results page you half-wrote yourself, because Google lifted your title, your description, or your sentences verbatim. Now the caption under your citation is a paraphrase, written per query, and the same page can wear different captions for different searchers on different days—none of them yours. As the Roundtable put it, “We got the AI, generating more AI, for more AI…” You cannot write the summary. What you can control is how summarizable your page is: facts stated plainly compress clean, because there is only one honest way to shorten them, while fog compresses into whatever the machine guesses. My companion piece: You Don’t Write Your Own Citation Anymore.
Narrator: SEMAI AI Search 2026 Report: one third of ChatGPT’s recorded sources are documentation pages
Narrator: Finally, the diet changed—or at least one careful count says it did. SEMAI, an answer-engine-optimization platform, analysed more than 250,000 URLs from its own visibility data across ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews. For ChatGPT’s current model, documentation and support pages made up 33.5% of recorded URLs, up from 1.4% when SEMAI ran the same queries the same way in February, while blogs and articles fell from 41.2% to 9.3%. Company websites still appeared in 79.4% of matched queries, so the corporate site is not dead; what changed is which part of it the machine eats. SEMAI’s summary is the pull quote of the week:
Narrator: “A model change reshaped what ChatGPT cites, and it did not happen on the other engines.”
Narrator: Two honest caveats. This is a vendor’s press release about its own instrument, not a census, and the other engines did not move the same way—Gemini still drew 50.6% of its recorded URLs from articles and guides, and AI Overviews 44.4%. Treat it as a strong signal, not a settled fact. The signal is still worth acting on: the machine reads the manual. Help pages, support docs, and plain factual pages—the unglamorous layer most marketing departments starve—are a citation surface now, and a model swap can reshuffle that diet overnight without any announcement, any ranking update, or any change on your site at all.
Narrator: What the week adds up to
Narrator: Three checks, then, for Monday morning. Are you cited inside the answers your customers actually see? What does your citation card say about you, in words you did not write? And is your factual layer—your help pages, your official task pages, your plain statements of who, what, where, and how much—written clearly enough to be quoted and summarized without drift? That is the audit I run: it reads the answers, the citations, and the cards, not just a list of rankings. The ten blue links had a good run. The paragraph that replaced them is the new front door, and this week Google rearranged the furniture, rewrote the nameplate, and—over at ChatGPT—changed what it eats for breakfast.
