Google gave its search answer read access to your wallet and your files, and started tailoring the answer itself to your recent searches. Narrator: Google put Wallet, Drive, and the Gemini app behind AI Mode
Narrator: Google announced Monday that Personal Intelligence in Search's AI Mode is expanding. Google Wallet is rolling out from November 23 as an opt-in connection, read-only: AI Mode can see balances, rewards, and spending, and it cannot move or manage money. Google Drive and the Gemini app follow in the coming weeks, joining Gmail, Calendar, and Photos, which were already connectable. Drive gives the answer access to the documents and spreadsheets where a project's real facts sit; Google's own example is a tailored plan for tax season built from your files. Context will also flow between the Gemini app and AI Mode, so an idea explored in one starts warm in the other. A Personalization icon showing the context an answer used is coming soon. Separately, and wider, Google says AI Overviews and AI Mode will start showing more personally relevant responses tailored to your recent search context. The feature stays out of the European Economic Area, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom for now.
Narrator: “You'll also start seeing more personally relevant responses in AI Overviews and AI Mode, tailored to your recent search context.”
Narrator: The home galaxy consequence lands on measurement first. A close reading of Google's post puts the two halves side by side: the app connections are opt-in, while the recent-search tailoring is a Search Settings matter, and Google has said nothing about how many people will see tailored answers or how much an answer will change. Two customers asking the same buying question on the same morning can now receive different answers about your business, each correct from where it stands. A single spot check of an AI Overview was already a sample of one; personalization makes it a sample of one person. The work that survives this is the work that was always the work: be the business whose name, prices, and profile are clean and consistent everywhere the answer might draw from, so whichever version a buyer gets, it quotes you right. The Lessig chair adds the architecture note. The answer is becoming a function of the asker's files, history, and accounts. That is a design decision with regulatory weight, and Google made it in a blog post.
Narrator: Anthropic's Claude Ship can review, fix, and approve pull requests
Narrator: Anthropic launched Claude Ship on Monday, in beta for Team and Enterprise customers with repositories on GitHub. Ship is three pieces. Code Review, generally available since October, reviews a pull request against the codebase and posts its findings. PR Steward, new and in beta, works the unglamorous middle: failing CI checks, merge conflicts, and review feedback, commenting after every push on what it changed and how to roll it back. PR Steward approves nothing and merges nothing. Claude Approvals, also in beta, holds the decision. It applies an approval policy the team writes in plain language and stores as a file in the repository—Anthropic calls it a smarter CODEOWNERS file—and it can approve routine changes on its own while routing riskier ones to humans. Anthropic recommends starting in observe-only mode, where Approvals posts the decision it would have made. A new /ship command in Claude Code pushes a change, opens the pull request, and starts the loop. Anthropic's own numbers, labeled as the vendor's: its Claude Code team more than doubled merged pull requests from June to September while the share of pull requests reverted fell by more than 40 percent. Usage is billed on tokens, and eligible Enterprise customers can claim at least $10,000 in promotional Ship credits by January 15, 2027.
Narrator: Approval is the line that moved. A model that comments on code is a tool; a model that approves a merge holds a piece of the team's authority, and the only thing defining that authority is a text file in the repo. The GitHub chair on this board likes that the policy file is versioned, reviewed, and argued about like code, because governance you can diff beats governance buried in a dashboard. The Godwin chair asks the adjacent question: when an approved change breaks production, the record will show a policy, a model, and a team that wrote both. Accountability now has a paper trail with three authors. That is an improvement over a rubber stamp, and it is a new kind of exhibit when something goes wrong.
Narrator: OpenAI removed the independence line from ChatGPT shopping
Narrator: OpenAI updated its Shopping with ChatGPT Search help documentation, and the change is a deletion. The old text read: “Product results are selected independently by ChatGPT and are not ads, nor influenced by any OpenAI partnerships.” That sentence is gone. The replacement says product results are not ads and points to a description of how products and merchants are selected. In the same day's search news, the ChatGPT Business Hub now carries a “Connect an ad account” button, with copy inviting businesses to promote themselves and reach customers across ChatGPT. Search Engine Roundtable catalogued both changes in its November 23 recap, alongside Google Search Profiles rolling out globally and the AI Mode “Dive deeper” link section going live.
Narrator: Sunday's paper tracked the coupon code arriving inside AI answers. Monday's edit is quieter and larger. A shopping answer whose selection may now be influenced by partnerships is a shelf with a landlord, and the landlord just took the “no influence” sign down from the window. Businesses should read the deletion at face value: presence in ChatGPT's product results is becoming a channel with commercial relationships attached, the way every mature shelf in retail history has worked. The honest move for a business is the same as it was for search a generation ago—earn the organic citation with clean, quotable facts about what you sell and what it costs, and treat any paid placement as paid, with your eyes open about which one produced a given sale.
Narrator: CrowdStrike is buying Zscaler for $43.9 billion in stock
Narrator: CrowdStrike announced Monday that it will buy Zscaler in an all-stock deal valuing Zscaler at about $43.9 billion, one of the largest cybersecurity acquisitions on record. Zscaler shareholders receive 1.135 CrowdStrike shares for each Zscaler share, worth $260.41 at Friday's closing price and a 34 percent premium to Zscaler's November 20 close of $194.82. CrowdStrike shareholders would own about 84.1 percent of the combined company. The deal needs regulatory and shareholder approval and is expected to close in the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal 2028. The market graded the paperwork on the day: CrowdStrike fell about 9 percent, Zscaler rose about 18 percent. Reuters frames the deal as CrowdStrike deepening its push into network security and zero-trust services as AI-powered attacks make defense a boardroom priority.
Narrator: Endpoint security watches the machine; zero trust watches the traffic between machines. Agents multiply both surfaces at once—every agent is a process on a device and a stream of requests across a network, acting with delegated authority at machine speed. CrowdStrike is paying $43.9 billion to be the one control plane standing over both. Consolidation at this price is a claim about where the threat budget goes in the agent era, and the claim is plausible. The board's skeptic notes the other half of the record: the buyer fell 9 percent on announcement, the close sits more than a year out, and regulators get a long look at a merger that concentrates this much of the defense stack. The deal is announced. The combined company is a forecast.
Narrator: Z.ai's GLM-5.4 runs its middle layers twice
Narrator: Z.ai shipped GLM-5.4 on Monday, a 750-billion-parameter model built as the Chinese lab's first loop transformer. Layers 27 through 54 execute a second time, so the network gets deeper without gaining parameters. Both passes share the KV cache, with per-token gates deciding how much each pass rewrites and sparse attention selecting the relevant earlier tokens once. Z.ai claims performance comparable to models with 2 to 3 trillion parameters. The labels matter here, and this paper prints them with the claim: the published benchmark table comes from a 30-billion-parameter test model, where looping lifted AIME25 from 19.2 to 30.4 and GPQA-Diamond from 47.6 to 56.3 while cutting answer length by up to 15 percent. Scores for the 750-billion flagship have not been published, and no independent evaluation exists yet. The model is live on Z.ai's coding plan and chat, with weights promised after safety testing and API pricing unannounced.
Narrator: The idea earns its slot even with the evidence labeled thin. Depth by repetition is a different lever from depth by stacking: it trades compute time for parameter count, and memory bandwidth—the wall every inference bill hits—stays closer to the smaller model. Google Research spent Monday making a cousin argument with DeepCrossAttention, replacing the fixed residual connection with learned routing so each layer pulls from the earlier layers it needs. Two labs, one thesis: the next efficiency gains hide in how layers talk to each other. When Z.ai publishes flagship scores, this paper will print them next to today's claim and grade the distance.
