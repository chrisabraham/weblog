Narrator: What the data says about how co-founders meet
Narrator: Start with the evidence, because it cuts against the romance. Cherry Ventures' Heartbeat report on 10,000 young European companies found that three quarters were started by one person alone, and that pairs who founded together had met at a former employer about 3.2 times as often as at a university, 257 pairs to 80, with McKinsey producing the most pairs of any single employer, Trending Topics reported in October. Co-founders mostly meet at work.
Narrator: Whether they should is another matter. A study of nearly 350 American tech IPOs and billion-dollar exits by Outcast found that founders who had worked together before had exit valuations 21 percent lower than those who hadn't, and schoolmates 7 percent lower, while founders who had already worked at a startup, even a failed one, had valuations 41 percent higher, Fast Company reported in March. Researchers at Maryland, the Technion, and INSEAD found that only about one team in ten deliberately mixes people who like each other with people whose skills fill the gaps, and that those hybrid teams do better, the University of Maryland announced. The lesson is to meet people on purpose, in rooms built for it, instead of grabbing whoever sits nearest.
Narrator: The first rooms: a garage and a diner booth
Narrator: On March 5, 1975, Gordon French and Fred Moore held the first meeting of the Homebrew Computer Club in French's garage in Menlo Park, because the first Altair 8800 had arrived in the area. Steve Wozniak credits that meeting with inspiring the Apple I, and he and Steve Jobs later showed it at a club meeting in the auditorium at SLAC, where a store owner named Paul Terrell bought the first 50. Club members went on to found Cromemco, Osborne Computer, Processor Technology, and Morrow Designs, the club's history records. One garage, one free meeting, a personal computer industry.
Narrator: In 1993 three friends, Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky, and Curtis Priem, met at a Denny's in Silicon Valley to talk about building a graphics chip. That conversation became NVIDIA. Huang had gotten his first job at a Denny's in Portland at 15, as a dishwasher, busboy, and waiter, and in September 2023 the chain put a plaque on the booth, NVIDIA's own blog recounts. The chip company the AI era runs on started over coffee refills, at a table anyone could have sat at.
Narrator: Up the road in Woodside, Buck's has been the breakfast spot for Sand Hill Road since Jamis MacNiven bought it in 1991. "Hotmail was founded here," he told NPR in 2010. "Netscape had their early meetings in the back room; Tesla was founded here; PayPal got funded here." Those are the owner's claims, and he tells them well. The point stands either way: the money eats breakfast somewhere, and it's usually the same place.
Narrator: Accelerators: rooms with a clock
Narrator: An accelerator takes a team that already exists, puts it in a room with other teams for a few months, and ends with a demo day. Y Combinator, founded in March 2005 by Paul Graham, Jessica Livingston, Robert Morris, and Trevor Blackwell, has funded more than 5,000 companies, including Airbnb, Stripe, Dropbox, DoorDash, Coinbase, and Scale AI, the record shows. Its standard deal is $500,000: $125,000 for 7 percent on a post-money SAFE and $375,000 on an uncapped SAFE, YC's deal page says.
Narrator: YC also runs a co-founder matching service. Its co-founder matching platform launched in July 2021, and within three months it had 16,000 profiles and 33,000 matches. Two thirds of matches paired a technical founder with a non-technical one, and 62 percent of founders said they wanted a co-founder who writes code, YC reported. Engineers are the scarce side of that market. If you can build, you're the one being courted.
Narrator: Talent investors: rooms for people without a team
Narrator: The more interesting rooms take you before you have a co-founder at all. Entrepreneur First, which Matt Clifford and Alice Bentinck started in London in 2011, invests in individuals "before they have established a cofounder or a specific business concept," spends the first stage helping them find each other, and backs the pairs that form. It accepts an estimated 1 to 3 percent of applicants and counts more than 600 companies, including Tractable, Cleo, and Magic Pony, which Twitter bought for a reported $150 million in 2016, its history records.
Narrator: South Park Commons in San Francisco describes its job as helping people go "from -1 to 0," the stage before you know what to build. Its residency runs six months at no cost and takes no equity. Its Founder Fellowship invests $400,000 for 7 percent up front and guarantees $600,000 more in the next outside round, the fall 2026 call says, and solo founders can apply. If you don't have a co-founder yet, these two are built for you.
Narrator: Studios: rooms that hire the founder
Narrator: A venture studio flips the order. The studio has the idea and the money and recruits the founder. Bill Gross started Idealab in Pasadena in March 1996 on that model. In Cambridge, Noubar Afeyan and Ed Kania founded what became Flagship Pioneering in 1999, and Flagship formed Moderna inside its own walls in late 2010, its history shows. If you're a scientist with no business partner, a studio is a co-founder that comes with a lab budget.
Narrator: Makerspaces and fab labs: rooms with tools
Narrator: Hardware co-founders meet over hardware. The fab lab began in 2001 with a National Science Foundation grant to MIT's Center for Bits and Atoms, went abroad to Vigyan Ashram in India in 2002, and counted 1,830 labs worldwide by 2019, the record shows. San Francisco's Noisebridge, an anarchist hackerspace in the Mission since 2007, is one long-running version. Chris asked about co-ops. These are the closest thing tech has: shared tools, shared rent, and a room full of people building something at 11 at night.
Narrator: Hacker houses: rooms you live in
Narrator: AI brought the co-op back as a house. In January 2023 a Bloomberg Beta investor, Amber Yang, is credited with coining "Cerebral Valley" for San Francisco's Hayes Valley, where AI founders had filled old Victorians with what the San Francisco Standard called all-inclusive "hacker houses." The biggest, Genesis House, opened in March 2021 with 21 bedrooms, Fortune reported. A hacker house is a dormitory where every roommate is a potential co-founder and every dinner is a pitch.
Narrator: Where to sit down
Narrator: If you can build, get on YC's co-founder matching. It's free, the demand for engineers is real, and two teams that met there made YC's Summer 2021 batch.
Narrator: If you're alone with no idea, apply to Entrepreneur First or South Park Commons. They're the only rooms designed for the stage before the team.
Narrator: Hire before you marry. The data says co-founders mostly meet at work and do worse when they're old colleagues. Work with someone on a real project for a few weeks before you split equity.
Narrator: Join the startup before you start one. Prior startup experience, even at a failure, was worth 41 percent in exit value in the Outcast study.
Narrator: Go to the public meetings. Homebrew was free and open. So are hackerspaces like Noisebridge. The cheapest room in tech is still the one with the most builders in it.
Narrator: Find the breakfast place. Every district has its Buck's. Learn where the investors eat and show up.
