Narrator: Chris remembers something from Hawaii. A big organization, maybe a grant-maker, would bring people together and put them in one place, and it was called an innovation lab or something like it. Google gave him the vocabulary: innovation district for a whole neighborhood built around the idea, innovation hub or innovation campus for a building or a cluster of them, and technology business incubator for the program inside. This part of Source Waters is about those places, the deliberate attempts to make a spring instead of waiting for one.
Narrator: Part 1 found the springs in universities and Part 2 found them in labs, weapons shops, and military units. Silicon Valley itself wasn't planned. It grew out of one lab and one walkout. Every innovation district since is a government, a university, or a billionaire trying to make that happen again on purpose. A few did.

Narrator: First, the Hawaii one
Narrator: Hawaii has had two kinds of engine, and either could be the one Chris remembers.
Narrator: The state's is the Hawaii Technology Development Corporation, a state agency created in 1983 to grow the islands' technology industry. For a quarter century it ran the Manoa Innovation Center, a tech incubator on land leased from the University of Hawaii, until the university took the building back in July 2018, Hawaii Public Radio reported. That April, HTDC broke ground in Kakaʻako on the Entrepreneurs Sandbox, a $7.3 million, 13,500-square-foot incubator built with developer Stanford Carr on state land leased for a dollar a year and a $3 million federal grant. HTDC called it the first phase of a "Kakaʻako Innovation Block."
Narrator: The grant-maker is Elemental Excelerator, which now goes by Elemental Impact, and it fits Chris' description better. It started in 2009 with Department of Energy money passed through a Hawaii nonprofit, won $30 million from the Office of Naval Research in 2013 and another $30 million in 2018, and renamed itself in 2017 when it partnered with Laurene Powell Jobs' Emerson Collective, its own history says. From downtown Honolulu it picked 15 to 20 climate and energy companies a year, gave each up to $1 million, and ran demonstration projects with them on the islands, VentureBeat reported in 2018. By 2021 it had put nearly $50 million into 136 companies that went on to raise more than $4 billion, ImpactAlpha reported. A Navy-funded accelerator in Honolulu that tested its founders' technology on the islands themselves is about as Hawaiian an idea as tech has produced.
Narrator: What an innovation district is
Narrator: The term comes mostly from Bruce Katz and Julie Wagner of the Brookings Institution, whose 2014 essay "The Rise of Innovation Districts" described compact, transit-connected, mixed-use neighborhoods where big anchor institutions and companies cluster beside startups, incubators, and accelerators. They sorted them into three kinds: the "anchor plus" district built around a university or hospital, like Kendall Square; the "re-imagined urban area" on old industrial waterfronts, like Boston's Seaport or Seattle's South Lake Union; and the "urbanized science park," the old suburban research campus trying to become a neighborhood, with Research Triangle Park as the type specimen.
Narrator: Kendall Square: the anchor plus
Narrator: By 2009 the Boston Consulting Group was calling the area within a ten-minute walk of the Kendall T stop "the most innovative square mile on earth," MIT Technology Review recounted. MIT is the anchor. Biogen was founded in 1978 and moved to Binney Street within five years. Novartis moved into the old NECCO building in 2003. Pfizer, AstraZeneca, Amgen, and Baxter opened research centers, and Google, Microsoft, and Amazon set up nearby.
Narrator: The building that turned the neighborhood into a district is the Cambridge Innovation Center, which MIT graduates Tim Rowe and Andrew Olmsted started in 1999 in 2,700 square feet at 238 Main Street. It housed more than 500 companies by 2013, HubSpot among them from 2006 to 2010, its history records. In 2015 Rowe told Technology Review that Kendall had about $14 billion in venture capital under investment, and half of it was in a CIC building. Then Rowe helped start LabCentral at 700 Main Street, a shared lab for biotech companies too young to build their own. The lesson from Kendall: the anchor makes the ideas, but somebody has to rent the founders a desk by the month.
Narrator: Research Triangle Park and Hsinchu: the parks that worked
Narrator: North Carolina's state government, its businesses, and three universities, Duke, the University of North Carolina, and NC State, created Research Triangle Park in 1959. It filled slowly until 1965, when IBM and the federal health department announced facilities there. Today it's the largest research park in the country, about 7,000 acres with more than 300 companies and roughly 65,000 workers, the record shows.
Narrator: Taiwan copied the idea and beat the original. The government opened Hsinchu Science Park on December 15, 1980, beside National Tsing Hua and National Chiao Tung universities, after a minister proposed a park "like that of Silicon Valley." TSMC and UMC were set up at the Industrial Technology Research Institute next door, which Morris Chang ran before founding TSMC, the park's history shows. By 2022 the integrated circuit companies in the park reported about $363 billion in revenue, more than three quarters of the park's total, Fortune reported. If you want one planned district that changed the hardware side of AI, it's this one, because TSMC started here.
Narrator: Zhongguancun: the street that became a district
Narrator: In the early 1980s a stretch of Beijing's Haidian District beside Peking and Tsinghua universities was known as "Electronics Avenue." The government made it an experimental high-technology zone in 1988, and Lenovo, Founder, and Stone Group all started there in 1984 and 1985, the record shows. It is still where China's AI lives. Of the 19 Beijing companies on Hurun's 2025 list of China's 50 most valuable AI companies, including Zhipu, Moonshot AI, and the chip designer Cambricon, 16 are in Haidian, Beijing's government reports. Moonshot's founder is the Carnegie Mellon PhD from Part 1. The springs connect.
Narrator: Station F, Cornell Tech, and King's Cross: billionaires, mayors, and a train station
Narrator: The newer districts were built by people with money and a deadline. Station F opened in June 2017 in a 1929 rail freight depot in Paris' 13th arrondissement, backed by the billionaire Xavier Niel. It has 34,000 square meters, room for up to 1,000 startups and 3,000 desks, and in its first five years supported more than 5,000 French startups, with Hugging Face as its first unicorn, its history records.
Narrator: In New York, Mayor Michael Bloomberg's government offered free land and $100 million to the university that would build an applied sciences campus. Cornell and Israel's Technion won in December 2011 after Stanford pulled out, and Cornell Tech opened on Roosevelt Island on September 13, 2017. Its students and graduates had started more than 50 companies by the end of 2018, the record shows. The Technion connection is the same Israeli spring from Part 2, piped into Manhattan.
Narrator: London's version grew around a railway station. The Knowledge Quarter counts the British Library, the Francis Crick Institute, the Alan Turing Institute, UCL, and the Wellcome Trust inside a mile of King's Cross. Google DeepMind and Google teams were due to start moving this past summer into Platform 37, Google's new building beside the station, named for AlphaGo's famous Move 37, with a public AI Exchange planned for the ground floor, Demis Hassabis announced in March.
Narrator: Toronto and Pittsburgh: where the AI springs got a building
Narrator: Toronto put its deep learning spring in a downtown district. The Vector Institute, founded in March 2017 by Brendan Frey, Geoffrey Hinton, and Raquel Urtasun with about 200 million Canadian dollars from governments and companies, was housed in the MaRS Discovery District before moving in 2024 to the Schwartz Reisman Innovation Campus, its history shows.
Narrator: Pittsburgh's district named itself. Robotics Row, a three-mile stretch of the Allegheny riverfront through the Strip District and Lower Lawrenceville, got its name from a local consultant, Jackie Erickson, after Uber and Bossa Nova moved in. "It was a very good marketing effort that cost zero dollars," she told The Incline. Carnegie Mellon's National Robotics Engineering Center had already been in a converted steel mill there since 1996. By 2024 Aurora, which made Pittsburgh its official headquarters in 2021, had about 900 people in the city, but RE2 and Bossa Nova had shut down, and the head of the region's nonprofit developer told the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette that innovation districts are "a little more of a brand than they are a reality."
Narrator: Why some districts work and most don't
Narrator: He's right, and the list above shows why. Every district that worked had a spring before it had a name. Kendall had MIT. Hsinchu had ITRI and two universities. Zhongguancun had Peking and Tsinghua. Robotics Row had Carnegie Mellon's robotics institute. Station F and Cornell Tech had billionaires and mayors who could keep paying while the companies grew. The building is the easy part. The districts that last pair an anchor that makes ideas with cheap space by the month, a customer nearby, and a reason for the founders to stay when the first company fails.
Narrator: How to use a district
Narrator: Rent the desk, not the office. CIC, Station F, and the Entrepreneurs Sandbox all sell space by the month. The point is the hallway, and the hallway is cheap.
Narrator: Find the anchor first. Before you move to a district, name the lab or university feeding it. If you can't, it's real estate.
Narrator: Use the public programs. HTDC, Station F, and Cornell Tech run open events. Google's AI Exchange at King's Cross is meant as a public space.
Narrator: Watch the government lists. Beijing published where its top AI companies sit. Hsinchu publishes its tenants. A district that publishes its numbers is a district that has some.
Narrator: Go where the customer is. Elemental tested its climate founders' technology on the islands themselves. The best districts put the founder next to the problem.
