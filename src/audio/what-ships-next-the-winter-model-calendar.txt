Narrator: Every “release date” for the next generation of AI models belongs to one of three species, and most of the trouble in planning comes from mistaking one for another. Some dates the labs themselves published. Some are reported—sourced by journalists and trackers, plausible, unowned. And some are merely what the release cadence suggests, arithmetic wearing a calendar’s clothes. This companion to today’s survey of the current field keeps the three in separate pens, labeled, because a rumor in a confirmed date’s clothing is how budgets die.

Narrator: Notice the shape before the dates: almost everything a lab will commit to in public is a price change or a funeral. Launches are the one thing nobody dates.

Narrator: If you build on any of these, your winter calendar is already written, and it is written by accountants and undertakers, not by launch teams.
Narrator: Reported: sourced, and not promised
Narrator: The release trackers who watch the labs’ pipelines for a living currently carry: a GPT-6.1 Terra estimated for October/November, filling the one gap in OpenAI’s weather-named family; a Claude Fable 5.5 estimated for November/December; a Grok 4.8 that was expected around October 1 and is now weeks late—the lateness is itself the data point, since xAI’s rhythm had been metronomic; a Qwen 4 and a GLM-6.0, both rumored and both late; and a DeepSeek V4.1 Pro tracked with no date at all. Separately, WhatsNew’s reporting has Google’s Gems becoming Skills for personal accounts in November. Treat every item here the same way: a reported date is a reason to keep your architecture flexible. It is never a reason to wait.
Narrator: “no, not yet”—the release tracker’s standing answer to every “is it out?”, and the correct prior for this entire tier
Narrator: Expected by cadence: my arithmetic, labeled as such
Narrator: Now the inference, openly flagged. xAI shipped flagships on August 12 and September 21—a six-week rhythm that points at early November for the next Grok, late rumor or not. Anthropic refreshed an entire four-model family between September 1 and October 7; a shop that can turn a family in five weeks plausibly turns the next one around the new year. OpenAI answered GPT-6 with a point release twenty-six days later, so expect the 6.1 family to fill in—Terra is the known gap—before anyone should hold their breath for a 6.5. Google ships a Flash roughly monthly and a flagship step roughly quarterly, which suggests more Gemini 4.x fills before year-end. This is weather, not timetable: cadence tells you the season, never the day.
Narrator: What a buyer does with a calendar like this
Narrator: Four rules, bought with experience. Build on what is shipping: the $2 tier documented in the companion survey is more model than most products will ever need. Budget the dated steps, because the only dates certain to arrive are the price rises and the funerals—December 1 and January 1 will keep their appointments even if every launch slips. Pin your model versions in production, because deprecations now arrive quarterly and a retired endpoint is a breaking change with a date on it. And never architect around a rumored model: waiting is a pricing strategy—inference fell by as much as 80 percent last quarter, as this morning’s roundup traced—but it is a terrible launch strategy. The next generation will arrive the way the last one did: unannounced on a weekday, cheaper than feared, and immediately compared against five rivals by people who put the same question to all of them.
