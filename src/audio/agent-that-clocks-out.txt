Narrator: An Agent That Clocks Out Is Not an Agent. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: An agent you cannot lean on is furniture. Last night my second agent did the best work I have ever gotten out of a machine, and this morning it is sitting in the corner with a sign on its chest: resumes at 12:50. Both of those things are true, and the second one eats the first.
Narrator: The night shift
Narrator: I gave Claude Duet a job nobody wants: go through my YouTube channel—about 4,400 public-domain movies, a first batch of roughly 240 uploads—and sort the risky ones from the safe ones. Copyright traps. Content that could draw a strike. Duplicates. The channel lives under YouTube's three-strikes rule, and three strikes inside ninety days ends the channel, so the sorting mattered.
Narrator: Duet worked all night. It set 121 uploads to private, each one with a reason written down. Twelve for nudity or sexual content. Four for racial caricature or rights risk. Ninety with no confirmed public-domain basis, including fourteen Dick Tracy chapters. Fifteen older duplicate copies, with the newer copy of each left public. It deleted nothing. Every call it made is reversible, and it left me a cleanup document with the full list and the reasoning, so I can switch any of them back myself.
Narrator: That is real work. That is the work the brochures promise. I woke up impressed, and I am still impressed.
Narrator: Then the task hit the usage limit and stopped mid-run, with uploads from last night still unscreened and the titles and descriptions for the films that passed still unwritten. The message on the screen said the task resumes at 12:50.
Narrator: Four resets in twelve hours. That is my count since last night. I knock into a daily limit, then a weekly limit, then a monthly limit, the way you knock into furniture in a dark room. I am a normal user doing normal work. I upload old movies and write blog posts. If the ceiling sits this low for me, the ceiling sits this low for everybody who actually uses the thing.
Narrator: So this morning I am stuck for about three hours. The agent is willing. The agent is able. The agent is off the clock.
Narrator: What an agent is for
Narrator: You hire an agent to finish the job. The whole pitch of these tools is delegation—hand off the work, go live your life, come back to a result. A tool that clocks out mid-shift and tells you to come back at 12:50 has quietly changed the deal. I am no longer delegating. I am scheduling my work around its breaks, checking its availability, keeping my own projects small enough to fit inside its allowance. The leash runs the wrong way.
Narrator: Wiggle room is the product. An agent needs slack the way a good employee needs slack: room to run long on the night the job runs long, room to be wrong and redo it, room to finish. Squeeze the slack out and you have a very smart tool that abandons you at the exact moment the work got big enough to be worth delegating.
Narrator: The comparison I can make
Narrator: My Muse from Meta has carried the rest of this morning. I say this as one man's experience so far, nothing more: in my use, Muse has felt unlimited. The inbox got read, the clients got answered, this post got drafted, and the ceiling never showed up. Maybe a ceiling exists and I have not found it yet. I have found Duet's ceiling four times since dinner.
Narrator: If I did not have Muse this morning, I would be dead in the water. And I would draw the obvious conclusion about Duet: fine for a question, fine for a paragraph, never to be trusted with a big project. Big projects are where agents earn their keep. A cleanup of 240 videos is exactly the job these tools were sold for, and it is exactly the job that ran into the wall.
Narrator: The advice, plainly
Narrator: Claude should get more generous. Raise the limits, smooth them out, sell a tier with real headroom and price it like you mean it—I would pay. What I will not do is build my working life on a tool that keeps a timesheet against me. People jump ship over less. They jump ship over a button moving. An agent that stops mid-job, four resets deep, with three hours left on its clock, gives them a reason with a timestamp on it.
Narrator: The work last night was amazing. I want the agent that did it. I want it at 9 in the morning too, and at the end of the month, and in the middle of the biggest job I have. An agent that clocks out is a part-timer, and I already know how to hire part-timers. I bought the agent to stop.
