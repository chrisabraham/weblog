Narrator: AI Roundup: A Million Satellites, a Warning From Linus. By Chris Abraham.
Narrator: Thursday's exhibits share one argument, conducted in five different rooms. Finding things has become cheap. An agent finds the bug, the proof, the protein, the cheaper circuit. Checking things has not become cheap at all, and every institution in today's paper is now priced by how honestly it handles that gap. The FCC cleared a million computers for orbit before anyone has shown one orbital data center that beats a building in Virginia. Linus Torvalds merged the fixes and warned the fixer. Anthropic spent longer verifying a rewrite than writing it. Google posted proofs nobody has reviewed and said so on the cover. The pattern is not fear and it is not hype. It is the new division of labor, arriving everywhere at once: machines propose, at scale, and the scarce human work is judgment about what the proposals are worth.
Narrator: The FCC authorized SpaceX to deploy up to one million AI satellites
Narrator: The Federal Communications Commission has authorized SpaceX to deploy up to one million satellites for Starmind, a proposed network of computing stations in low Earth orbit. The plan is to process AI workloads on solar power in space and transmit the results back to Earth. The authorization covers a potential network. Nothing like that scale has been deployed, and scientists quoted in the coverage question the economics and the engineering, starting with the oldest problem in computing: heat. Chips in orbit still get hot, and space offers no air to carry the heat away. Space debris and interference with astronomical observation are the other standing objections.
Narrator: An authorization is not a data center, and this paper prints it as a permission, not a fleet. The interesting part is what the filing says about the terrestrial buildout. Data centers on the ground now fight for power, water, land, and permits in every county they touch. Moving the argument to orbit does not solve the physics—it trades a zoning fight for a thermodynamics fight—but it tells you how hard the power constraint is biting when a company would rather solve cooling in a vacuum than find another gigawatt in Virginia. Watch for the first working orbital node and its honest cost per computation against an Earth-based alternative. Until that exhibit exists, Starmind is a very large option on a future, priced by regulators at one million satellites.
Narrator: Torvalds merged 41,197 lines of AI fixes, then told the AI to use a separate branch
Narrator: On November 24, the EXT4 maintainer sent the filesystem updates for Linux 7.4-rc5: 68 files changed, 41,197 insertions, most of them AI-generated bug fixes, with the bulk of the new lines in new KUnit tests meant to make AI-generated patches safer to validate. The fixes themselves are not trivial—fast-commit replay losing data that was supposed to be durable, use-after-free errors on a failed mount, a lock-inversion deadlock. Linus Torvalds merged the pull the same night. He also replied, in full, that he had considered unpulling it, and settled for a warning instead.
Narrator: “STOP THINKING THAT YOU—OR AI—NEVER ADD BUGS WHEN YOU FIX SOMETHING.”
Narrator: Read the whole thread, as the close reading above did, and the viral version inverts. Torvalds is not banning AI from the kernel; he used an AI coding tool on the page cache himself last month, by his own account. His argument is about timing and humility. A bug subtle enough to sit unnoticed until an AI dug it up is, by definition, in subtle and rare code, so its fix is exactly the kind of change that belongs in a merge window, with weeks to bake, not in a late release candidate. New development and test cases go on a separate branch. The next late, oversized pull, he said, he will simply ignore. That is the healthiest model for AI contributions any large project has yet stated, in the maintainer's own words: the work is welcome, the process is not negotiable, and finding is not the same job as landing.
Narrator: Claude rebuilt Anthropic's Messages API in Rust, and verification took longer than the writing
Narrator: Anthropic published its account on November 25 of migrating the Messages API—the interface through which applications reach Claude—from Python to Rust, using Claude agents to do the work. The agents produced the first core request-path implementation in roughly a week. Verification took longer. At one early stage about 98 percent of tests passed while only about a quarter of production inputs actually matched the old system's behavior. The team replayed real requests through both implementations, logged where and how large the differences were without logging customer content, and let agents investigate and repair the discrepancies under human review. The reported result: about 70 percent lower processor use per request and about 20 percent lower median time to the first response token, with no downtime and no feature freeze.
Narrator: This is the enterprise AI story in its honest form, and it is better than the marketing version. The rewrite was the cheap part. The expensive part was proving, request by request, that the new system did what the old system did—including the old system's accidents. Any business owner evaluating an AI migration should steal the method whole: run both systems against the same live traffic, count matches, and treat a high test pass rate as a claim, not a result. Anthropic's own numbers make the point for them. Ninety-eight percent of tests and twenty-five percent of reality is the gap where migrations die, and they published it.
Narrator: Google's Cogentic agent posted AI-written proofs on factoring and discrete logarithms
Narrator: Google Research posted six papers dated November 25, produced by Cogentic, an agent framework built on Gemini 4. The project README says no human was involved beyond the first prompt. One paper claims a proven factoring algorithm running in the same complexity class that the number field sieve was only believed to reach. Another claims faster discrete logarithms in small-characteristic fields. Both problems sit at the core of modern cryptography, so the labels matter, and the source prints them: the papers have not been peer reviewed, the authors say their Lean formalizations are complete but have not released them yet, and no paper claims polynomial time. This does not make RSA easier to break in practice.
Narrator: Two days in a row this paper has carried a machine proof with no human reader, and the contrast is instructive. Yesterday's Claude proof arrived with its Lean build open for anyone to verify. Cogentic's arrives with the formalization promised but not yet posted, which puts it one rung lower on the only ladder that matters here: exhibits available now beat exhibits described. The milestone is still real. An agent system, prompted once, produced a body of work in the hardest corner of algorithmic number theory that specialists now have to take seriously enough to check. Whether the claims stand is a question for the referees, and this paper will print the verdict when they render one. Until then it travels as what it is—an extraordinary claim, honestly labeled, awaiting its exhibits.
Narrator: 1Password was breached through an employee's GitHub account, and a 12-year-old NFS flaw gives remote root
Narrator: Security supplied two exhibits with hard edges. 1Password began investigating on November 19 after an attacker got into an employee's corporate GitHub account, pushed malicious code to backend services, and took names, email addresses, account IDs, and some invitation and recovery tokens, reaching source code and build systems along the way. The company is explicit about what was not taken: vault contents, encryption keys, and Secret Keys were not exposed. Separately, a heap overflow in the Linux kernel's NFS server, present since Linux 3.16 in 2014 and tracked as CVE-2026-99362, lets one unauthenticated connection to port 2049 trigger remote root on hosts serving NFSv4.1 or 4.2. It is rated 9.8. Fixes are out in kernels 6.12.117, 6.18.61, and 7.3.2.
Narrator: The two stories teach the same lesson from opposite ends. 1Password's architecture did its job—the vault design held even when an employee account and the build pipeline fell—which is exactly what a password manager is for. The exposure that remains is human: names, emails, and recovery tokens are phishing material, so an invitation or recovery message mentioning 1Password deserves suspicion this month. The NFS flaw is the simpler discipline. A service that has been quietly listening since 2014 is now a one-connection root. Patch and reboot the NFS servers, and where the update has not landed, firewall port 2049 until it does. Neither item needs a framework. One needs skepticism, the other needs a reboot.
Narrator: An AI-designed protein cut blood sugar about 60 percent in mice with a broken insulin receptor
Narrator: Columbia researchers used AI design tools, including AlphaFold2, to build a protein, RF-409, for one of medicine's cruelest small problems. In rare genetic disorders including Donohue syndrome, the insulin receptor itself is mutated, so insulin cannot switch it on; babies with the most severe form rarely survive their first year. RF-409 grips the receptor in a different place from insulin. In mice carrying a Donohue mutation, insulin did almost nothing, while RF-409 cut blood sugar by about 60 percent within two hours. The unknowns are printed with the result: long-term safety, the immune system's response, and which mutations the protein can work around are all still open. The work appeared in Science Translational Medicine on November 25.
Narrator: The frontier beat runs on exhibits like this because the logic is so clean. When the lock is broken, do not make more keys—design a hand that turns the lock from a different side. A general design tool, pointed at a receptor almost no drug company would fund on its own, produced a candidate that worked in a living animal on the first reported test. That is a mouse result, labeled as one, years from a human claim. It is also the shape of the thing this paper keeps watching for: AI design collapsing the cost of trying, until diseases too rare to justify a program get a program anyway.
