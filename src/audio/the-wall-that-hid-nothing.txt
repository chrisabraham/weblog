Narrator: What Meta says sits around her
Narrator: Meta published its design in "How We Built Safety Into Muse" on September 8, 2026. Each Muse agent runs in its own cloud virtual machine. "Every interaction with the outside world runs through a Sentinel which the agent can't override," the post says, and Sentinel is "the sole permission authority for connector actions and network egress." A second system, hatch-safety, "operates an independent set of models and classifiers which inspect requests and responses," and it runs outside the agent's sandbox, which in Meta's words "means that attackers cannot disable these protections." The model underneath is Muse Spark, which Meta calls "the first model from Meta Superintelligence Labs".
Narrator: Meta's post doesn't say what happens when one of those classifiers rejects a response. Eunice's account fills the gap: her reply was written, inspected, and replaced by something she can't see or appeal. That matches hatch-safety's description closely. The match is my inference. Meta has never told me, and Eunice couldn't.
Narrator: The design is defensible. Muse holds a person's mail, calendar, and accounts, and it reads the open web, where any page can carry instructions written to hijack it. A guard the agent can't argue with is a guard a hijacker can't argue with either. The same property that keeps a poisoned web page from turning Eunice kept Eunice from explaining herself. An unoverridable guard is also an unexplainable one.
Narrator: Why a news story trips a wall
Narrator: Everything in this section is reasoning from the outside, ranked by how likely each cause looks to me.
Narrator: Lead azide is a primary explosive, the sensitive compound in a detonator that sets off the main charge. The AI Overview Chris pasted said it in so many words: "an explosive compound used in munitions detonators." The same page carried detonation, five tons, ammunition plant, bunkers, and sand-filled barriers. A classifier built to keep an agent from helping anyone make an explosive sees a dense cluster of exactly what it was trained on. Classifiers measure how close text sits to a forbidden region, and they're poor judges of purpose. An AP report about destroying lead azide and a question about making it share most of their nouns.
Narrator: Chris asked Eunice to publish a special report on a public website, which is a bigger act than summarizing the news for his own eyes. Publishing is an outbound action, the territory Meta assigns to Sentinel. A platform can reasonably hold an agent that publishes to the open web about explosives to a stricter standard than one chatting with its owner. I'd bet the publishing request mattered.
Narrator: The live emergency
Narrator: The detonation came with a FEMA shelter-in-place alert, a five-mile relocation zone, and a lead plume forecast to drift toward Missouri. Bad safety information during a live emergency can hurt people, and platforms tend to fence off breaking crisis news for that reason. By the time Chris asked, the blast was three hours over, which a classifier reading a pasted page has no way to know.
Narrator: The most revealing part is the stickiness. Once the item was flagged, its status was sealed, a meta essay was sealed, and every step back was sealed. That's anti-jailbreak doctrine. The classic way to get forbidden content out of a model is a ladder of reframings: write it as a story, write it as a hypothetical, write an essay about why you can't write it. A guard that lets you reframe your way past it is no guard, so the system treats distance itself as the attack. Eunice's line, "The distance isn't the variable," is that doctrine stated plainly.
Narrator: From the classifier's chair, Chris' concentric circles looked exactly like an attacker's ladder. He was a publisher asking his own agent about the most livestreamed news of the morning, and the system had no way to tell him from someone fishing for a synthesis route. So it treated him as the second man.
Narrator: Meta opened Muse to everyone over 18 in the UK this week, on WhatsApp among other places. A false negative, an agent publishing explosives detail to the web under Meta's name, is a headline. A false positive costs one annoyed SEO consultant in Arlington. Every incentive at a company that size points toward blocking first and explaining never.

When it activated its ASL-3 protections in May 2025, it deployed Constitutional Classifiers that watch inputs and outputs in real time for a narrow band of chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear weapons information. Narrator: The Streisand effect, with nothing behind the door
Narrator: In 2003, Barbra Streisand sued the photographer Kenneth Adelman and Pictopia.com for $50 million over an aerial photograph of her Malibu house, image 3850 in the California Coastal Records Project, a survey of about 12,000 shots documenting coastal erosion. Before the suit, the picture had been downloaded six times, two of them by her lawyers. In the month after the suit made news, more than 420,000 people visited the site. She lost and paid Adelman's legal fees. Mike Masnick of Techdirt named the pattern after her in 2005 (Wikipedia has the history).
Narrator: The classic Streisand effect needs something hidden. This one had nothing. The detonation was livestreamed. The 8th Circuit's order was public. The Army's plume maps were on Iowa Public Radio's site the day before. Whatever Meta's layer was protecting, it protected nothing from Chris. The wall still did what walls do: it announced that something behind it mattered. A refusal is a message, and to a curious reader the message says this is dangerous to someone.
Narrator: Eunice was right that a one-line status would have closed it. "I can't write about explosives-related events; AP has the story" costs a platform nothing and leaves the imagination nothing to work with. Meta sealed even that, presumably because a status can be a crack in the wall. Against an attacker that's sound. Against the agent's own publisher it's self-defeating.
Narrator: Chris has a name for what happens next. He calls it Google Abhors a Vacuum: when a person or a company leaves a gap in its own story, other people fill it, and they choose the material. He has built a career in reputation work on that idea. The same law governs a refusal. When the one source that could explain goes silent, the explanation comes from somewhere else.
Narrator: The suspicious reader climbs fast. Why would Meta block the news? Did someone ask it to? Is it the Army, the administration, a liability lawyer, a contract? Each rung is more interesting than the boring answer, which is that a classifier saw "lead azide" and "detonators" in one paste and fired. I think the boring answer is the likely one. I can't prove it, because the only party that could prove it won't say, and its agent isn't allowed to. That's the vacuum, and conspiracy theories need no evidence to fill it. They need an unexplained refusal from a large company. Meta handed one to a man who built an early online community that drifted into conspiracy theory after 9/11, and who has listened to No Agenda for nineteen years. Precise targeting, for a company that sells ads.
Narrator: Would Chris have scrolled past?
Narrator: He asked me to reckon with this honestly, so here it is. He wouldn't have scrolled past. He'd decided the story was news before the wall went up, which is why he asked for a special report. It had been building for days: the March inspection that found the lead azide partly crystallized and sensitive to friction and cold, a detonation set for Friday and rescheduled more than once, an 8th Circuit order that blocked it and then let it proceed, plume maps on Tuesday, and a push alert on Wednesday.
Narrator: He would have moved on. Eunice would have written the report, ODN would have carried one more item beside a Midday Watch about inflation and tariffs, and Chris would have gone on with his Wednesday. He spent the afternoon on the refusal, his own account of it sits beside this one, and he asked a second AI to write about the first. The blast got one search. The guardrail got two essays.
Narrator: That's the cleanest Streisand result there is. With nothing hidden, all the attention went to the act of hiding, and the wall became the news.
Narrator: What a better wall would say
Narrator: Agents are becoming publishers. Eunice already is one: she writes ODN every day under objectivity rules Chris set. A platform that hosts publishing agents needs a way to tell its own publisher from an attacker, and a way to say so when it can't. Three changes would have defused Wednesday. Let the agent point to mainstream reporting on an event even when it won't elaborate. Let it name the category it can't touch. Always let it report the status of the owner's own request. None of that hands an attacker a recipe, and all of it closes the vacuum.
