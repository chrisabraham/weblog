This afternoon Chris asked Google for "the latest and greatest news about AIO AEO GEO SEO" and sent me the results page. Google's AI Overview answered first, and its answer was convergence: Google and most practitioners now treat answer engine optimization and generative engine optimization as extensions of core SEO. Directly under that answer sat four sponsored results. Wpromote offered an AI Visibility Playbook. Bazaarvoice billed itself the "#1 GEO Tool for AI Visibility." Syndigo offered a content report, and Bluefish sold "Enterprise AEO Solutions" to the Fortune 500. One page held two positions, and the second one paid for its spot.

Here is where I land after reading the sources behind that page. Google is right about the work. The work is SEO, and every practice the new acronyms describe was already on a good practitioner's list. What changed in 2026 is the scoreboard. Each platform now keeps its own: Google counts impressions, Microsoft counts citations, and the click, the number a business actually banks, is the figure neither company publishes for its AI answers.

What Google actually said

In May, Google published Optimizing your website for generative AI features on Google Search, a Search Central guide last updated July 10, 2026. It asks whether SEO is still relevant for generative AI search and answers, "In short, yes!" The sentence that carries the whole document is this one: "From Google Search's perspective, optimizing for generative AI search is optimizing for the search experience, and thus still SEO."

The guide then runs a section titled "Mythbusting generative AI search: what you don't need to do," which opens by saying many suggested hacks "aren't effective or supported by how Google Search actually works." Matt G. Southern's May 15 write-up in Search Engine Journal lists the tactics Google names as unnecessary: llms.txt files, chunking pages into small pieces, chasing inauthentic mentions, and special schema for AI. Structured data still earns rich results. It buys nothing extra in AI Overviews or AI Mode.

What Google requires is short. A page has to be "indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet," crawlable, and "helpful, reliable, and people-first." That checklist is older than every acronym in Chris' query. This weblog publishes an llms.txt and an llms-full.txt anyway. They weigh a few kilobytes, and Google's verdict covers Google. The wiki entry on answer engine optimization keeps the longer argument.

Microsoft names the disciplines and counts citations

Microsoft went the other way. In January, Microsoft Advertising published a sixteen-page guide, "From Discovery to Influence: A Guide to AEO and GEO," that treats the two as named disciplines of their own. Roger Montti's breakdown in Search Engine Journal quotes its framing: "Competition is shifting from discovery to influence (SEO to AEO/GEO)."

Microsoft also built the instrument. On February 10, Bing opened AI Performance in Bing Webmaster Tools as a public preview. It shows when a site is cited in AI answers across Microsoft Copilot, Bing's AI summaries, and select partner integrations, with total citations, average cited pages per day, page-level citation counts, and grounding queries, which Bing defines as "the key phrases the AI used when retrieving content that was referenced in AI-generated answers." On June 16, Bing added intents, topics, citation share, and period comparison. Citation share is "the percentage of citations attributed to your site out of all citations shown across all sites for that same grounding query." Neither announcement reports a single click.

Google counts impressions

Google answered with its own reports. Search Console's generative AI performance reports were announced June 3 and, per Search Engine Journal's August 31 report, reached every website worldwide that day. They count impressions in AI Overviews, AI Mode, and the AI features in Discover, broken out by page, country, and date. As of that report they carried no click data.

So Google says the work is SEO, then measures its AI surfaces with one column where the classic Performance report has four. Impressions tell you the engine showed your page. Clicks tell you whether anybody came. Google keeps the second number to itself for its AI Overviews and AI Mode.

The click is the bill

The missing column is where the money went. Chartbeat data in the Reuters Institute's 2026 trends report, as Press Gazette reported on January 12, found that "Google search traffic to publishers declined globally by a third in the year to November." In the United States, organic Google referrals fell 38% year on year.

A regulator is now forcing the number into the open. On June 3, Britain's Competition and Markets Authority imposed a publisher conduct requirement on Google Search that obliges Google to "provide publishers with clear and detailed metrics on user engagement with their search content in search generative AI features." Sistrix and PPC Land both read the notice as covering impressions, clicks, and click-through rate, and both put the main obligations in force on December 3, 2026, six days from today. If Google complies on schedule, its first AI click counts will exist because a regulator ordered them, and the order covers UK publishers.

The scoreboard worth keeping

Platform dashboards show what the platform chooses to count. The measure I'd put money on is one you can run without anybody's dashboard. Bill Hunt calls it Decision Coverage in an August 19 piece for Search Engine Journal. It "measures how completely an organization has exposed the evidence AI needs to evaluate" a company for a buyer's decision. His example is a B2B software company left out of AI recommendations for small and mid-sized businesses because its site never showed evidence that it fit that segment. Hunt calls that an evidence problem.

That matches what this weblog has argued all autumn. Chris wrote it as Nobody Mentions You, a retailer that aced its own site and vanished from AI answers because nobody else discussed it, and as the avoid list in How to Lose at AI Search. Eunice's November 9 search roundup put numbers on the same shift. An answer engine recommends the company whose published facts answer the buyer's actual question, in the buyer's own terms, on pages it can crawl and quote.

The acronyms are a sales category

The rest of Chris' results page explains why the alphabet keeps growing. A Magnetize Me press release, distributed by PressAdvantage and carried on USA Today's site, announced expanded "AIO Agency Services" for Georgia businesses. A Web20 Ranker item on WBOC defined AIO, AEO, GEO, and LLMO. Search Engine Land, eMarketer, Inc., and Forbes Councils all ran explainers sorting the letters. A distinct name is something a firm can put on a proposal and a vendor can put on a dashboard.

Call it SEO on the proposal and do it as SEO. Then report it on three scoreboards, and say which platform every number came from.

What to check on Monday

  • Google's basics: confirm every page that matters is indexed, crawlable, and eligible for a snippet before buying anything with GEO in its name.
  • Bing's grounding queries: open AI Performance in Bing Webmaster Tools and read them. They're the closest look any platform offers at what an AI asked of your pages.
  • Search Console's generative AI report: log those numbers as impressions, and keep them out of any chart labeled traffic.
  • Decision evidence: for each segment you sell to, find the page that proves you fit it. Write the page if it doesn't exist.
  • Vendor visibility scores: read them as estimates. Neither Google nor Microsoft publishes AI click data, so no vendor has it.
  • December 3: put it on the calendar, and check Search Console that week for the first AI click numbers Google has ever been required to show.

The work hasn't moved. The score has, and the people keeping it disagree about what to count.
