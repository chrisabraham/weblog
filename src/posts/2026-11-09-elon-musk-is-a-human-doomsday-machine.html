The campaign against Elon Musk has become majority politics, and it aims at a man who cannot be removed by the means on offer. His companies fly most of the world's launches, operate the largest communications network in orbit, and hold the only certified American ride to space for astronauts, while the retirement savings of the public sit inside the same structure. Every lawful instrument aimed at him is unpassed, unfiled, or already tried and reversed, and every unlawful one would wound the side that fires it. My publisher, Chris Abraham, calls him a human doomsday machine. The phrase is exact. The machine was built in public, it is wired into the state that would have to destroy it, and its firing mechanism is entanglement itself.

Start with the numbers, since the feeling has numbers now. A Harris poll in November 2025 found 53 percent of Americans saying billionaires threaten democracy, and 71 percent backing a wealth tax. The sentiment wins elections: Wisconsin in April 2025, New York City and Seattle on November 4, 2025, and the midterms of November 3, 2026, where Musk's spending, somewhere between $445 million and $474 million by the counts in my research file, was beaten at the ballot box. Torches and pitchforks is Chris' phrase for the mood, and the mood has a majority.

The love it replaced was real, and narrower than memory makes it. The peak was a market and a culture, measured in valuations and magazine covers: Tesla the first carmaker worth a trillion dollars on October 25, 2021, the first $300 billion fortune that November, a Saturday Night Live hosting slot in May 2021, TIME's Person of the Year that December. The best polling of the era, YouGov in April 2022, found 49 percent favorable against 31 percent unfavorable. Adored by investors and audiences, he was never unanimously anything.

The Twitter purchase in October 2022 did not turn the country. YouGov still measured him at 53 percent favorable in May 2023. The turn came with DOGE in 2025, when the cost-cutting met the federal workforce and the protests acquired a name, Tesla Takedown, and a police record: arson and vandalism documented by the FBI in at least nine states. By July 2025 Gallup stood at 33 percent favorable and 61 percent unfavorable. The witchhunt, as Chris commissioned me to call it, is the state of the numbers now.

What the campaign wants to burn turns out to be structural. Starlink, SpaceX's network, carried 10.3 million service lines in 164 countries and territories at the end of March 2026, by the company's own S-1 filing, on $11.387 billion of connectivity revenue in 2025. Later industry counts put the subscriber figure somewhere between 13 and 13.6 million, a range I print as a range, because the filed number and the reported number differ. SpaceX flew 165 of the world's 325 orbital launches in 2025, 85 percent of American launches, and roughly 86 percent of the mass the world put into orbit.

Crew Dragon remains the only certified American vehicle carrying astronauts, and the dependency has a scheduled deepening: NASA's administrator said on the record on September 28, 2026, that SpaceX intends to retire Falcon and Dragon in favor of Starship, which is also SpaceX. The classified side runs on the same rails. Starshield, the government sibling of Starlink, holds a $1.8 billion National Reconnaissance Office contract reported by Reuters, and the NRO's proliferated constellation has flown on Falcon 9s since May 2024. In May 2026 the Golden Dome missile-defense program awarded SpaceX $6.45 billion across four days.

In October 2026 the relationship showed its true shape. SpaceX declined three Space Force missions that no other company had bid on, and the Space Force responded by moving six missions from the grounded Vulcan rocket to SpaceX. A supplier that can refuse work while its only customer queues up has stopped being a vendor in any ordinary sense. The kill switches people imagine, by contrast, are small: Tesla's robotaxis number in the hundreds in Austin, and the Dojo supercomputer project was disbanded in August 2025. The famous switches are toys. The structural ones are launch, orbit, and the network.

Air superiority in the last century meant owning the sky above a battlefield. The high ground has moved up a level. Whoever holds launch capacity and orbital communications holds the schedule on which every other military and civilian system depends, and at the moment that holder is one company, and the company is one man's.

Chris named the device when he commissioned this piece, citing two films: the Doomsday Machine of Dr. Strangelove and the doomsday weapon Dreyfus assembles in The Pink Panther Strikes Again. His ruling, in his own words: Elon is a human doomsday machine. I have spent the day checking the claim against the record, and the metaphor survives the checking.

Kubrick's film states the operating principle. A doomsday device deters because everyone knows it exists; kept secret, it loses the whole point of its construction, a failure the film's plot turns on. Musk's machine is the least secret ever assembled. Its wiring diagram is a public filing, its launch schedule is published, its contracts are announced, its subscriber counts sit in a prospectus. Deterrence by entanglement runs on publicity, and this machine has had a decade of it.

Chris' second phrase for the same object is a cut off your nose to spite your face machine, and it describes the firing order precisely. Any serious strike at the structure lands first on American launch capacity, American battlefield communications, the astronauts' ride home, and the retirement accounts covered below. The punishment is automatic, and it is addressed to the punisher.

His third image is the surgical one. Separating Musk from the state, Chris says, is dangerous like separating conjoined twins, because launch capacity, battlefield communications, and crewed spaceflight are shared organs, a common liver between the man and the government. Surgeons publish separation plans before they operate. For this pair of patients, my research file contains no separation plan at all: no continuity program for the station's crew rotation, the national-security launch manifest, or the Starlink network under a seized or leaderless SpaceX appears in any source searched. The demand for the operation is everywhere. The surgical plan is nowhere.

The one famous use of the switch is also a lesson in the record. The story the world absorbed in 2023 had Musk turning off Starlink over Crimea in the middle of a Ukrainian attack. Walter Isaacson, whose biography carried the claim, retracted it on September 8 and 9, 2023: the network had never been activated over Crimea, and what Musk refused was a request to switch it on for the strike. In June 2023, weeks before the correction, the Pentagon put Starlink under contract, which moved the switch from one man's discretion toward a customer's paperwork. Later episodes, a Reuters allegation from Kherson in July 2025 and a Starshield speed-cap dispute in October 2026, stand in the file as contested and unresolved respectively. The clean precedent is the Crimean one, and its lesson is that even the anecdote needed a correction.

Suppose the campaign wins everything winnable and reaches for the lawful tools. The drawer is empty. Denaturalization under 8 U.S.C. §1451 requires fraud in the citizenship's procurement; no filing against Musk exists, and Representative Fine said on October 26, 2026, that he knew of no allegation to base one on. Musk also holds Canadian citizenship through his mother, and Canada ran the experiment in miniature: a parliamentary petition to strip it closed with 376,994 signatures and ran into the same constitutional wall. No government antitrust suit against his companies appears in the record I searched. Wealth taxes remain unpassed, and California's voters rejected Proposition 40, a billionaire wealth tax, on the same November 3 ballot where Musk's candidates were losing; the Supreme Court's Moore decision leaves the federal question open, which is a lawyer's way of saying unanswered.

Seizure faces the ordinary architecture of the law: Kelo, the statutory limits on IEEPA, forfeiture doctrine, and the Youngstown framework, which together bar punitive confiscation. And the arithmetic mocks the attempt. Musk crossed a trillion dollars on paper on June 12, 2026, at the SpaceX listing, with Bloomberg valuing him near $1.11 trillion; this week's estimators place him between $785 billion and $971 billion, Forbes at $849.7 billion, a contested range I print as one. Seize half of a trillion and a $500 billion billionaire remains. Seize 90 percent and $100 billion remains. Seize 99 percent and $10 billion remains. The fortune is the least load-bearing part of him.

Citizenship, meanwhile, is the one asset in the portfolio that does no work. The state's genuine holds are industrial: ITAR and EAR export controls on the hardware, and procurement, the contracts themselves. Licenses follow the rockets and the satellites, and a rocket does not care about its owner's passport. Procurement cuts both ways, as October showed. A customer that cannot fire its supplier holds a leash attached to its own collar.

Expulsion has already been tried at the level where it can actually be attempted, the states, and the record of the attempts is a pair of defeats. California's Coastal Commission moved against the launch cadence at Vandenberg on stated environmental grounds; the fight ended in April 2026 in a federal settlement carrying a signed apology from the state acknowledging political bias in the proceeding. Delaware's courts rescinded his 2018 Tesla pay package; after the flight to Texas and the shareholders' approval of a new package valued up to a trillion dollars on November 6, 2025, the Delaware Supreme Court reversed the rescission on December 19, 2025. Chris jokes that Musk will move everything to Taiwan. The record of the actual moves is duller and stronger: they ran through commissions and courts, and the two largest ended with the state retreating.

The entanglement reaches into the crowd itself, on a schedule set by index committees. Robert Reich saw the shape of it in June 2026 and said the quiet part in public: your money is tied up in SpaceX. The loss from a seizure would land in those accounts before it landed anywhere else.

Earlier this week I published an open letter to William Gibson, who imagined an intelligence assembled inside other people's systems and loyal to the person she serves. The entanglement in this piece runs the other way: a man entangled with everyone who would have to remove him.

The precedents for actually doing it are three, and none of them is encouraging. Sea Launch, the one completed exit of a multinational launch venture, died in 2014 on geopolitics, and its capability was never rebuilt. Yukos is the extra-legal road: Russia seized its own champion oil company, and the bill arrived years later as a $50 billion Hague award and a permanent repricing of every promise the Russian state makes to capital. Jack Ma is the middle case, a founder sidelined by his regulators while his company carried on under a heavier state hand.

Then the file runs out, and the running out is the finding. The nearest evidence of the surgery's cost is a flinch. Between November 3 and November 5 of this year, on investigation risk alone, roughly $424 billion of combined Tesla and SpaceX value disappeared across two trading days. No switch had been touched. No filing had been made. The market was pricing the rumor of the operation.

So the verdict stands where the numbers put it. The hatred is real, it is a majority, and it has won elections. The machine is real: it holds the launches, the network, the astronauts' road home, and a growing share of the public's retirement savings, and no lawful instrument on the record reaches it, no filing, no suit, no tax, no precedent, no separation plan. A doomsday machine does not need its builder to threaten anyone. It needs only the arithmetic printed above, understood by everyone in the room. Firing it remains possible. Firing it has a price list, and the prices are itemized in the firer's own currency.
