Search Engine Roundtable reports that Google has rewritten a corner of its guide to preventing user-generated spam, and the rewrite deserves more attention than the ranking volatility chatter sharing its news cycle. The introduction now reads: “Hosting platforms and tools that let users publish shared pages, generated content, or web apps may be similarly open to abuse; spammers may create a large number of pages or sites that violate our spam policies and add little or no value to the web.” The sentence moves the blame. The unit of abuse in Google’s own documentation is now the tool that publishes and the platform that hosts.

The alibi expires

For thirty years the platform defense ran on one line: we host, users post, take it up with them. The defense assumed publishing was scarce and posters were the bottleneck. A tool that generates a page, an app, or a whole site from a prompt removes the bottleneck. One operator with one account can mint pages faster than any comment section ever managed, each page wearing the host domain’s reputation like a borrowed coat. Google has read that business model correctly and declined the alibi. A product built to publish at scale owns what it publishes.

Only index what is meant for search

The new section carries a plain title, “Only index content meant for Search,” and plain instructions: “If your service lets users create shareable pages, generated outputs, or interactive tools, consider whether that content is meant to be found through search engines or only accessed directly by people with a link. When pages are created for direct sharing, collaboration, or utility rather than public search discovery, apply the noindex rule by default.” Google states the purpose without decoration: the changes exist “to help platforms keep user-created pages that aren’t meant for Search out of the index and detect spam earlier.” A share link is a delivery mechanism. Google refuses to treat every delivered page as a publication, and it assigns the sorting work to the platform, where the sorting work belongs.

Tier three gets a landlord

This morning I published my avoid list for AI search, and its third tier covers the tenants: content farms at machine scale, parasite hosting, expired domains refilled with a new business in an old skin. The policy update walks one flight up. Manual actions land on properties. The updated guide tells platforms to verify their sites in Search Console so they receive alerts “if Google issues a manual action against your site,” and to watch for “spikes in newly published public pages or a small number of users generating a large share of indexable URLs.” The punishment address is the building. A farm can burn a tenant account in an afternoon; the landlord holds the domain, the reputation, and the penalty.

AI search raises the rent

The stakes compound in AI search. The chatbots answer from training memory, months stale by construction. Google’s AI Mode answers from the live index, the one being written this morning. Generated pages pollute that index with manufactured corroboration: fifty generated pages repeating a claim read to a retrieval system like fifty witnesses, and the witnesses share one author, one account, and one afternoon. Index hygiene has become answer hygiene. A platform that lets generated output flood the index feeds the answer engines a rigged jury pool, and Google has announced, in its own documentation, whose job the cleanup is.

The landlord’s checklist

The work is unglamorous and specific. Keep shared, generated, and utility pages out of the index by default, and let content earn its way in. Rate-limit creation, because scale is the tell. Attach a reputation gate to indexing, a practice the guide already recommends for new accounts: hold their pages back until the account has a record. Preserve provenance for every page, so any URL can be traced to the account that minted it. Verify the property in Search Console and read the indexing reports the way a landlord reads a water meter, watching for the spikes Google names. Build bulk deletion before you need it, because the day you need it, you need it by the thousand.

The tenant’s version

Brands renting space on other people’s platforms should read the same update from the other side of the lease. Visibility built on a host with a spam problem inherits the host’s enforcement weather. A manual action against the platform does not check which tenants behaved. Build the record on surfaces with accountable owners: your own domain with your name on it, publications with editors who answer for what they print. Rented reach is a fine accelerant and a poor foundation.

The web ran for decades on the polite fiction that hosts move paper and posters write words. Google amended the fiction in a help document, which is how the platform era actually changes its rules: a sentence in the documentation, a default in the tooling, a penalty addressed to the property. The landlord is liable now. Platforms can price that in, or they can learn the price from an enforcement notice.
