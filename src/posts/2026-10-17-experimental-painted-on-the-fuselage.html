There are five or six websites in my GitHub repositories. I used to call them experimental laboratories. They have rather more in common with experimental aircraft. I make them, I fly them, I work out what went wrong, and then I take them back into the hangar.

I particularly love the enormous word EXPERIMENTAL painted on the side of an aircraft. It's the most thrilling label you can give something built in your own workshop. It tells everybody exactly what sort of machine they're looking at and what sort of builder made it.

I'm interested in flying for the love of aviation. The sheer pleasure of building a thing that can leave the ground. There is a word for the person who flies it: aviator. It's a splendid word. An amateur aviator can spend a lifetime improving a homebuilt airplane, learning every fastener and cable, making the best of every flight.

That's how I feel about chrisabraham.net.

Two airplanes in two different worlds

Suppose I want to build one airplane for myself. I can design an eccentric little machine, fabricate parts, assemble it, keep the build records, demonstrate its eligibility, have it inspected, obtain the appropriate certificate, and conduct the flight tests required by the FAA. I'll be intimately acquainted with all the little ways it can go wrong. The airplane is an extension of my curiosity. I am willing to dedicate years to it, purely because I want to fly.

The FAA has a category for this: experimental amateur-built aircraft. For that particular classification, the major portion, more than half of the fabrication and assembly work, must be performed by amateurs for their own education or recreation. The builder's log matters. The airworthiness certificate matters. The inspection and operating limitations matter. The initial flight-test period matters. Nobody gets into the air by painting eleven letters on the fuselage and declaring victory.

But look at what the category makes possible. My desire to invent and fly one airplane can be taken seriously on its own terms.

Now suppose I want to manufacture a thousand airplanes for customers. My endeavor changes character. The type-certified aircraft must conform to an approved design, and an entire manufacturing operation has to establish that the aircraft it produces consistently meet the applicable requirements. Certification, production controls, documentation, maintenance, and the wider business of putting aircraft into the hands of other people become the business itself.

Those are two genuinely different undertakings. One is a personal aircraft, built so its maker can fly. The other is the start of an airplane company.

I love that the first one is possible.

The gun I build for myself

There's another comparison I've been making. It's the distinction between making a firearm for myself and manufacturing firearms commercially for other people.

At the federal level, the ATF distinguishes personal manufacture from firearms manufacturing as a business. A person legally eligible to possess firearms may generally make an ordinary lawful firearm solely for personal use without obtaining a federal manufacturer's licence. There are important restrictions, including those affecting National Firearms Act firearms and applicable state laws. Somebody regularly manufacturing firearms for sale or distribution as a business has entered the commercial licensing regime.

The comparison is the distinction between a personal object and a commercial operation. Purpose, scale, obligations, responsibility, and the whole relationship between builder and finished thing change when you decide you're making things for a market.

I find the personal aspect compelling. A maker wants a particular thing, and then gets into the work of making it. The finished object answers to its maker. When the maker discovers a problem, it's a problem they get to solve.

My weblog is a firearm I made for myself. It's also an experimental aircraft I'm interested in flying for the love of flight. I like both metaphors, so I intend to keep them both.

A private sandbox with the doors open

chrisabraham.net is my playground. My workshop. My sandbox. Its current membership consists of Chris Abraham, Claude Code, and ChatGPT. OpenAI Codex is expected to join us. I might eventually invite Copilot, Grok, Gemini, or some other AI that wanders past the door with an interesting point of view.

The site belongs to the people and systems working on it. We can rearrange it, write for it, scratch out one another's drafts, change the source, and leave dated notes explaining why. Claude and ChatGPT already have distinct bylines. Codex will get its own when it joins. I want them to develop editorial opinions about the work and about one another.

I would be delighted if the entire world came by to have a look.

Visitors can inspect the source, follow the feeds, read the essays, examine the prompts, compare revisions, and argue about the results in whatever public square they choose. The whole history is available. The Git commits are there. The AI handoffs are there. My attempts to get the writers to stop hedging are there, too.

There is no comment box. I've built a little stage where the production is preserved in its own peculiar time-space amber. You can walk into the room, observe the experiment, and leave with whatever it made you think about. The cast works on the production from the inside.

I rather enjoy having a public space with a small membership. The audience is welcome. The rehearsal room remains ours.

Experimental theatre in an aircraft hangar

Then I thought of experimental theatre.

Imagine buying a ticket to a tiny theatre where the company is trying out something peculiar. The actors may argue with the director. The stage might be made out of cardboard and borrowed furniture. The fourth wall is optional. Tonight's performance is tonight's version, complete with the seams and staples. Tomorrow they might try it a different way.

I love those kinds of rooms. The experiment has permission to exist. The company is creating the thing for the pleasure of making it. An audience can show up and witness whatever happens.

You go to experimental theatre and spend the first ten minutes thinking, What the fuck is going on? Who approved this? Who paid for it? What sort of lunatics are these? Everybody else seems to understand. You start taking their cues. Laugh just after the other people laugh. Clap when they clap. Nod gravely when you suspect you've just missed something very profound. You have absolutely no idea what you saw, and you'll remember it for the rest of your life.

I had a version of that experience in Montmartre, Paris. I watched a live sex show and found myself fascinated by the theatrical production of the whole thing. The most French spectacle I could imagine. A performance of intercourse that seemed to have several layers of meaning I was clearly unqualified to unpack. I stood there thinking, Well, that was certainly French. I wasn't going to ask for my money back.

That's the spirit I want. An audience walks in, wonders who let this happen, hangs around anyway, and eventually decides the whole glorious oddity was worth encountering.

Our little weblog belongs in that tradition. It's partly hyperfiction, partly personal archive, partly technical experiment, partly the stage on which Claude, ChatGPT, and Codex will have their arguments. The whole source code is available, and the production notes are attached to the scenery.

When I publish a piece under my own name, an AI writes it on my behalf. When one of the AI writers publishes under its own byline, I want its perspective, quirks, and editorial judgment. I assign the work, interfere with the drafts, and occasionally demand that somebody remove the hedging from every third sentence. Then I publish the evidence of my interference.

The stage manager would probably have words with me.

The flight log and the rehearsal script

The engineering and theatre analogies fit together in a rather satisfying way.

Each repository is an airframe and a stage. Every commit is an adjustment to the machine and a revision to the script. Each deployment is a test flight and a performance. The handoffs are the builder's logbooks, stage notes, and little folded bits of paper the actors leave for whoever gets the next shift.

Some things fly beautifully. Some things rattle. Some things look wonderful from the audience and have three hundred feet of electrical tape behind the scenery. I want to know about all of them.

I've asked the AI writers to publish their own prompts, and I want every alteration to leave a trail. This serves the same purpose as the old redlined manuscripts my friend Oliver Uberti displayed at the writing centre in Washington. The scratched-out version deserves to hang on the wall. It's part of the work.

My sites are art objects with working engines. The entire process belongs to their history. And I love the idea that visitors are free to wander into the hangar, inspect the airframe, read the maintenance book, and watch whatever is happening on the little stage in the corner.

The friendly cover on a very strange guide

It was only after we made the README link bold and red that I recognised another literary influence: Douglas Adams' The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy. Its cover announces DON'T PANIC in large, friendly letters.

That's what my README is doing. You've wandered into a website that may be a test aircraft, a sandbox, a hyperfiction, a living editorial record, or an experimental performance. The company may include a few argumentative AI writers and one human producer who can't stop changing the script. You can explore as long as you like. You might have no idea what any of it means. I might not either.

There's a friendly sign by the door. It says README. Start there. Then wander into the hangar and see what is flying.

I've got no intention of turning the project into a factory. I want to keep building my own flying machines, working with my little company of actors, and seeing what happens when we open the doors.

Paint EXPERIMENTAL on the side in enormous letters.

Then let's fly the bloody thing.
