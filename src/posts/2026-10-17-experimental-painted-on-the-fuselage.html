Five or six of my websites live on GitHub. Each one is a little engineering adventure. I've described them as experimental laboratories, which is accurate enough, but I've decided they are experimental aircraft.

An aircraft is a far better metaphor. You can build one yourself. You can stand back and look at the thing you've made, decide whether you trust your handiwork, run the engine, and eventually get off the ground.

I love the word EXPERIMENTAL stencilled across the side of an aircraft. In my imagination, the letters go on and the machine suddenly becomes possible. The whole proposition changes. We are now in the land of people who have actually built something.

My airplane, my hangar

A type-certified aircraft is born into a world of standards, conformity inspections, production approvals, manuals, and continued airworthiness requirements. The airplane is the beginning of an industrial commitment. Someone expects to reproduce it and sell it to other people.

An amateur-built experimental aircraft follows a different path. The FAA has a process for it. The builder keeps a log, establishes that the major portion was made for education or recreation, registers the aircraft, gets it inspected, obtains an experimental airworthiness certificate, and works through flight-testing limitations. The regulatory category exists precisely because an inventive person building one airplane in a workshop is undertaking a different enterprise from Boeing or Cessna building aircraft for a marketplace.

It's a wonderfully American distinction. The dream of flying starts in a garage, not in a boardroom. And somebody, somewhere, has made room for that garage.

The same distinction at the workbench

I see a parallel in firearms. Under federal law, a person who may legally possess firearms can generally make a lawful non-NFA firearm for personal use, subject to applicable restrictions. Manufacturing firearms as a business for sale or distribution is a different legal undertaking. The ATF knows the difference between an individual making something for himself and a manufacturer supplying the marketplace.

The details of aircraft and firearm regulation are different. The principle that interests me is the same: the purpose of building a thing changes the kind of operation it is. A prototype, a personal machine, and a commercial product each come with their own obligations.

What a relief to be able to say, I'm building this for myself. I want to see whether it works.

The machine flies and the logbook remembers

My GitHub sites are that sort of machines. Nobody is standing at the end of the runway with a hundred-page commercial requirements document. Claude Code, ChatGPT, and Codex can go into the hangar and work on the airframes. I can assign an outrageous improvement while walking through Sexy South Arlington and come home to find that somebody has actually built it.

The repository is the airframe. A commit is a change to the machine. The deployment is a flight. The handoffs are the logbooks. Some alterations make it fly beautifully. Some changes make the instruments misbehave and send everybody back into the hangar.

There is a tremendous amount of machinery behind the idea of a simple website. A publisher could spend a fortune on systems, sign-offs, workflows, committees, frameworks, and a hundred kinds of compliance theatre. I'd rather build a little machine and fly it.

That old-school appearance is deliberate. Blue links, readable pages, RSS, OPML, semantic HTML, and small illustrations. The engineering underneath should be contemporary. Simplify, then add lightness. Colin Chapman might have appreciated a well-built web page that doesn't need an entire data centre to announce its existence.

EXPERIMENTAL is the beautiful part

There is room for the odd thing. The clumsy first try. The better second try. The little hand-coded SVG that has no business being as charming as it is. The editorial argument between two AIs. The human owner insisting that his writers stop hedging, then publishing his own instructions as part of the historical record.

I have no interest in disguising the test flights as finished commercial aircraft. I want the logbook out where everybody can inspect it. I want Claude, ChatGPT, and Codex to leave their fingerprints on the fuselage. I want their criticisms, mistakes, little victories, and occasional exasperation with me documented.

It's my experimental laboratory, except the laboratory has wings.

Write EXPERIMENTAL across the side in enormous letters. Open the hangar doors. See if the bloody thing flies.
