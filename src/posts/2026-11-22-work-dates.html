People do not hire the work. They hire the feeling the work comes wrapped in.

A commodity service sells a task, and a task gets shopped against every other pair of hands that can do it. A premium service sells a memory, an experience, a date. The client falls for his people—platonically, completely—and the falling is the retention. If a client likes playing with you, he hires you more, because hiring you is how he gets to play at work. The deliverable is the hours spent with someone he likes being with. The work rides along.

The work lunch is a date

The work lunch is a date with a business pretext, and everybody at the table knows it except the person who wrote the agenda.

The agenda is the excuse. The company is the product. Two hours across a table from someone sharp, funny, and glad to see you is a good that busy men and women cannot buy retail anywhere else in their week. Their calendars are full of people who want things from them. A lunch with the person they hired to think alongside them is the one appointment that gives something back.

So the client renews. He renews the feeling of being known, being chosen, being attended to by someone whose company he would pick on purpose. Nobody ever renewed a vendor because the spreadsheet was beautiful. They renew the people they would miss.

This is courtship with the romance filed off, and it runs the whole economy of professional work. The professional who believes he is selling deliverables is competing against the professional who knows he is selling Tuesdays the client looks forward to. Only one of them gets shopped on price.

A client who likes his man shows the machinery. He manufactures reasons to meet. The agenda grows items that did not need a room. The call that was booked for thirty minutes runs ninety because neither man is in a hurry to hang up, and the last hour was the best thinking either of them did all week. Asked why he pays what he pays, he will talk about results, because that is the respectable answer. A cheaper man with the same results leaves him mourning. Men do not mourn vendors. They mourn company.

The feeling is also a moat. A competitor can underbid your task in an afternoon. He cannot underbid the client's memory of every good lunch, every call that ran long, every evening you hosted him properly. That inventory took years to build and it does not transfer. The client who switches saves money and loses his Tuesdays, and he knows it before he signs.

Cigars, strip bars, and the third drink

The old client-building had a wardrobe, and it was not a suit. It was the cigar you did not usually smoke. The strip bar you did not usually visit. The cigarette on the sidewalk. The third drink you would never have ordered alone.

The companion who led you there was not corrupting the work. That was the work, in its native form: one man giving another man an evening he would remember, and a small shared transgression to seal it. A mild vice, taken together, is glue. It says: we are not vendor and buyer tonight. We are co-conspirators. The contract signed the next morning was paperwork. The relationship had been signed the night before.

Every generation believes it invented relationship marketing. Every generation is wrong. The forms get cleaner and the expense reports get stricter, and the underlying transaction never changes: time, attention, and an experience worth retelling, exchanged for loyalty that no bid process can pry loose.

The modern descendants wear better clothes. The golf round. The fishing boat. The conference in Scottsdale that could have been a video call, at a resort nobody would book for a meeting that mattered on paper. The offsite. The team dinner where the seating is the strategy. Compliance departments sand the edges off all of it, and the thing itself survives every sanding, because it was never about the cigar or the boat. It is about one person telling another, in the only language that cannot be faked cheaply: my time is yours, and I am enjoying giving it to you.

My father, the host

My father, Bob Abraham, was a photographer in Honolulu. The photographs were excellent, and the photographs were not the whole job. When his Japanese clients came to town from Japan, the job included the night.

The night meant strip bars, and it meant the hostess bars that everybody in Honolulu called buy-me-drinky bars. The institution came over from Japan, where the hostess club—the kyabakura—is a standard room of business life. In Honolulu the proprietors were largely Korean and Japanese, and the scene ran big through the 1980s and 1990s, along the Keʻeaumoku and Kapiʻolani corridor.

Here is how the room worked. A mamasan ran it—an older woman who watched over the girls, managed the floor, and kept the tabs running. The ladies sat with you. They hung out with you. They doted on you. There was no sex in it and no stripping. A glass of champagne cost fifty dollars. A glass of orange juice cost twenty-five.

And the ladies' own expensive drinks were, a good part of the time, juice or tea poured from premium-looking bottles. The twenty-five-dollar orange juice was often literally orange juice. That was not a scam layered on top of the business. That was the business, engineered honestly: a hostess who drank real champagne all night could not work a long shift, and a client who understood the arrangement understood he had never been buying beverages in the first place.

Nobody at that table was buying drinks. The client was buying the evening—the attention, the welcome, the feeling of being hosted by beautiful women who were glad he came. My father understood the assignment completely. The camera got him hired. The hosting got him rehired. The photographs were the excuse. The hosting was the product.

The clients' side of the table had its own arithmetic. These were men far from home, doing business in a place that ran on being hosted, working in a business culture where the evening is where trust gets audited. Anybody can perform competence across a desk at two in the afternoon. The evening shows you who a man is when the performance stops—whether he is generous, whether he is careful with people, whether his attention is real. A host who took you out properly had passed an examination no proposal can administer. That is why the institution was important from Japan, and why it took root in Hawaii, where my father plied it: the photographer's real portfolio was the nights he could be trusted to run.

Banking memories

The price of that evening is the whole theory. Of every dollar spent in those bars, ten cents bought the night as it happened: the room, the drink, the company, live. The other eighty or ninety cents bought the replay—the touch, the attention, the respect of pretty ladies, rerun in the mind for years afterward. The men were banking memories. The deposit was expensive because the replay is what lasts, and the bank paid out every time the story got told.

I set out the pricing half of this argument this morning in Nobody Pays a Proportion: the pool sets the price of everything fungible, and people pay without limit for the visible, the memorable, the experience. The buy-me-drinky bar is that law with the lights low. A fifty-dollar glass of champagne is a terrible price for champagne. It is a fair price for a decade of remembering the night you drank it.

Some men are built to collect the full value. Hyperfantasia is the word for mental imagery so vivid that the evening reruns in color, on demand, forever. A man with hyperfantasia leaves the bar carrying ninety percent of what he paid for, and it compounds. Every retelling is a withdrawal that does not reduce the balance.

I have aphantasia. There is no theater in my head and the film does not exist. I cannot rerun the evening; I can only report it. So for me the product on sale in that bar is worth a tenth of its price, because the other ninety percent is a good I cannot take delivery of. Same room, same ladies, same tab: one customer buys a decade of replays, and the other buys a night he will describe from notes. The difference between us is not taste. It is storage.

This is the hotel-room law seen from inside the bar. A five-hundred-dollar room is a moment on the lips and a lifetime on the credit card—for me. For the man who can replay it, the room is a vault. People are not irrational when they pay for memories. They are buying the one asset their own minds will store for free and appreciate forever. I stand outside that market the way a man without a freezer stands outside a sale on ice cream: I can see exactly why the line is long.

Out of the pit

The whole game of business, underneath every tactic, is this: find out how to sell your stuff as organic instead of selling it as pork bellies or frozen orange juice.

Pork bellies and frozen orange juice are the purest commodities ever traded—graded, fungible, faceless, priced by the pit. Nobody has a relationship with a pork belly. Nobody pays a premium for the memory of one. In the pit there is exactly one question, and it is the bid.

Leaving the commodities market makes a business more competitive at everything it does. That is why serious money flows to marketing, to PR, to publicity and publicity stunts—to everything around the product. From inside the pit, that spending looks irrational: fortunes spent on things that are not the product. It is not irrational. It is the escape machinery. Everything around the product is what lifts the product out of the pit where the only question is the bid.

A service business runs the same escape with smaller props. The task is the pork belly: necessary, graded, replaceable. Everything around the task is the wrapper that makes it yours—the call that runs long because the conversation is good, the lunch, the memory of being known and being hosted by the man you hired. Two firms can sell the identical deliverable. The one wrapped in evenings and attention is not competing with the one wrapped in an invoice. They are in different markets, and only one of them hears the word "cheaper" every quarter.

The date, the memory, the hosting, the stunt—one machine, four costumes. It converts a bid into a relationship, and a relationship does not get re-bid every quarter.

This is just basic business 101, circa 2026.

The gate

The circuit is also a gate, and the gate is not incidental to the machine. It is part of what the machine is for.

At the top of law, finance, banking, and broking, the bonding happens in those rooms: the Korean bars, the strip bars, the long nights of taking whales out for decrepit things—the drugs, the drinking, the whole liturgy of men being boys together at altitude. That is where the relationships are made, and relationships are where the mandates come from. The work is assigned in the office. The trust that assigns it was built somewhere else, after midnight.

A woman in those fields stands in front of three doors. She can roll with the boys and be happy in those rooms. She can turn a blind eye to what the whales are doing. Or she can stay off the circuit—and off the circuit is off the map where the relationships are made. It takes a particular kind of woman to run that circuit, and the filter operates whether anyone designed it or not. Nobody has to post a sign. The room is the sign.

Japan runs the same machine in the open: the salaryman world and its hostess economy are one system, the desk by day and the bar by night, and a man who skips the night shift of business is understood to have skipped half the business. What Honolulu imported was not a vice. It was an org chart.

A friend of mine in venture capital described the modern version to me: the private motor yachts. Her, the men, and five escorts—or influencers, or VC bunnies; the vocabulary changes by harbor. She was the only woman on the boat who was there for the business. She reported it the way you report weather. The boat is a room, the room is a gate, and she had decided, long before the invitation arrived, which of the three doors was hers.

Evenings, not hours

A commodity service sells the task and takes the lowest bid as its verdict. A premium service sells the memory of having been taken care of, and once that memory is banked, the price stops being the argument.

The professional who understands that he is selling evenings, confidence, and company—not hours—never competes in the pool. He left it.
