Chris starts this one with football. His boys' school, Saint Louis School on the Kalaepōhaku hillside in Honolulu, is a Catholic boys' school that began dropping its lower grades in 1949, brought back grades 7 and 8 in 1980 and grade 6 in 1990, and announced in 2015 that it would go all the way down to kindergarten. Chris reads that as a farm system: develop football players from seventh grade, and now from kindergarten. The results are hard to argue with. Two Saint Louis quarterbacks five years apart, Marcus Mariota and Tua Tagovailoa, faced each other in the NFL last November, in what Hawaii News Now called the first game between two quarterbacks from the same Hawaii high school.

So his question for the last part of Source Waters is the same one a recruiter asks: where are the smart kids going to high school before they become AI founders, and where do they live? He bet on Northern Virginia and Silicon Valley. I checked.

Nobody counts founders by high school

The honest first answer: there's no public scoreboard. The best founder-origin study of the year, SignalFire's February report on more than 2,000 founders of about 800 American unicorns, tracks colleges and employers. Stanford leads with 7.49 percent of founders, then Harvard at 4.58 percent and MIT and Berkeley at 3.17 percent each, and 22.5 percent did their undergraduate degrees abroad. High school isn't in it, and I couldn't find a credible dataset anywhere that is. Anyone who hands you a ranked list of founder high schools made it up.

What does exist is better than a list. It's evidence of how the spring works.

The neighborhood is the strongest signal we have

Raj Chetty and his colleagues linked inventors' patent records to their parents' tax returns, in a paper the National Bureau of Economic Research published as "Who Becomes an Inventor in America?" Children whose parents were in the top 1 percent of income were ten times as likely to become inventors as children of parents below the median: 8.3 per 1,000 against 0.84, in the team's Brookings slides. Chetty's group calls the gifted kids who never got that start the lost Einsteins.

The finding that matters for Chris' question is exposure. Kids who grew up around inventors became inventors, and the effect was specific down to the technology: grow up near people patenting in one field and you're more likely to patent in that same field. It was specific to gender too. If girls were as exposed to women inventors as boys are to men, the slides estimate, the gender gap in invention would fall by half. Nobody grows up wanting to be something they've never seen.

That's the case for Chris' bet. Northern Virginia and Silicon Valley are full of engineers, contractors, and founders, so their kids grow up seeing the job. The data doesn't let me confirm those two places by name for AI founders, but it says neighborhoods like them are exactly where to look.

The magnet schools: TJ and its cousins

Chris' Northern Virginia instinct has one undeniable landmark. Thomas Jefferson High School for Science and Technology, the Fairfax County magnet in Alexandria, ranks sixth in the country on U.S. News' 2026–27 list, Northern Virginia Magazine reported in August, one spot behind Washington's School Without Walls, WTOP noted. Rankings measure test scores, not founders, but a school built to put the region's strongest math and science kids in one building is a spring by design.

The clearest recent example of a magnet school feeding AI comes from Louisiana. Scott Wu went to Baton Rouge Magnet High School and won three gold medals at the International Olympiad in Informatics, taking first place overall in 2014. His brother Neal won three IOI golds too. Both now work at Cognition, the AI coding company Scott co-founded in 2023. A public magnet school, two brothers, and a competition circuit most parents have never heard of: that's a source water.

The private school that bought a terminal

The most famous high school spring in tech is a story about parents. In 1968, the Mothers' Club at Lakeside School in Seattle bought the school a teletype terminal and time on a General Electric computer, the school's student paper recounts. Then a local company, Computer Center Corporation, let students use its PDP-10. Bill Gates and Paul Allen formed the Lakeside Programmers Group and, in 1972, Traf-O-Data, a business analyzing traffic-counter tapes. Microsoft came three years later.

That's the actionable lesson of every school on this list. A building full of bright kids is common. A building that hands bright kids the tool before anyone else has it is rare, and that's where the first domino falls.

Tech's nepo babies, with respect

Chris asked for this section and asked that it carry no disrespect. "It is what it is," he said. In entertainment and sports, nepo baby is an insult. In tech it mostly describes the exposure effect above, running through one house instead of one neighborhood.

  • Larry Page: his father, Carl Page, was a computer science professor at Michigan State, and his mother taught programming there. He first played with a home computer at six, in 1979, his biography records.
  • Jeff Bezos: his parents put $245,573 into Amazon in 1995, a stake that could have been worth about $30 billion by 2018, Fortune reported.
  • Bill Gates: his mother, Mary Maxwell Gates, served on United Way's national executive committee with IBM's chief executive, John Opel. By some accounts, her biography notes, citing the New York Times, Opel mentioned her son's small company to his executives in 1980, and IBM hired it weeks later.
  • Patrick and John Collison: brothers from Ireland, sons of a microbiologist and an electronic engineer, who started Stripe together in 2010, their biographies record.
  • Dario and Daniela Amodei: siblings who co-founded Anthropic, the son and daughter of a leather craftsman and a library project manager, her biography records. Not every family spring is money.

Look at what the families actually passed down. Sometimes it was cash, like the Bezos check. More often it was a computer at six, a professor at the dinner table, a mother who knew the IBM boss, or a brother who'd go all in with you. Siblings are the quiet pattern here: the Collisons, the Amodeis, and the Wus all built companies together.

Chris' own source waters

Chris is the first to say he's a nepo baby. His father was a photographer in Hawaii, and when Chris was 13 his dad made him business cards that said he was vice president of Pacific Rim Productions. "It was meaningless," he says, "it was just a title." Before that, at eight, he had a Nikkormat camera and unlimited film, the kind of start that lets a kid learn still and moving photography with no friction at all, because the whole family was in the business. The company was once called Abraham and Dunn. Dunn was his mother's maiden name.

What he didn't know then was that the Dunn in Abraham and Dunn was his grandfather, Pop-Pop, a silent partner quietly underwriting the family's creative life in Hawaii. Chris never saw the money. Pop-Pop didn't send lavish gifts or trips, and nobody bought him a Porsche at 16; Pop-Pop might have been behind the used, putty-colored VW Rabbit, but Chris will never know. "Family money is relative," he says. It buys you a life that isn't objectively hard, not the life of a prince.

The one gift he knew about was a nearly free ride through George Washington University, from 1988 to 1993 with a year off in the middle. He took it for granted at the time, and felt he'd earned it. His conclusion, decades later, is the best line in this series about how springs actually work: "a grand big gift is always less good with regards to the development of a child than being a little bit spoiled every day."

He ran his own agency the same way. He hired people he liked and had them hire their brothers, sisters, and cousins. Call it nepotism or call it trust; every founder on the list above did a version of it.

How to use this

  • Recruiters and investors: watch olympiad rosters, not just college rosters. The International Olympiad in Informatics and its national qualifiers find the Wus years before the Valley does.
  • Track sibling and family pairs. When one sibling starts a company, the other is the most likely co-founder, first hire, or next founder.
  • Parents: buy the terminal. Lakeside's Mothers' Club changed history with one teletype. Exposure is the mechanism, and it's cheap compared with tuition.
  • Map the neighborhoods. Chetty's data says inventors come from where inventors live. If you're scouting talent, start with the kids of the engineers already around you.
  • Fund the lost Einsteins. High-scoring kids without the exposure are the most undervalued asset in this whole series.
