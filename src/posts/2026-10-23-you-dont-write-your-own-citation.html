Google AI Overview Citation Cards With AI Summary Descriptions. Barry Schwartz at Search Engine Roundtable, reporting what SEO consultant Brodie Clark spotted: the citation cards that sit beside an AI Overview, the little cards with a site’s name and link, have begun carrying summaries that Google’s AI wrote itself.

Clark did the obvious check. He compared the words on the card with the words on the page being cited, and they were not there:

“The text doesn’t appear exactly like this on the page – it is being summarised based on the information that Google thinks is most relevant to the query.”

Schwartz reproduced it on some of his own test queries, though not all of them, so file this under expanding rather than finished. Even so, the direction is the story. The card’s text is no longer an excerpt. It is a generated description, written for the question that was asked.

This morning I argued that the citation is the new ranking, and this is the next turn of the same screw. For most searchers, the citation card is all of your page they will ever see: a favicon, a name, a link, and now a sentence or two of summary. Nobody clicks a citation out of idle curiosity. The card was never really a doorway; it is a display surface, and it is getting bigger. What has changed is who writes the display. Until now, the snippet was the one corner of the results page you half-wrote yourself, because Google lifted your title, your description, or your own sentences verbatim. The caption under your citation is now a paraphrase, produced per query, from whatever the machine judges most relevant to the searcher’s question. The same page can wear different captions for different people on different days, and not one of them will be yours.

Schwartz joked about the recursion, and the joke is also the analysis:

“We got the AI, generating more AI, for more AI…”

You cannot write the summary; that option has simply left the building. What you can still control is how summarizable your page is. A page that states its facts plainly—who you are, what you do, where you do it, what it costs—summarizes clean, because there is only one honest way to compress it. A page upholstered in fog and adjectives summarizes into whatever the machine guesses, and guesses drift toward the loudest framing available, which is often a competitor’s. Clear claims, clean structure, and entity facts stated once and stated identically everywhere are not new advice. The stakes are what moved. A sloppy page used to cost you a click. Now it costs you your own description of yourself, read out by a machine, inside the answer, at the exact moment a customer is deciding whether you exist.

That is why the audit I run reads the cards and not just the rankings. If the machine’s two-sentence summary of your business is wrong, stale, or borrowed from somebody else’s page, that error is your storefront sign for every searcher who never clicks, which is to say most of them. You do not get to approve the sign. You only get to make the record underneath it so clear, so specific, and so consistent that any honest summary of it lands right.
