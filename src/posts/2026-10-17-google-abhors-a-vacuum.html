Google abhors a vacuum. I have been saying that for more than twenty years. Search somebody's name and the results will consist of whatever the internet has managed to preserve: a biography, a business, an abandoned webpage, an old newspaper story, a furious former customer, a litigation record, or the adventures of an entirely different person with the same name. The results still appear if the subject of the search has never written a word.

Somewhere in my original explanation of this idea I thought of Audrey II, the great carnivorous plant from Little Shop of Horrors. Feed me, Seymour! Google is a hungry beast in my imagination. The actual search engine has more complicated rules than a singing houseplant. But a public record with nothing in it leaves a great deal for other people to supply.

I started working in online reputation management in 2002. I am proud of that work. I would take another serious ORM assignment tomorrow, particularly if a former colleague at one of the big public relations firms invited me onto a campaign. I've defended people accused of doing appalling things. Some were guilty of those things. My business partner and I sleep perfectly well at night.

I believe in America. I believe everybody deserves a good defense.

The right to a good defense

I've called myself a good lawyer without a JD. I like the discipline of an adversarial case. What exactly happened? What can be proved? Who has a motive to tell the story? What was left out? How old is the information? Does the name belong to the right person? What is the strongest truthful account that can be made from the evidence?

I have represented clients I liked, clients I disliked, people unfairly accused and people who had made a spectacular mess of their own lives. The work remains professional. A guilty person can be falsely accused of a different offense. A true story can omit twenty years of relevant context. A rumor can acquire the appearance of fact after enough repetitions. A person may have done real harm and still have a career, work, accomplishments, and a subsequent life worth documenting.

There are people in this country whose profession is to prosecute a case. There are people whose profession is to defend one. I've chosen a profession in which I assemble and defend a public account. I don't need to approve of a client to do the job well. The difficult clients are frequently the ones who make the principle worth having.

A reputation professional's tools are research, writing, publishing, technical search knowledge, public relations, and persistence. I've spent years using them. Some cases are about correcting the record. Some are about bringing a much larger record into view. Some require showing up while the whole world seems delighted to throw rocks at the subject. I am perfectly comfortable doing the work.

The bespoke reputation coachbuilder

Early online reputation management was gloriously peculiar. I began at New Media Strategies in 2002 and later worked at Reputation.com, among other stops described in my own account of the work. In the early days everything felt custom-built. You researched the person's history, read the forums, found the old mentions, conducted interviews, built websites, and made things legible to the engines of the time. I came to prefer that work over mechanical production.

My term for it is reputation coachbuilding. Before automobiles became completely standardized, a coachbuilder fitted an individually designed body to the chosen chassis. I admire the same mentality in modern restomods such as the creations of ICON 4x4. The finished machine has the character of an older design and the engineering to work in the present.

A venture capitalist with a forty-year business history, a family-owned manufacturer, a controversial artist, an obscure industrial supplier and a wealthy recluse each require a different public architecture. They have different material in the attic, different dangers, different reasons for being private and different things worth saying. That is the charm of the work. A bespoke history takes interviews, research, archival digging, web engineering, and the willingness to spend a great deal of time getting details right.

I still maintain long-standing clients, including relationships of around ten years and longer. I haven't spent every recent year running full-time crisis campaigns, and the technical environment has moved on since I started. I am particularly fond of the slower work of Online Perception Management, establishing and maintaining the record before the name becomes an emergency.

Everybody wants to pay for the fire engine

The economic problem is familiar. People hate paying to prevent a disaster that hasn't happened. Once the house is on fire, there is suddenly a budget, a meeting, an emergency contract, and a willingness to spend anything to make the flames stop. Preventive work asks the client to pay now for a catastrophe that might never arrive. Crisis management comes with its own persuasive visual effects.

During the January 2025 Palisades fire, reporters documented homes whose owners and architects had invested in fire-resistant designs years earlier. A house designed by Greg Chasen survived with fire-resistant walls, roofing and glazing, a carefully treated perimeter and limited opportunities for windblown embers to enter. Architect Michael Kovac and Karina Maher had built their own house with resilient siding, glazing, roofing and an exterior fire-retardant system. Their home survived as neighbouring houses burned. The Guardian, the Los Angeles Times, and ABC7 reported on those examples.

Preparation improved their chances, and luck remained a factor. I have no reliable count of every Palisades home built with fire prevention in mind. I only need the principle: preparation stings; catastrophe is worse. The work is easier when the flames are years away.

Politics has a version of this problem too. A politician gets a ribbon-cutting photograph for a new bridge. Nobody poses for the cameras while the drains are cleared, the expansion joints inspected and the rust treated. Years of maintenance may never become news. A bridge failure certainly will. Brookings has described the political incentives that favour visible construction over routine upkeep and the much higher price of delayed repairs. Many governments do maintain their infrastructure. The incentive remains a useful explanation for neglect.

Building an online reputation is closer to maintaining a bridge than arranging a rescue. A current biography, a carefully preserved archive, accurate links, well-maintained domains, clear ownership and ordinary publication over ten years do not make exciting news. When a crisis arrives, the value of that work becomes apparent in a hurry.

Google abhors an empty attic

I like telling people to bring down the attic. Almost every long-running business and most interesting adults have boxes full of material the internet has never seen. Old press clippings. Conference programmes. Photographs. Reports. Interviews. Awards. Failed enterprises. Archived newsletters. Product catalogues. Letters. Volunteer work. Speeches. Things from twenty, thirty, forty, even fifty years ago.

There is often enough genuine history for an excellent website. Scan it. Date it. Attribute it. Ask permission where permission is needed. Make the documents accessible. Publish a real CV and an actual biography. Explain the company and the work. Preserve evidence of what went wrong along the way. Write now and then about what is happening now.

I have followed this habit rather extravagantly on my own blogs. I share professional work, hobbies, enthusiasms, contradictions, mistakes, corrections, and now the exact prompts with which I get AI systems to write and build things. I have said that there's nothing to see under my kimono and I show everybody anyway. That is my personal appetite for publicity. Another person may keep an entirely different set of boundaries, especially where family safety, addresses, children, assets and daily routines are concerned.

A legitimate charitable foundation, if there is one, belongs in the record. So do the actual outcomes of its work. A blog, Substack, company history, or press page can belong there as well. These are places to preserve facts. A person who has spent twenty years quietly doing something worthwhile should not be introduced to strangers exclusively through the worst afternoon of their life.

Let the coral reef grow

In a 2015 Biznology essay, I compared a durable online presence to an artificial coral reef. First you sink an old fishing trawler. For a while it is simply an ugly lump of metal at the bottom of the sea. Then larvae settle and polyps grow. Sponges, molluscs, fish and a great many other creatures begin to make a living around it. Years later the wreck supports a small world.

The first website is the trawler. The biography, first documents and old photographs begin the structure. Independent references and links arrive because people find useful things. The archive grows. A name becomes associated with an identifiable body of work. The reef has time to develop organisms that actually belong there.

That is what I have watched with my long-standing perception management clients. A consistent site and real public history can hold a useful place in name searches over many years. Other articles link to the material. People come to recognise the official source. We repair old pages and extend the history instead of rebuilding the whole thing whenever someone has a bad Wednesday.

I used to give domain age considerable credit for this. What matters is the history that may accumulate with age: readable content, working references, real links, and continuity. A domain does not earn a favourable Google ranking merely for surviving another birthday. A decade can support the growth of something valuable. It cannot manufacture the value on its own.

The used Mercedes problem

An old domain name can have an old life. We have been using the web long enough for great domain names to come up at auction after twenty years of use by somebody else. Some are excellent acquisitions. Others come with an unpleasant inheritance.

It reminds me of buying an old Mercedes 600 or S63 at auction. Wonderful car. Beautiful upholstery. You have no idea whether its previous owner was a respectable accountant or a drug dealer. The registration transfer changes the owner. It doesn't remove whatever history the car acquired before you bought it.

Domains are much the same. A name might arrive with old inbound links, abandoned URLs, historical snapshots of dubious products, malware associations, misleading old search descriptions, or references to a business entirely different from yours. It can resemble getting a recycled telephone number and discovering that the previous owner had some extraordinarily persistent callers.

A good technical SEO audit investigates the prior use. I look at historical copies in the Internet Archive, old URLs, linking sites, Search Console evidence, security issues, and actual manual actions. Some legacy URLs deserve redirects to genuinely equivalent content. Others correctly return a 404 or 410. Google's spam policies distinguish abusive repurposing of expired domains from legitimate use of an old name. Random junk links, ordinary 404s and an unpleasant Analytics bounce rate do not all constitute the same kind of search problem.

I've used my own names for my personal domains over many years, including chrisabraham.com, chrisabraham.net and chrisabraham.org. I treat them as parts of a continuing personal history. A buyer picking up a domain at auction ought to examine its past before assuming the attractive name comes with a clean slate.

Mea Maxima Culpa

I wrote Mea maxima culpa marketing in 2013 after humiliating myself with an email merge. The placeholder {first name} appeared in the emails where actual names should have been. A very impressive display of my technological sophistication. I apologised, and some recipients were gracious. Others were amused. The reaction belonged to them.

The larger lesson applies to more serious cases. Somebody who has caused harm needs to own what happened, listen, and change their conduct. An apology delivered for the purpose of ending the complaints will rapidly show its seams. Genuine acknowledgment has consequences. Sometimes they are expensive. Sometimes forgiveness never arrives.

The word marketing in my old formulation is intentionally provocative. A reputation strategist can help a person communicate what they have done and what they intend to do. The actual admission and the changed conduct belong to the person who caused the problem. If there has been restitution, repair, or a genuine second act, those events become part of the public record over time.

Metanoia

I have occasionally had a difficult conversation with clients who keep causing their own reputational crises. I can hold the line for a while. I can keep the castle's gates defended, repair the walls, manage the public record and put forward the best account available. If the client continues making victims, breaking promises or committing the same ugly acts, the job becomes more expensive. There is always another emergency, another new allegation, another fresh fact, another wave of legitimate public anger. The cheques get larger and the walls get thinner.

At some point you have to change your life.

The word I use is metanoia. Turn toward God. Repent. Sin no more. I mean a real change of heart and behaviour, a second act lived differently from the first. A person can begin that change without a public announcement. If they sustain it, the record may eventually show it. If they keep doing the same things, the expensive reputation machinery will spend the rest of its life chasing their next disaster.

The people with the pitchforks may have excellent reasons to be angry. Sometimes the village has endured years of the conduct that made the castle rich. The second act has to exist in their world too. The work of becoming a better neighbour, employer, business owner or citizen is what eventually gives the defense something durable to stand on.

The search rules have changed since 2002

I am an old hand at this, and old hands can collect old superstitions. We used to talk constantly about Google sandboxes, punishments, penalties, and getting blackballed for behaving too aggressively. In 2026 it pays to use more precise language. Check for an actual manual action in Search Console. Check what can be crawled and indexed. Understand the content being published and the links pointing at it. A lost ranking is evidence that something changed; it is not proof of a secret punishment.

A well-maintained public identity has a fairly ordinary technical foundation. Maintain a fast, accessible website with stable URLs and accurate titles. Publish original work and verify the old material in the archives. Keep official profiles consistent. Use helpful profile structured data that reflects facts visible to readers. Fix broken navigation. Redirect URLs when the destination actually corresponds to the previous page. Let independent institutions and writers link because the information is worth linking to. Revisit the site at sensible intervals, particularly when real events create something new to document.

The same work now has an AI search dimension. People search names using ChatGPT, Google, Perplexity, Claude and other tools. These systems sometimes mix up namesakes, preserve outdated descriptions or state their interpretations with remarkable confidence. It is worth recording what they say, tracing their source material where possible, and correcting underlying inaccuracies. No website owner has a right to dictate every answer they give.

Google's people-first content guidance and spam rules should be the starting point for contemporary work. Build real content, preserve clear identity, earn real citations, and avoid synthetic networks whose entire reason for existing is to manipulate the results. The old coral reef benefits from being alive. Plastic fish do not improve it.

Keep the bridge standing

Once the reputation crisis arrives, every minute becomes urgent, every question becomes political, and every proposed action carries the additional cost of doing it under fire. We all understand why emergency work commands a premium. The opportunity I particularly enjoy is the one available before the emergency: maintaining the bridge while people are still driving over it, hardening the house before the sparks arrive, and letting the coral reef establish itself over years.

We spend fortunes rescuing public identities after they have collapsed. An accurate biography, a functioning website, a properly catalogued history, and a little regular attention will never earn anyone a ribbon-cutting ceremony. They will just be there, doing their work, when someone comes looking.

And when the worst happens, a good defense is still a good defense. I believe everybody deserves one.
