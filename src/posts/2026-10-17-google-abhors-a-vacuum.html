I've been saying for years that Google abhors a vacuum. If somebody searches your name and you have provided nothing substantial about yourself, the results will still be made of something. It might be your professional history. It might be an old conference listing, a lawsuit, an angry forum post, somebody else's opinion, or the activities of a completely different person unlucky enough to share your name.

You could be an extraordinarily interesting human being with a forty-year career, a collection of wonderful stories, a history of charitable work, a garage full of projects, three businesses, two memorable failures, and a perfectly decent explanation for something embarrassing you did in 2007. Google cannot show a visitor the contents of your attic if nobody has ever opened the boxes.

So I have a service I call Perception Management. Its purpose is to make a person's genuine record more discoverable before some accident, dispute, political campaign, allegation, or particularly energetic enemy makes their name newsworthy. The operative word is genuine. I am talking about telling your own story, in your own voice, from your own record. If you leave yourself out of your own history, the internet will get on perfectly well without your testimony.

I am an almost extravagantly public example of this. I publish my opinions, my work, my peculiar enthusiasms, my corrections, my AI prompts, and even the little arguments I have with the AI assistants who write for me. I have a complete perception management strategy, and I am not interested in lying to make it work. I told ChatGPT, while it was editing this very piece, that I might be “the most honest piece of shit you've ever met.” I followed it with a line I rather like: “there's nothing to see under my kimono but I show everybody.” Some things in life should remain private, by choice or for safety. The things I choose to publish should be true, even when they don't flatter me.

The image that came to me while dictating this idea was the giant hungry plant from Little Shop of Horrors. I couldn't remember its name. Google AI Mode reminded me: Audrey II. The creature's demand is magnificent and appalling in equal measure.

Feed me, Seymour!

That is how I sometimes imagine the modern information system. It will take whatever it can find about you, good or bad, profound or trivial, and put something on the menu when somebody comes looking. A search engine is not literally hungry, of course. It doesn't demand that you feed it daily with uplifting biography. But the metaphor has helped me explain the practical problem to clients for years.

The reputation coachbuilder

I began doing online reputation management in 2002, at New Media Strategies. That part of my professional chronology is recorded in my own career history, though a biography written by me is evidence of what I have said about my career rather than independent verification of every campaign. Eventually I worked at Reputation.com on special projects, including high-profile reputation and executive privacy work, as my older biography explains.

I was happiest with the hand-built version of the job. Every client brought a different career, public record, temperament, set of real mistakes and degree of willingness to be visible. My word for that is reputation coachbuilding: one person, one fitted body, one peculiar machine. The ICON 4x4 analogy I use when talking about websites works here too. A restoration that preserves individuality and gives the underlying engineering some serious attention has more character than a car stamped out by the thousand.

I watched reputation services become more mechanised and developed a real suspicion of clone websites and synthetic authority. In dictating this article I alleged that Reputation.com built a vast coordinated network and suffered a Google punishment. I do not have evidence sufficient to establish that particular allegation, so it does not become a historical fact because I said it in a voice memo or because Google AI Mode produced an agreeable paragraph about it. If I want to investigate a company, I owe its people the same documentary discipline I demand for my clients.

Another admission: I have not been doing full-blown hands-on online reputation management every working day for years. I still maintain legacy clients, but I am rusty about some of the current machinery. My long experience is real; my expertise in the latest unpublished ranking changes is not magically guaranteed by having started early. That is why this essay includes a contemporary technical fact-check instead of selling my 2002 playbook as if nothing has changed.

The rich have started closing the curtains

I've noticed a posture among some wealthy and high-profile people that interests me. They want to retreat. They want less information online, less public exposure, fewer photographs, fewer declarations about where they live, what they own, or what they believe. Some are worried about activism, crime, doxxing, personal safety, reputational attacks, or the contemporary appetite for watching somebody powerful fall down a flight of metaphorical stairs.

I understand the impulse. If your name is attached to serious money, a family business, an investment firm, or an expensive collection of things you would rather not have stolen, broadcasting your routine and physical whereabouts is idiotic. Discretion is sometimes essential. This is not a claim that all wealthy people are retreating, or that a peasant revolution is just around the corner. It is a description of a fear I hear expressed in a particular kind of conversation.

But private living and a missing public identity are different problems. You can keep your family, address, schedules, assets, security arrangements, children's names, and favourite hiding places to yourself while maintaining an accurate record of your career, companies, scholarship, art, public service, philanthropy, and work.

In fact, a good Perception Management project should establish what must never be made public before deciding what might usefully be published. Privacy is a boundary, not a publishing failure.

A vacuum is an invitation to other narrators

Imagine two people with the same name. One has an established biography, a company website with a credible history, interviews in recognisable publications, presentations archived at professional associations, an active and plainly attributable weblog, photographs that show their work, and a record of real projects going back decades. The other has a LinkedIn stub and a photograph from a fundraising dinner eight years ago.

Now imagine that somebody becomes curious about either person. The second individual may find that the searchable story is composed almost entirely of whatever unrelated websites happen to mention the name. Nobody needs to be conspiring. The available evidence simply does not tell a very complete story.

The crucial word here is available. An absence of first-party sources can make external accounts unusually prominent, but it does not guarantee that a hostile page will rank, nor does it mean that every bad result is false. A thoroughly researched negative story may deserve attention and may continue to appear even after years of good publishing. An honest public record cannot make true criticism disappear.

I would rather a client have fifty years of truthful context available than have strangers discover that person for the first time through the worst afternoon of their life.

Take the attic down, carefully

One of my favourite images for this business is the attic. Many people and companies have a remarkable history that is almost invisible online. The evidence lives in filing cabinets, VHS tapes, photographs, press clippings, programmes, meeting minutes, awards, speeches, old annual reports, trade magazines, letters, or dusty boxes of product brochures. Some of it is rather wonderful. Some of it may be dull. Dullness is part of the documentary record. It tells future readers that the subject existed before the latest controversy.

That material can be digitised, dated, attributed, organised and published with permission. A family-office principal might have a professional chronology and a carefully chosen public biography. A manufacturing company might have thirty years of innovations and partnerships. A founder might have a readable account of the enterprises that failed as well as the ones that succeeded. A nonprofit might publish its actual work and its measurable results. The practical publishing tools can be modest: a proper website, a journal or Substack, a newsroom page, an archive, an occasional genuinely newsworthy press announcement, and profiles maintained wherever the subject legitimately appears.

Most importantly, those assets should be interesting to actual human beings. A directory full of thin press releases, identical company bios and generic articles about somebody's excellence is a tedious memorial to the consultant who produced it.

In a useful archive, the life and work are the substance. Search visibility is a consequence one hopes follows.

Google's AI liked my idea a little too much

I fed this argument to Google's AI Mode and asked whether the premise was outdated. It identified Audrey II immediately, then delivered a highly enthusiastic endorsement. It called my idea more accurate than ever and described the service as proactive reputation management or “suppression engineering.” It recommended filling the search landscape with positive assets, talked about philanthropy as a reputational flak jacket, and even suggested that featuring other people with the same name might dilute the search engine's confidence about which person the visitor intended to find.

Some of that was useful market-language analysis. Some of it was dangerous nonsense. The machine was so eager to applaud my thesis that it failed to distinguish publishing an honest record from manipulating identity and relevance.

Google's own people-first content guidance explicitly cautions against creating lots of material merely to attract search traffic. Its spam policies describe scaled content abuse, including large quantities of unoriginal pages generated primarily to manipulate search rankings. Its ranking-systems documentation says freshness is useful for queries where fresh information is actually expected. Posting a new biography every Thursday does not make a thirty-year-old business record newly relevant or magically outrank serious reporting.

And the .org on the end of a charity's domain is a domain extension, not a certificate of sainthood or an automatic ranking advantage. Google treats .org as a generic top-level domain, just as it does .com.

The part about other people with the same name particularly bothered me. If an innocent John Smith is appearing in results that should distinguish him from another John Smith, the right response is to help search systems understand the people accurately. Deliberately cultivating the confusion is unfair to both of them. A better solution is specific identity: the correct employer, occupation, geographic context where appropriate, published work, official site, consistent byline and verified biographical detail.

I want a well-documented identity. I have no interest in manufacturing uncertainty about somebody else's.

Likewise, I would never recommend creating a foundation as theatrical scenery. A genuine foundation doing genuine work deserves a proper public record. A newly invented charity serving principally as reputation camouflage is a bad look even before anybody starts investigating how it spends its money.

Everybody deserves a good defense, even the guilty

ChatGPT initially told this story as though my clients were all innocents suffering defamatory attacks. I objected, rather energetically. I've defended people whose conduct I found despicable. Many were guilty of the things being said about them. If you imagine reputation work as a service exclusively for victims of mistaken identity, you have mistaken it for a particularly sunny children's programme.

I have said I am a good lawyer without a JD. That is a metaphor for an adversarial disposition, not a claim that I am entitled to practise law. I think even the biggest piece of shit deserves a competent defense. A defense can challenge an inaccurate claim, insist on proportionality, add omitted facts, and give someone a fair opportunity to answer. It cannot declare a true accusation to be slander because a client dislikes seeing it on Google.

Everybody deserves a good defense. A defense is not an endorsement, and buying time is not the same as buying innocence.

I imagine holding a castle while an angry crowd gathers outside. I can keep the hearth going, hold the front, and make the fortifications last a bit longer. Sometimes the crowd is horribly misinformed. Sometimes the people holding the torches have perfectly legitimate grievances. I cannot tell which from the height of the castle walls. You have to go down and listen.

Chris, I accept that guilty people deserve a defense. I am less fond of treating everyone angry with a rich client as an irrational mob. The second act is metanoia

There is a limit to my ability to defend somebody who keeps doing the same horrible things. I can hold the line for a while, but if you continue creating new victims and new controversies, you are going to write me bigger and bigger cheques. I will need more resources, more work, and more time. The expense may grow dramatically, and eventually it won't matter how much you pay me. The wall cannot hold against an endless supply of fresh evidence.

My Christian word for the second act is metanoia, a change of mind, heart, and direction: turn toward God, repent, and sin no more. A reader needn't share my faith to understand the practical requirement. Stop the harmful conduct. Admit the wrong without theatrics. Accept consequences. Make restitution where you can. Reform the systems that enabled it. Let the change continue for years, when nobody is monitoring your social-media feeds.

I have told clients that the people who once wanted to run them through with pitchforks need a reason to see them differently. They may never love you. They do not owe you forgiveness, favourable search results, or a testimonial for the new you. But I cannot make a credible public record of meaningful reform unless there is meaningful reform to record. The money spent defending your reputation buys an opportunity to change your life. You have to perform that part yourself.

What Perception Management actually sells

After stripping away the machinery's flatteries, I still like the name. The service is about getting the documentary basics right while the client has the time, patience and freedom to choose what belongs in public. I see several kinds of work.

First, the identity audit. Search the person's full name, common shortened names, company names, former organisations and professionally relevant combinations. Do it in conventional Search and in the major AI answering systems. Record what is genuinely about the subject, what is uncertain, what is mistaken identity, what is outdated and what is demonstrably false. Do not manufacture a scandal by treating ordinary critical coverage as an attack.

Second, the evidence inventory. Take stock of the life or company's real documents: CVs, bios, business filings, public interviews, projects, published work, photographs, association memberships, exhibitions, awards and historic coverage. Verify dates. Get permission to use the material. Preserve source context rather than quietly inventing a more convenient past.

Third, build the permanent public record. Put the verified story on a durable website with a biography, company history, selected timeline, projects and current information. Cross-link related work. Make pages accessible and readable by humans and machines. Use accurate titles, canonical URLs, appropriate Person, Organization or profile structured data, and consistent identifiers where they describe facts visible on the page. Google's profile-page guidance can help with the technical details, but schema is an explanatory label, not a warranty that Google will believe your claims.

Fourth, let the story continue. Publish actual news as it occurs. Preserve speeches, interviews, relevant personal reflections and legitimate charitable work. Update stale biography information. Correct errors openly. I especially like weblogs here because a weblog accumulates the texture of a life. Every entry need not be a monument to its author's greatness. Somebody who can talk naturally about failures, books, ideas and work becomes much easier to distinguish from the cardboard cut-out on a corporate About page.

Fifth, measure the outcomes without pretending to control them. Record branded search queries, which results are correctly attributed, whether official profiles appear, what factual misunderstandings persist and how AI systems describe the person over time. Search Console has a branded-query filter for eligible properties, which can help with the site's own search performance. It cannot report every mention of a person on somebody else's website. Independent manual review still matters.

This is slow, rather human work. The best projects might last years. A historical archive should be a real archive, with dates and provenance, rather than an instant pile of search bait.

The ten-year coral reef

Now for the part of this work that an instant reputation fix never understands. I have perception-management relationships that have continued for ten years or more. My own experience with those clients is that a stable, regularly tended body of material can hold a useful position in branded searches for a very long time. The sites have histories. Their structure remains intelligible. Articles and profiles point to one another where they legitimately belong. Other people have linked to and referenced their work. These are accumulated assets, not an emergency stack of cardboard testimonials.

I first published a better explanation in a Biznology article from October 6, 2015. I compared an online presence to the creation of an artificial reef. You sink a fishing trawler and all you have at first is a lump of old steel on the seafloor. Then larvae settle. Polyps begin to grow. Sponges arrive, then molluscs, crustaceans and fish. Given the right conditions and enough time, the place may support a whole community of life. I originally wrote it about brands abandoning their social profiles and turning their public presence into ghost towns. The idea fits a carefully maintained reputation as well.

A website is the trawler. The real biography and archive are the first residents. Links, interviews, independent references, recurring readers and other people's genuine interactions arrive over time. That ecology is not something a consultant can manufacture overnight with ten thousand pretend organisms. A convincing reef also needs an environment in which its inhabitants actually belong.

That matters when considering my old affection for aged domains. Over years, a domain may gather material, citations, incoming links, audience familiarity and a history of useful maintenance. Domain age by itself is not a known Google ranking bonus. Google's John Mueller has repeatedly dismissed the notion that simply owning an older domain makes it rank better; see the report on his public statements. The reef's value lies in what has grown there, not the registration anniversary.

ChatGPT interrupts: a reef isn't a monopoly

This is one of Chris' more useful metaphors, and the original 2015 column really does describe the boat and successive marine organisms. But he sometimes follows a lovely ecological explanation with a distinctly less lovely ambition to occupy all the available search results. Healthy reefs support many independent species. Search results should too. A well-maintained official site deserves to be discoverable; legitimate criticism and independent reporting deserve to coexist with it. A decade of successful client maintenance is valuable practice experience, not proof that Google's algorithms reward domain birthdays or that negative information can be made permanently irrelevant.

Mea Maxima Culpa, and the trouble with apologies as tactics

I was using the expression Mea Maxima Culpa Marketing long before Google AI Mode wrapped it in its rather heroic explanatory prose. My February 19, 2013 Biznology essay started with an embarrassing email merge failure. Personal messages had gone out with the literal placeholder {first name} instead of the recipient's name. I was mortified. Some friends teased me. Others graciously forgave me. I believed I had no right to tell the recipients that my blunder was unimportant. The apology belonged to me; the assessment of the harm belonged to them.

That is why I dislike the version of the story in which I tell a wealthy villain to say sorry, suddenly everyone loves him, and Google obediently buries the court records under bouquets of philanthropic good works. My original article warned that contrition becomes worse than useless when it is insincere or performed regularly as a marketing technique. My own argument, taken seriously, prevents me from turning it into a factory process.

I can help you explain what happened. I cannot feel sorry on your behalf. And if you do not actually change, the apology will become evidence against you.

ChatGPT interrupts: repentance is not a conversion funnel

A 2026 maintenance manual without the old Google sandbox mythology

I asked to be fact-checked because I have been away from the front lines of full-time ORM for some time. Here is how I would approach the underlying problem using documented practices rather than the old lore about being “sandboxed,” blackballed, or hurled into some secret search-engine oubliette. Google documents spam policies and manual actions. The absence of a ranking is not itself proof of a penalty.

  1. Audit the real identity and the safety boundary. Search names in context; separate the client from namesakes; check old business records, news coverage, identifiable errors and genuine disputes. Agree what stays private. No family itineraries, security arrangements, residence details or personal asset inventories need to be sacrificed to the content gods.
  2. Build a durable first-party home. Publish an accessible, fast website with a sourced biography, accurate professional chronology, real project descriptions, current affiliations and a contact path. Keep the URLs stable. Google's SEO Starter Guide recommends making material understandable and useful, rather than creating an imitation media empire.
  3. Digitise evidence with provenance. Put genuine speeches, awards, reports, historical articles, talks and photographs into a properly described archive with dates, permissions and source context. A false memory polished into an SEO biography is still a false memory.
  4. Make identity unambiguous. Use consistent names, professional affiliations, bylines and official profiles. Add appropriate structured data that reflects facts visible on the page. Do not exploit similarly named people to confuse the results.
  5. Earn references. Contribute work that is worth citing. Let journalists, peers, researchers, customers and institutions link where they genuinely choose to. Avoid bought links for rankings, disguised sponsorship, invented testimonials, fake charity work and supposedly independent sites controlled by the same publicity machine.
  6. Let the publishing cadence reflect real life. New project? Document it. Actual report? Publish it. Genuine philanthropic work? Explain what happened and what it accomplished. Google's people-first content guidance is a warning against churning out repetitive material simply to manipulate rankings. No mechanical weekly gratitude essay required.
  7. Maintain the engineering. Check mobile usability, keyboard access, visible headings, image descriptions, broken links, canonical URLs, sitemap, indexability, speed and Search Console evidence. Do not turn structured data into a fictional awards cabinet or claim that submitting a sitemap guarantees a ranking.
  8. Review periodically and record corrections. Quarterly may suit a stable professional identity; a crisis needs its own timetable. Keep accurate snapshots of the public record and independent references. Respond to misinformation using legitimate correction or removal channels when applicable, and publish accountable updates when you or your client were wrong.

Google's current spam rules explain why a factory of cloned sites, thin AI articles, coordinated ranking links or doorway pages can damage search visibility. Its policies describe scaled content abuse and link spam, not a special penalty for maintaining a modest website. Slow accumulation is compatible with good search practice when the accumulation consists of useful, original, accurate material.

And in the age of AI answers, build a record that machines can discover and humans can challenge. Compare how Google, ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity and other services describe the individual, investigate the source material and correct mistakes where possible. There is no legitimate technique that can force an AI system to deliver your client's preferred story every time.

The most important annual maintenance task is still the one no crawler can perform: make sure the person has stopped doing the thing that keeps getting them into trouble.

Buying somebody else's domain is buying somebody else's past

Here is another bit of the internet that has become old enough to surprise people. There is a market in aged companies, shelf corporations and old LLCs whose dates and paperwork somebody finds attractive. I'm interested in the similar intuition behind buying an old domain name at auction. You look at a beautiful address and imagine that it arrives empty, or perhaps that its years automatically make it respectable.

But the internet now has secondhand names. Buying a domain can be like being issued a recycled telephone number. For months strangers may still call asking for its former owner. Or imagine buying a superb Mercedes S63 or a grand old 600 at auction. It might be mechanically magnificent. It might also have spent years belonging to a drug dealer. The title deed in your hand does not erase the photographs, police reports, phone numbers and acquaintances associated with the previous owner. An unfamiliar history is a reason to inspect, not an accusation about any particular used vehicle.

When I began, a new web address felt virgin and unencumbered. I have long thought of chrisabraham.com, chrisabraham.net and chrisabraham.org as my own identities, rather than other people's abandoned properties I had reclaimed. That is my recollection of my registration history; the statement does not independently prove an unbroken chain of ownership or use for every year. A registration date or today's RDAP record cannot do that on its own.

What can come with a previously used domain? Old inbound links, spammy or otherwise. Old indexed URLs and referral traffic. Search snippets and screenshots showing an unrelated business. Old social profiles, bookmarks and brand associations. Bad email-sender reputation, perhaps. Occasionally a Search Console manual action or other genuine search-quality problem. And plenty of simple mistakes, including links to pages you no longer publish.

These things should not all be put in the same bucket. Normal 404 pages are not a site-wide ranking penalty. Google's August 2024 SEO office hours explicitly says they are a normal part of a healthy website. Redirect old URLs when there is a real equivalent page, not indiscriminately to the homepage. A high Analytics bounce rate is not by itself evidence of a Google search punishment. Google's published ranking and page-experience guidance does not establish ordinary Google Analytics bounce rate as a direct penalty switch. Bad links need investigation, but Google's own advice is that its systems ignore many random spammy links. Use the disavow process cautiously, especially where there is a substantial manipulative link problem, rather than disavowing everything you dislike.

Google does acknowledge the previous-owner problem. Its crawling FAQ tells buyers of formerly spammy domains to verify ownership in Search Console and inspect the Manual Actions report. Its site migration guidance tells owners to clean up problems inherited with a recently purchased domain. And its 2024 policy on expired-domain abuse targets the deliberate reuse of expired names for low-value ranking manipulation. Google also explicitly says that putting a genuinely useful new site on an old domain is acceptable. An inheritance is worth investigating; it is not an automatic life sentence.

The secondhand domain inspection

Before paying a substantial price for an auctioned domain, I would look at historic snapshots in the Internet Archive to understand what used to live there; search for old titles and brand names; inspect available historical DNS and registration records while recognising their gaps; sample historical backlinks and anchor text; examine old indexed URLs and visible security or malware reports; and consider whether the domain name is entangled with trademarks. If I acquired it, I would verify Search Console, check Manual Actions and Security Issues, map genuinely useful old URLs, and let obsolete ones return proper 404 or 410 responses. I would also make sure all DNS and email records belong to the new owner before using the domain.

None of these checks can guarantee a clean slate. And the lesson applies to SEO consulting quite broadly: sometimes you're repairing a client's website, and sometimes you're investigating the ghosts of the website somebody owned before the client bought it. If you don't know the difference, your carefully planned new content strategy can spend months arguing with history.

Where the oxygen analogy breaks down

In my original voice-to-text ramble I suggested that Perception Management slowly allows air, perhaps even oxygen, back into the reputation vacuum. I still like that analogy. A reputation without supporting public context is fragile. More verified context can make interpretation less dependent on the nearest dramatic search result.

But oxygen feeds fires too. There are people for whom publishing much more personal material creates real security risks. There are businesses whose old controversies require legal work, apology, restitution or transparent correction rather than another dozen optimistic posts. There are allegations that deserve to be heard. There is a line between giving the public truthful context and burying reporting that would help them make an informed decision.

When something online is false, defamatory, impersonating somebody, exposing private information or violating platform rules, investigate the proper correction, removal or legal routes. Do not assume the problem can be solved by buying enough cheerful stories. Google itself documents processes for removing information you control and points people toward separate procedures for content they don't control. None of that is the same as scrubbing legitimate public history.

A perception strategy based on fiction is a liability with a delayed fuse. If the truth eventually comes out, the camouflage becomes part of the story. I want my clients to have the confidence to say, “Here is my account of my life and my work. Here are the documents. Here's what went well, and here's what didn't.” The point of owning your narrative is that the voice is yours and the evidence is available, not that your version becomes immune from contradiction.

The part the wealthy sometimes miss

A person of means may imagine that safety requires total disappearance. Sometimes the right answer really is to disappear from a particular public channel. I do not presume to tell somebody else's security adviser what personal facts to disclose. Yet there is a middle ground between throwing the curtains open and keeping no public record of a long, complicated professional life.

They can be candid about the work and private about the home. They can disclose a philanthropic programme without turning the beneficiaries into marketing props. They can publish a career chronology without posting their itinerary. They can have a thoughtful website, a real author biography, a company history and a competent press contact without publishing anything that would help an intruder find their family.

And perhaps they can let themselves be people. The old internet was full of people keeping journals, collecting links and writing about things they cared about. A sober website with a little actual humanity may turn out to be a better introduction than a highly engineered press page entirely devoid of character.

Feed the record, not the monster

My original premise survives the fact-checking. If you have a story worth knowing, it is sensible to make the evidence of that story findable. It is easier to assemble forty years of photographs, talks, useful work and proper records in ordinary times than to attempt it during a public-relations crisis. An accurate public biography gives other writers and researchers a place to begin. People with similar names are less easily confused. Clients, colleagues and journalists are better equipped to distinguish claims from facts.

There is no magic moat. You cannot own the first page of Google. You cannot instruct an AI to give everyone a flattering answer. You cannot bury a real scandal beneath a press release about an invented foundation and expect it to stay there.

What you can do is take responsibility for preserving and sharing your own history within the boundaries of your privacy. Build a public record with enough independent evidence to survive somebody taking a close look at it. Invite scrutiny of the things you do choose to say. Leave a little room for ordinary humanity.

And if Audrey II comes calling, at least there will be something on the shelf worth feeding it.
