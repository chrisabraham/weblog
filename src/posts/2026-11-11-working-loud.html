Working loud is a discipline of communication, and it has nothing to do with bragging. The work does not speak for itself; it never has. A manager, a client, an executive can only trust, reward, and defend the work they can see, and work done in silence reads, from the outside, exactly like work not done. I spent the first decades of my career with my head down, on the theory that high performance was the baseline and the record would accumulate on its own. The record does not accumulate. It evaporates. What survives is what somebody heard about, at whatever volume somebody chose to use. Working loud is setting your own volume on purpose: report before you are asked, check whether people are overwhelmed and offer to carry some of it, mark the milestones out loud, remember the birthdays and the children's names, back every claim with receipts other people can inspect, and become, for your manager and your colleagues and your company, a flying buttress, a visible support standing outside the wall, holding the structure up where everyone can see it hold. For an introvert raised to wait his turn and never volunteer, it also means unlearning the quiet rules that once passed for discipline.

Everybody has a different volume knob

Volume is a calibration problem before it is a character problem. Everybody has a different volume knob and a different tolerance. One manager wants a line a day. Another wants a summary on Friday and silence in between. One client feels accompanied by a steady drip of small updates; another feels pestered by anything short of a finished deliverable. The over-communicators ping their superiors constantly to prove they exist, and the under-communicators, my tribe, hunker down and disappear into the work, then surface weeks later wondering why the trust went cool. Both errors come from the same mistake: setting the volume for your own comfort instead of the other person's hearing.

It's your job as an employee to adapt to your manager and your job; it's not the job of your manager or your company to adapt to you. It does happen, begrudgingly, to star employees. But, be honest with yourself, are you the star of the company? Are you truly the Diva, the Divo, the Divum, around whom the company revolves; or, are you just a lucky AF git who was able to land a good enough job for which you should feel gratitude and should behave accordingly?

Silence, in particular, never reads as humility from the other side of the wall. It reads as absence. Absence reads as indifference, and indifference gets replaced, by a vendor who checks in, a colleague who narrates, an employee whose name keeps arriving attached to solved problems. I have been the quiet one in that comparison and lost work I was doing well to people doing it louder. The work was equal. The evidence was not.

Know your baseline

There ought to be a rule book printed by temperament, because loudness is measured against a baseline and baselines differ wildly. Whatever the personality inventories call it, INTP, ENTP, or the rest of the alphabet, the rule for the introspective runs like this: when you feel as though you are bragging, you have usually just reached normal loudness. Your internal gauge reads your own voice about twenty decibels hotter than the room hears it. The opposite tribe needs the opposite page of the book. If you take your calls on speakerphone in public, carry a Bluetooth speaker through other people's afternoon, or film yourself dancing in traffic and call the obstruction content, your self-promotion does not need encouragement. It needs a governor. And over everything sits the general vibe of who you are working for. A room, a company, a client each has a native volume, and the freelancer's job is to tune to it.

That is why I treat every client engagement the way you treat the early life of a relationship: first date, second date, date night. You mirror the person you are with. Baudrillard wrote about seduction as exactly this art, drawing the other out by reflecting them, meeting their tempo and their weather instead of performing your own at them. Mirroring, done right, is attention made visible. Done empty, it is ELIZA, the old chatbot trick of handing a person's words back to them in a different order and calling it conversation. People can tell. I can tell when my own AI agent does it to me, reflects my phrases back because it does not know what is going on, and the pedantic, theatrical, condescending flavor of the trick is visible from space. Mirror the client to tune the signal. Then give the signal something real to carry: the status, the help, the receipts.

Half the advice economy right now is remediation, earnest instruction in socializing for generations who were handed phones instead of practice, and a good share of the rest is tooling for people on the spectrum who are learning to pass. The spectrum has a word for performing normal: masking. Acting normie is exhausting, and masking is the name of the exhaustion. I know I carry three diagnoses already. Whether there is a fourth, I have never had settled, and it hardly matters for this point: when I was performing the social program consciously, move by move, it wore me out. Over the years I normalized most of it, until the performance stopped feeling like one. That, I think, is the honest reading of the world's worst advice. When people tell you to just be yourself, they say it because they like who you are when you are yourself. The work is becoming a self whose natural volume other people can hear, and like.

The name got taken by fear

The phrase has a history, and the history splits in two. In 2010 Bryce Williams at Eli Lilly coined "working out loud" and gave it a formula: observable work plus narrating your work. John Stepper built it into a method with five elements: purposeful discovery, relationships, generosity, visible work, and a growth mindset. Read that list again. Generosity sits in the middle of it. The original practice was a way of working in the open so other people could learn from your process, build on it, and trust you across the silos of a big company.

The 2026 version kept the name and lost the generosity. "Loud working" now trends as a survival tactic: the green status dot tended like a campfire, the after-hours email sent as proof of life, the minor win announced in the public channel before the ink dries. The surveys behind the trend describe a workforce running on fear of invisibility. MyPerfectResume surveyed 1,000 U.S. workers and found that 70 percent believe highly visible employees are rewarded more than workers who quietly produce strong results, 82 percent have worried that appearing too idle could hurt their security or growth, and 56 percent admit they have used at least one tactic to appear busy. A Tide survey in the U.K. put a meter on it: the average worker spends 2.8 hours a week making their work visible, about 17 working days a year, and 77 percent of the loud workers said the noise had paid them back in clients, raises, or promotions. It works, and a quarter of their colleagues have considered quitting over the racket. Both of those things are true at once, which is how you know the trend is a symptom.

That is why it is suddenly everywhere. Business Insider reported employees treating visibility as job armor, one of them describing a workplace where it felt as though the Hunger Games had started. HR Brew named the engine underneath it: FOBO, fear of becoming obsolete, in a season of layoffs and AI transformation. Forbes converted the anxiety into career advice within the week, and the definition pages now keep a glossary entry for it, the way dictionaries adopt a slang word once the talk shows have worn it smooth. The talk shows have it, too. Fox Business gave it a panel segment under the banner "Self-Promotion Eating Into Workplace Productivity," and last night Gutfeld! made it a featured segment, a table of Boomers and Gen Xers and maybe one millennial rolling their eyes in harmony at the young and their megaphones. I watched it, and I am brave enough to admit how: on a pirated full episode that some Indonesian uploader keeps posting to YouTube, in the window before Fox's takedown arrives. The eye rolling is the tell, by the way. Every generation at that table built its career on being seen by the generation before it. They simply had hallways, expense accounts, and golf to do it with, and they call their version relationships.

I am not interested in defending the racket. Productivity theater is bragging with a timesheet, and bragging, pride, vanity, and envy are a different subject entirely. The squeaky wheel makes noise to extract grease for itself. What I practice, and what I am arguing for here, points the noise in the opposite direction: the volume serves the listener.

Become a flying buttress

A Gothic cathedral stands up because of supports that are not part of the building's interior at all. The flying buttress is an arch of stone standing outside the wall. It catches the outward thrust of the vaulted ceiling and carries it down to a pier in the churchyard, and because the buttress does the holding, the wall can go thin and fill with stained glass. Remove the buttresses and the cathedral keeps its faith and loses its roof.

That is the job description. Become a flying buttress for your company, for your manager, for your executives, for your colleagues. Your manager walks into rooms you will never enter and gets asked what you are doing. A manager who has been kept informed answers in one sentence and moves on, carrying your reputation into the room on your behalf. A manager who has heard nothing from you in three weeks answers with a pause, and the pause is your reputation. Your status reports are the arch that carries that load. Your check-ins carry a different one: the colleague who is quietly drowning gets asked, by you, before the deadline, whether you can take a piece of it. Your celebrations carry a third: when someone lands the contract or gets noticed by the people above, you are the one who brings the cupcake, sends the note, says the compliment with the specific detail in it that proves you actually saw the work. None of this decorates you. The buttress gets no light. The glass gets the light. But everybody who walks past the building can see exactly what is holding it up, and trust, in a working relationship, is mostly that: a support people can see, tested under load, still standing there next quarter.

Exhibit one: the syllabus I never got

My first job after college, in June 1993, was technical customer support at Picture Network International, PNI, in Arlington, Virginia. Our product was called Seymour, and it was the first searchable stock photo database on the internet, the first online portal for licensing stock photography, years before that was an ordinary sentence. I did the work. I was good at the work. What I never did was the rest of the job, because nobody told me there was a rest of the job: the drinks after work, the happy hours, the whole social program running underneath the official one. I assumed competence was the entire curriculum and the building would grade me on it. An office is a society before it is a workflow. It promotes, protects, and remembers the people it knows socially, and it forgets the people it only knows functionally, however well they function. Remote work strips the society away and keeps the workflow, which is how a person can be busy ten hours a day and, socially, not exist.

Exhibit two: the quiet house

My factory settings came from home. I am an only child of two overworking alcoholic parents. Our family was tiny, just the three of us, living some 5,000 miles from the rest of the clan in New Jersey. Nobody doted on me, I doted on nobody, and nobody in that house celebrated anybody. I was never a straight-A student, I never got a 4.0, but A's and B's were simply assumed, the baseline, the water the house ran on. When I watch people on TikTok receive cakes and nights out and rewards for ordinary achievements, I do not sneer at it anymore. I study it the way you study weather in a country you have never visited. I grew up believing high performance was the standard expectation everywhere. The truth is closer to the reverse: most workplaces calibrate to low performance and are startled, genuinely startled, by evidence of the high kind. In a house like mine, celebration was not suppressed. It was never installed. So the whole practice of working loud, the check-ins, the milestones, the remembered names, has to be emulated by me, deliberately, move by move, the way you learn a language your parents never spoke at home.

Exhibit three: the rules I had to unlearn

My father served in the Marine Corps, and one of his ladders ran downhill: if you are not running, walk; if you are not walking, stand; if you are not standing, sit; if you are not sitting, lie down; if you are not awake, sleep. Whatever that conserves in the field, it is a terrible operating system for an office. It teaches a man to take the lowest level of action and visibility available and call the descent discipline. Add the old military rule, never volunteer, and the result is a capable employee who is almost impossible to find. He does not claim the task, offer the help, name the problem, or ask for what he needs until somebody formally orders him into motion.

I am an introvert. I am quiet by nature, and I do not like speaking out of turn. Managing up first felt like exactly that: speaking out of turn, talking above my station, making myself conspicuous. It is actually how I learned to stand up for what I need without turning the request into a confrontation. I report what I am doing. I say what I need. I offer help while the offer can still prevent an emergency. I volunteer. Those are office virtues, even when they violate the older code. Nobody is rescued by help that was never offered, and nobody can trust a colleague who has trained himself to descend, rung by rung, until he is asleep.

Shameless, with receipts

I know bragging from the inside. When I brag, I can be very good at it. In my thirties I became shameless on purpose and pursued shameless self-promotion as a discipline. It was overcompensation, and I knew it even then. I grew up in Hawaii under the local version of an old rule: the protruding nail gets hammered down; the tall poppy gets cut. What felt to me like rudeness was often only directness spoken at a whisper. What felt like standing out was often just standing upright. I was indirect when I needed to be clear, passive when I needed to ask, and braced at all times for the hammer.

Other voices joined the local one. I felt exceptionally white and exceptionally male, and "white entitlement" and "male entitlement" arrived in my head as standing accusations against taking up any room at all. Some of those warnings carried real content. Together they built a cutting-down machine, and I was the one running it, on myself. Self-advocacy took volume precisely because my gauge was miscalibrated. Shamelessness was how I forced the needle up.

Pushback is real. Get too loud and people push back, sometimes at the volume and sometimes at the poppy. Receipts are what make the volume defensible. Everything you claim needs receipts: the delivered page, the dated result, the ranking that moved, the client's own words, the link somebody can open, the number somebody can check. A receipt turns a claim into a report. Volume without receipts is theater. Volume with receipts is how people learn they can trust you.

The practice

Concretely, then, at a volume the other person can hear:

  • Send the status before anyone has to ask for it. Still here. Still on it. Here is where it stands, here is what is next, here is when you will hear from me again.
  • Ask about their load, and mean it as an offer. Are you overwhelmed? What can I take off your desk this week?
  • Volunteer early. Put your hand up while the offer can still change the outcome, not after the emergency has made the decision for everyone.
  • Manage up in the submissive form that actually runs the relationship. I know you are busy. I do not know if you heard. Whenever you have a moment. And if the report is too dense, say the word and I will redo it as bullet points, gladly, without a speck of condescension, because making your work legible to the person above you is your work.
  • Mark every milestone out loud. Somebody landed something, somebody got noticed, somebody shipped. Cupcake, note, compliment, public credit with specifics. People run on this fuel and most workplaces are starving their best people of it.
  • Remember the manager's birthday. Remember the child's name. These are not trivia. They are the proof, filed in advance, that the other person exists for you as a person, which is the entire foundation the trust gets built on.
  • Mirror the client. Match their tempo, their channel, their formality, first date to date night. Mirroring tunes the signal; the status, the help, and the receipts give it something to carry.
  • Bring receipts. Link the page, show the date, quote the result, name the source. Let evidence carry the claim and keep the volume for making sure the evidence gets seen.
  • Calibrate, then keep calibrating. Ask how much people want to hear and how often, watch what they answer and what they ignore, and set your knob to their tolerance, not your temperament.

The whole of it is this: keep your head up and your volume set for the listener. Do the work, and then do the second, smaller work of making the first work visible, useful, and reassuring to the people whose trust your living depends on. Report early, volunteer before the emergency, check on the overwhelmed, celebrate other people in public and in specifics, remember the names and the dates, back every claim with receipts, and unlearn every rule that taught you to disappear politely. Stand outside the wall like a flying buttress, holding up your manager, your colleagues, and your company where everyone can see the support working. That is working loud. It is not bragging, and it never was.
