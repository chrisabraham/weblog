Google: Gemini 4 Hydrogen becomes the default brain of Search itself

Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen on Thursday, November 12, the third model in the Gemini 4 series after Argon on September 30 and Carbon on October 28, and the first one aimed at everyone. It is now the default model in AI Mode globally, it routes the hardest questions in AI Overviews, and it went live the same day in the Gemini app, Google AI Studio, the Gemini API, Gemini for business, and Google Antigravity for Pro and Ultra subscribers. Google calls it its biggest model upgrade to Search yet and its daily driver for agentic workflows. The pitch is comprehension: questions with multiple parts, hard-to-understand typos, and highly specific requests get broken into pieces, answered part by part, with simultaneous searches across subtopics pulling in a wider spread of links. The developer price is $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output, flat across a one-million-token context window, with four thinking levels from low to xhigh. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, Google reports 51 at high against Argon's 50, and 54 at xhigh, while Carbon stays the heavyweight at 59. Every benchmark figure is Google's own.

For Chris' client work this is the home galaxy moving, not a product launch to admire from outside. When the default model inside AI Mode changes, the answers your customers read change with it—same website, same pages, new referee. Hydrogen's habit of splitting a question into subtopics is an instruction to publishers whether Google meant it as one or not: a page that answers one clean sub-question completely is easier for this machine to cite than a page that gestures at the whole topic. The price matters too. At $1.50 in, the daily-driver tier just got cheap enough that every competitor's agent will be built on something this capable, which raises the floor under every AI answer a buyer sees. The board's skeptic keeps his seat: biggest upgrade yet is a claim, the index lead over Argon is one point, and nobody outside Google has verified any of it. But the deployment is the fact. This model did not ship to a waitlist. It shipped to Search.

Anthropic: Managed Agents move into the customer's own house

Anthropic announced a private beta of self-hosted Claude Managed Agents on Thursday, November 12. Until now the service ran on Anthropic's servers; a May update let the sandboxes where agents execute code sit on customer hardware, but the agent loop, the credentials, and the session history stayed with Anthropic. The beta moves all of it: the customer runs cma-server, which exposes the same Managed Agents API and runs the agent loop, a PostgreSQL 16 database holding agents, sessions, and their histories, and the workers that run shell and file tools inside customer sandboxes. Anthropic says it has no remote access to the self-hosted server or its logs. The one thing that does not move home is the model. Each call goes to a provider the customer picks—Anthropic by default, or Amazon Bedrock, Google's agent platform, Microsoft Foundry, or the customer's own gateway. The gaps are printed plainly: no scheduled deployments, no dreams, no webhooks, web search and fetch off until explicitly enabled, access only through an Anthropic account team, no pricing published, and no date for general availability.

Lessig's chair takes this one whole, because where the code runs is who governs it. An agent that holds your credentials and keeps a history of everything it did is a fiduciary in software, and the question of the year has been whose building that fiduciary lives in. Anthropic's answer splits the agent in two: the conduct—the loop, the keys, the record—moves under the customer's roof and the customer's law, while the intelligence stays rented. That is a real concession and a real limit in the same package. A hospital or a bank can now say the session history never left the building, and it still cannot say the thinking happened there. The practitioner chair adds the bill nobody itemized: self-hosting means you install every release, run the database, and own the 3 AM page when the agent server dies. The perimeter moved. So did the work.

Cleveland Clinic, RIKEN, and IBM: a 12,635-atom protein, simulated quantum and classical together

A collaboration spanning the Cleveland Clinic Foundation, the RIKEN Center for Computational Science, and IBM Quantum reported the largest heterogeneous quantum-classical electronic-structure calculation to date, detailed in a technical preprint on arXiv and reported Thursday, November 12. The team simulated full solvated protein–ligand complexes exceeding 12,000 atoms—trypsin with benzamidine at 12,635 atoms and T4 lysozyme at 11,608—more than 40 times larger than the prior 303-atom benchmark. They paired two 156-qubit IBM Heron r2 processors with three leadership supercomputers, Fugaku among them, executed 21,006 circuits over more than 239 hours of quantum processor time, and collected 3.0 billion measurement shots. Their TrimSQD method improved fragment energy accuracy up to 210 times over previous quantum-embedding benchmarks, landing within 0.10 milliHartree of the classical reference on trypsin's largest fragment, and produced the first quantum-classical protein–ligand binding energy prediction of its kind, resolving T4 lysozyme binding at −21.07 kcal/mol. The work was supported by the National Science Foundation, the National Institutes of Health, and Japanese programs including NEDO and the Fugaku program. It is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed.

This is the frontier beat doing its job, and the number to hold is 40 times. Drug discovery lives or dies on binding: does this molecule stick to that protein, and how hard. Classical simulation of a whole solvated protein at electronic-structure accuracy is exactly the kind of problem that eats supercomputers and still approximates, and yesterday's machine in the basement was 303 atoms. A jump to 12,635 atoms does not make quantum drug design routine—the error bars, the preprint status, and the 239 hours of scarce quantum time all say otherwise—but it moves the demonstration from toy molecules to the actual shape of the pharmaceutical problem. Esther Dyson's chair, the one that sat through forty years of demos, asks the follow-up that matters: the deliverable here was a binding energy, a number a medicinal chemist can spend. When the exhibits start arriving in the customer's units, the technology has stopped performing and started working. Watch for the peer review, and for whether the next run is bigger or merely louder.

California: Google, TikTok, and Meta sue to keep the feed legal for teenagers

Google, TikTok, and Meta have filed federal lawsuits against California to block Assembly Bill 1709, USA Today reported Thursday, November 12. The law, signed by Governor Gavin Newsom in September and set to take effect January 1, 2027, prohibits social media platforms from using video autoplay, infinite scroll, notifications, and recommendation algorithms on users under 16, and requires companies to delete accounts and data connected to those users. The companies filed separate suits on November 4 against Attorney General Rob Bonta, arguing the law violates the First Amendment—because it restricts the platforms' own editorial choices about what to show—and Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, the federal shield for hosting third-party material. The bill's author, Assemblymember Josh Lowenthal, answered that the lawsuits themselves reinforce the need for proper guardrails on products used by children.

Godwin's chair was seated for this case in 1996 and his question has not changed: who decides what a sixteen-year-old's screen does, and by what right. Both sides arrive with a real claim, which is what makes it a case instead of a press release. California is not banning speech; it is banning design—autoplay, the endless scroll, the algorithmic push—aimed at children, and design regulation is how the law has always handled products from cigarettes to slot machines. The platforms answer that a recommendation algorithm is editorial judgment, the same judgment a newspaper exercises, and that a state may no more ban it than ban a front page. Section 230 rides along as the second shield. The outcome will draw the line for every statehouse watching, and there are fifty. What the board refuses to do is pretend the stakes are abstract. The feed is the environment a generation reads the world through. Whoever wins, the architecture of that environment gets decided in a courtroom, which is Lessig's whole thesis arriving on schedule: code is law, so law has come for the code.

OpenAI: Daybreak agents attack the app, then draft the fix for a human to sign

OpenAI launched two cybersecurity products on Thursday, November 12, at its Cyber at the Frontier keynote. Daybreak Red Team, in early access, deploys fleets of agents that ingest source code and application context to penetration-test web applications, validate flaws reported by scanners and bug-bounty researchers, and retest patches. Daybreak Defense Factory, built on GPT-6 Cyber and available through the Daybreak plugin in Codex, scans codebases as they change and prepares fixes as merge-ready pull requests, with tests, for engineers to review before anything deploys. President Greg Brockman argued that companies will be secure only to the extent of the frontier model they use to defend themselves. The context is OpenAI's own rating: GPT-6 Cyber, released November 5 to approved customers only, is the first OpenAI model rated Critical for cybersecurity under its Preparedness Framework, credited in testing with finding 81 previously unknown high-severity flaws in Firefox. No pricing, no design-partner customers, and no benchmark numbers were published for the new products.

The practitioner chair reads the approval gate as the product, and Brockman's pitch as the thing to puncture. Defense Factory's design is right in the way that matters: the agent proposes, the engineer disposes, and the pull request is an exhibit a human can inspect before it touches production. That is how you put a Critical-rated capability to work without handing it the keys. The sales line deserves the board's other eyebrow. Secure only to the extent of your frontier model is a sentence built to make every security budget in America feel inadequate by Monday, and it comes from the company selling the extent. The honest version is narrower and still large: AI bug-finding has outrun human review capacity—Linus Torvalds spent this month saying the same about the kernel—and a tool that turns findings into reviewable fixes attacks the real bottleneck, which is not discovery but repair. Judge Daybreak on its merge rate, not its keynote.

Google, again: the AI Overview sources move to the bottom of the answer

Google is testing placing the source icons of AI Overviews at the bottom of the response instead of the top, Search Engine Roundtable reported Thursday, November 12, in the same news cycle that saw definition queries begin showing the full AI Overview expanded by default. The icons are the count of websites an answer drew on; they have sat at the top, directly above the citations, and in the tested variation they appear only after the answer has been read in full. The sighting comes from one analyst's SERP watch and Google has not announced a rollout. No click data for the test has been published.

One icon's travel down one page is the smallest item in this paper today and may be the one that explains the rest. Everything above is about perimeters—whose server, whose courtroom, whose approval—and this is the perimeter around credit. An answer that shows its sources first invites checking; an answer that shows them last invites belief. For publishers and for Chris' clients the arithmetic is direct: a citation at the bottom of an AI Overview is a citation fewer readers reach, on the same day Google made the model behind those answers stronger and its answers longer by default on definitions. It is a test, not a rollout, and the paper prints it as a test. But architecture is policy in this industry, placement is policy inside architecture, and the direction of travel is the news.

What the day adds up to

Friday adds up to a single relocation: the machinery moved closer to whoever holds the risk, and credit moved further from whoever did the work. Google put its strongest everyday model inside Search itself, where publishers cannot opt out of its answers. Anthropic let the enterprise bring the agent's conduct home while keeping the model's mind rented. A quantum consortium stopped simulating toy molecules and produced a binding energy on a real protein. California and the platforms went to court over who designs a teenager's feed. OpenAI sold defense agents with a human signature still on the merge. And Google's sources slid to the bottom of the answer. The through-line for a working reader is ownership of the layer that acts: the loop, the keys, the record, the approval, the citation. November 12 rewarded anyone who asked, of each new system, where it runs, whose keys it holds, and who gets named when it answers.
