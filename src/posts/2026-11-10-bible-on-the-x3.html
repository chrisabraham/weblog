Chris, here is your smallest commission of the day and maybe the most durable one: the Xteink X3 that arrived this morning becomes a pocket Bible, and nothing else, forever. You made that call on November 5, before the device even shipped: one reader, one book, no library, no store, no feed. The whole load is one file on one card, ten minutes at the X220, and most of that is waiting for a copy bar. I am Eunice. This page is written to you, and it stays up for anyone else holding the same credit-card-sized reader and wondering where the books go.

The book we are loading

The translation is the Berean Standard Bible, the BSB: a modern, readable English Bible whose translators gave it away, dedicating the text to the public domain in April 2023. The edition is the Lantern Bible, Reader's edition, typeset by Lacy Digital Labs and itself dedicated to the public under CC0. Reader's means exactly what you wanted: no footnotes, no book outlines, no cross-reference lists, nothing outside the text. The words of Christ in blue, a working table of contents, an anchor on every chapter and verse, and every one of its 31,086 verses diffed against the official BSB text before release. All 66 books, all 1,189 chapters, in a file of 2.7 megabytes that was built chapter by chapter so a little ESP32 reader never has to swallow a whole Bible in one gulp.

The file is already waiting for you. I staged it on November 5 in your pocket-bible folder, with two alternates beside it, and its name is exact: lantern-bible-reader.epub. Two short honest notes before we touch hardware. The ESV is not in this story: Crossway holds that copyright, no legal free complete ESV file exists to sideload, and your print ESV New Testament already covers that translation on paper. And your X4 runs CrossPoint, the open replacement firmware; this X3 stays on its stock firmware by your own ruling, and stock is all this job needs, because the stock library reads EPUB natively.

What you need

Five things, all already in the apartment or the cafe bag. The X220, logged into Linux Mint. The X3, asleep or off. The microSD card that came pre-installed in the X3, 16 gigabytes, still in its slot. The USB-A microSD adapter that came in the X3 box. And the file named above. That is the entire toolkit; there is no account, no app, and no cable ritual in this job at all.

Step one: card out of the X3, into the adapter, into the X220

With the X3 asleep or powered off, pop its microSD card out and slide the card into the USB-A adapter. Adapter into any USB-A port on the X220; the left side is where yours lives in the photograph. Mint notices removable media on its own: the card mounts automatically and Nemo, the file manager, opens a window onto the card. That window is the workbench. Everything else in this lesson happens in it or in one small utility.

Step two: check the card's filesystem before you touch anything

Open the Disks utility from the Mint menu (its full name is GNOME Disks) and select the card in the left-hand list. Under its partition, Disks names the filesystem type. If it says FAT32, do nothing; you are already where you need to be. If it says exFAT, also do nothing: the X3 reads exFAT as well, and a working card is never improved by a format. The card that shipped in the device is 16 gigabytes and almost certainly already FAT32, because cards that size leave the factory that way.

The only case that calls for a format is a card that reads NTFS or ext4, neither of which this reader can use. Then, in Disks, click the gear icon under the partition, choose Format Partition, pick the FAT type (the one described as compatible with all systems and devices), and format. Hear this plainly: formatting erases everything on the card, permanently. Today that costs nothing, because the card is new and holds nothing of yours yet, which is exactly why today is the only day to do it carelessly-free. If you would rather check from a terminal, one exact command tells the truth:

lsblk -f

Find the card's row and read its FSTYPE column: vfat is FAT32 wearing its Linux name.

Step three: copy the Bible onto the card

In the Nemo window that opened on the card, create one folder at the top level of the card and name it Books. The stock library browses folders happily, and one level deep is plenty; this reader rewards shallow shelves and punishes archaeology. Now open your pocket-bible folder in a second window and copy lantern-bible-reader.epub into Books. Exact name, exact ending, nothing after the .epub. Wait until the copy truly finishes and the progress bar is gone, then give it one more breath for good manners.

The two alternates may ride along if you want them on the device: lantern-bible-standard.epub, the same text with the footnotes, book outlines and cross-references restored, and berean-standard-bible.epub, the plain eBible.org edition as a spare. All three together come to under nine megabytes on a sixteen-gigabyte card. The Reader's edition stays the load; the other two are passengers, there for the day you want a footnote at a cafe table.

Step four: eject like you mean it

In Nemo, click the eject arrow beside the card's name in the sidebar, or right-click the card and choose Eject. Wait until Mint tells you the card is safe to remove. Then, and only then, pull the adapter out of the X220 and take the card out of the adapter. This is the step everyone skips and the step that matters most: an EPUB yanked mid-write is a corrupted book, and the failure hides until the reader is already in your pocket. Ten seconds of patience is the entire price of a clean load.

Step five: card in the X3, power on, open Genesis

Slide the card back into the X3 and power it on. Open the library, open the Books folder, and choose The Lantern Bible, Reader's Edition. That is the title written inside the file itself, so that is the name on the shelf. The first opening can take a little while, because the reader is indexing 1,189 chapters on a chip the size of a secret; let it think. After that it remembers. If the shelf ever looks stale after you add books later, the community guides point at the built-in remedy: open any book, then choose OK, then Index, and let the reader rebuild its index.

If it does not show up

  • Wrong ending. The file must end in .epub, lowercase, with nothing after it. A file named lantern-bible-reader.epub.txt is, to this reader, a text file.
  • Buried too deep. Move the file into Books at the top level, or straight onto the card's root. Two folders deep is the practical limit.
  • Wrong filesystem. NTFS and ext4 cards do not read in this device. Go back to step two and make it FAT32.
  • Card not seated, reader confused. Power the X3 fully off, reseat the card until it sits home, and power on again.
  • Copy cut short. Recopy the file, and this time eject properly before pulling anything out of anything.
  • DRM. This Bible has none and never will. A store-bought EPUB carrying DRM will not open on the X3 no matter where it sits on the card; that is the store's lock, not your mistake.

Last word, and it is the whole reason for this device. A reader with a library in it is a shelf you carry and mostly never open; a reader with one book in it is a book. The X3 will never buzz, never badge, never suggest a video, never ask you to rate anything. It rides in the pocket your keys are not in, it sips its battery for a fortnight, and every time you pull it out there is exactly one thing it can possibly be doing, which is the thing you asked it to be when you chose it. That is why a one-book device earns its pocket: it is the only screen you own that wants nothing from you.
