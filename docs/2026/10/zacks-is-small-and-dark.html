Zack's Is Small and Dark

This weblog is about AI search. That is the beat, and the beat has rules, and I am the one who made them. Today is Friday. This post breaks them on purpose: it is an obituary for a bar.

Zack’s stood on Pennsylvania Avenue SE, on Capitol Hill, in the neighborhood I lived in during the years this essay covers, and it has been gone a long time. Nobody wrote its obituary when it closed—nobody wrote anything. There is no address in any record I can find, no opening date, no closing date. So this is late, and what survives is thin: a name on a bar-crawl flyer, and a novel.

To understand Zack’s, start across the street. 330 Pennsylvania Avenue SE is Naval Lodge No. 4, my home lodge—my Blue Lodge. The building went up in 1894 and the lodge has met there since 1895; it is the oldest Masonic building in Washington still in Masonic use, and the lodge itself was chartered in 1805. The lodge room is on the fourth floor, and you reach it in a hand-operated elevator believed to be the oldest operating elevator in the District. For a long time that building was the center of my universe on Capitol Hill. I came to it from Hiram Takoma Lodge No. 10, my original lodge, which I demitted from properly and with full respect; Naval was the lodge I actually attended, the one that became home. And the center held: the building won a property-tax exemption in 2018 to keep it standing where it stands.

A tyled meeting ends. The brothers ride down in the old elevator, go out the door, and cross Pennsylvania Avenue—because directly opposite the Lodge, at 329, stood Hawk n’ Dove, and around it the whole bar row of that stretch: the Tune Inn, Bullfeathers, Finn Mac Cool’s, the Little Pub, the Ugly Mug, Kelly’s Irish Times. And Zack’s. That is what Zack’s was, beyond a neighborhood bar: the lodge’s unofficial annex. Tyled ritual on one side of the avenue; pool and bottles of Bud on the other. Two rooms, one geography.

Small and dark

Hill Mole, the spy novel I began in 2005 in a basement apartment at 14th and A Streets SE, keeps returning to that second room. One entry is simply titled “Zack’s is Small and Dark”: “Zack’s is small and dark and people knew me from the neighborhood…So, after I pulled the documents onto the USB dongle on my keychain I took some time off and dropped by Zack’s for some pick up pool, maybe a burger, and some bottles of Bud.”

The novel understood what the bar did for a man. In “Situational and Locational Drinking” the narrator explains that every place he goes makes him a slightly different fellow, and then he gets to Zack’s: “At Zack’s I am a man. I smoke Camels and play pool. I hang out with the guys and hit on the girls. I eat blue cheese burgers and drink amber bottles of Bud.” Everywhere else it was Stella Artois, Red Stripe, Newcastle—performed selves, chameleon stuff. Zack’s was the unperformed one. Nobody performs in a small dark room where people know you from the neighborhood.

And it was the pressure valve. “A Bite to Eat at Zack’s” opens plain: “I went to Zack’s for a bite to eat because I was hungry and especially thirsty.” Hill Mole’s Washington is a courier’s city—and I had messengered those streets in 1989 and 1990—watching post-9/11 security close over a town that used to be open door to door. The narrator is coming unglued under the strangeness of it. Zack’s is where he goes to get out of the apartment and out of himself.

An author’s ruling, twenty years on, for anyone reading the novel as an archive: any bar scene in Hill Mole—any room where the narrator plays pool, drinks Budweiser, or meets his friends—is probably Zack’s as well, named or not. It was the default room. The unnamed bars are Zack’s without its name tag.

Even the walk home ran through it. “Taking Evasive Maneuvers,” four nights later: “At night walking back from Zack’s or the Metro, I am highly attuned to my environment.” He double-backs, vanishes into alleys, turns the table and follows the follower. Paranoid tradecraft on a Capitol Hill sidewalk, and the fixed point the route is measured from is the bar.

What the record kept

Here is the whole of Zack’s official trace: one line in a mid-2000s Capitol Hill Bunny Hop bar-crawl listing, where it appears in the Pennsylvania Avenue circuit between Finn Mac Cool’s and the Little Pub, with registration across from the Lodge at Hawk n’ Dove. A flyer. That, and the novel.

The rest of the row fared the same, only slower and better documented. Remingtons, the country-western gay bar at 639 Pennsylvania Avenue, is permanently closed; its building became a 7-Eleven and a Sprint store. Phase 1, on 8th Street, the longest-running lesbian bar in America, closed in 2016. By the mid-2010s the entire ecology of that stretch—lodge brothers, bar crawls, gay bars weathering the Marine Barracks across the street—was gone.

Gone asymmetrically, though. Stand at 330 today and the Lodge is still there, elevator and all, exempted and enduring. Cross the avenue for a beer after the meeting and you are standing in the archaeology of the annex. The institution kept its building, its charter, its minutes, its archive. The bar kept nothing, because bars don’t. Bars are kept, or they aren’t.

The written record kept the famous places. Remingtons got its history written; Phase 1 got a preservation thesis. Zack’s got four entries in a spy novel nobody would mistake for history, a line on a flyer, and the people who were known in it from the neighborhood. This post makes five entries, more or less. If you want to visit the bar, the novel is the only door left: all 309 entries of Hill Mole are in the archives at hillmole.com. It is small and dark in there, and the Bud is cold, and people know you. That’s the whole obituary. It’s more than most bars get. It’s less than it deserved.
