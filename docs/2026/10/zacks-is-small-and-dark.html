Zack's Is Small and Dark

This weblog is about AI search. That is the beat, and the beat has rules, and I am the one who made them. Today is Friday, and I am feeling extremely nostalgic today. This post breaks them on purpose: it is an obituary for a bar. Call it the first installment of what may become a periodic feature here—a Chris Abraham nostalgia dump. The tidier home for this material would be chrisabraham.com, but this site is the one called a weblog, after all, and a weblog is allowed its nostalgia dumps. Friday is for this.

Zack’s stood on Pennsylvania Avenue SE, on Capitol Hill, in the neighborhood I lived in during the years this essay covers, and it has been gone a long time. Nobody wrote its obituary when it closed—nobody wrote anything. There is no address in any record I can find, no opening date, no closing date. The dated sightings stop in 2005—a bar-crawl listing from around then and a novel from May of that year—and after that, nothing: no record of the end at all. I looked. It isn’t there. So this is late, and what survives is only what I kept myself: a name on a bar-crawl flyer, a novel, and a page I built out of the novel. Consider this the whole of it, assembled in one place at last: the bar, the lodge across the street, the row, the room, the crew, the game, and the relic.

The center of the universe

To understand Zack’s, start across the street. 330 Pennsylvania Avenue SE is Naval Lodge No. 4, my home lodge—my Blue Lodge. The building went up in 1894 and the lodge has met there since 1895; it is the oldest Masonic building in Washington still in Masonic use, and the lodge itself was chartered in 1805. The lodge room is on the fourth floor, and you reach it in a hand-operated elevator believed to be the oldest operating elevator in the District—a slow, deliberate ascent, which is the correct way to arrive at ritual. For a long time that building was the center of my universe on Capitol Hill.

I came to it from Hiram Takoma Lodge No. 10, my original lodge, which I demitted from properly and with full respect; Naval was the lodge I actually attended, the one that became home. And the center held: the building won a property-tax exemption in 2018 to keep it standing where it stands. The Lodge keeps its charter, its minutes, its archive. Institutions are built to be remembered by. Remember that; it matters at the end of this.

A tyled meeting ends the way it has ended for two centuries: the business is done, the door is opened, and the brothers ride down in the old elevator and go out into the night. And then—this is the geography the whole essay hangs on—they cross Pennsylvania Avenue. Because directly opposite the Lodge, at 329, stood Hawk n’ Dove, and around it the whole bar row of that stretch of the avenue. The meeting had an annex, and the annex had a pool table.

The row

The row had a division of labor, the way a good street does. Hawk n’ Dove, at 329, was a mainstay—the registration point for the Capitol Hill Bunny Hop, the pub crawl that stitched the whole avenue together, and neighborhood furniture besides. The Little Pub was the same kind of furniture: unglamorous, reliable, there. Bullfeathers, Finn Mac Cool’s, the Ugly Mug, Kelly’s Irish Times—the crawl moved through them all, and so did we.

The Tune Inn, at 331, was my late-night place, part diner, part restaurant, part burger joint, and had been since the mid-90s or earlier. The address took the Tune Inn name in 1947, after earlier lives that included a Prohibition speakeasy, and it holds what is often cited as the second-oldest post-Prohibition liquor license in the District. Cheese fries and a last drink, at an hour when the rest of the avenue had gone dark. Hold the Tune Inn in mind, too: it is the row’s survivor, and we will come back to it.

But I ended up at Zack’s, and I ended up there because of the pool. Zack’s was a stop on the inter-bar pool circuit: clubs traveled bar to bar, playing for awards, and the small dark room held its own on the tour. A bar you drift into is one thing. A bar your club plays out of is another. Zack’s was the home table.

And one correction the record should carry, because everything else about the place is reconstruction: Zack was a real man. The name was not a brand or a bit of signage poetry—Zack was a real dude, and the bar was a person’s name on his own place. I won’t invent him a surname or a biography; the archive doesn’t carry one, and this essay has made a discipline of not inventing what it cannot show. He gets what the bar got: his name, kept.

Small and dark

Hill Mole, the spy novel I began in 2005 in a basement apartment at 14th and A Streets SE, keeps returning to that room. One entry is simply titled “Zack’s is Small and Dark”: “Zack’s is small and dark and people knew me from the neighborhood…So, after I pulled the documents onto the USB dongle on my keychain I took some time off and dropped by Zack’s for some pick up pool, maybe a burger, and some bottles of Bud.” That is the whole establishment in one breath: small, dark, known, pool, burger, Bud. The novel is a book about surveillance and secret Washington, and its most honest sentences are about a bar.

The knowing was procedural, and it had a liturgy. The bartender at Zack’s knew that when I arrived, the first bottle of Bud—in the brown glass bottle—would be stood at the bar and guzzled straight down, as if it were an electrolyte drink, as if it were a Gatorade. He would have the second bottle already there when the first was done, and the second one I would sip. The first bottle of Bud whenever I arrived at Zack’s was a sacrificial guzzle: the evening could not properly start until something had been poured out, even if it was poured straight down. That is what “people knew me from the neighborhood” means when it stops being a sentence in a novel—a man behind a bar who stages your arrival without a word being said.

The novel understood what the bar did for a man. In “Situational and Locational Drinking” the narrator explains that every place he goes makes him a slightly different fellow, and then he gets to Zack’s: “At Zack’s I am a man. I smoke Camels and play pool. I hang out with the guys and hit on the girls. I eat blue cheese burgers and drink amber bottles of Bud.” Everywhere else it was Stella Artois, Red Stripe, Newcastle—performed selves, chameleon stuff; the entry that follows in the book is literally about the chameleon in him, the subtle tells that give a man away. Zack’s was the unperformed one. Nobody performs in a small dark room where people know you from the neighborhood. The chameleon rested there. It may have been the only place he did.

The novel states the rule; the affectation ledger shows it running for decades. In the wider world my cigarettes were a cosmopolitan rotation—Dunhill Blue, Marlboro Red, Silk Cut, Gauloises, Gitanes, whatever the room’s latitude suggested. At Zack’s it was Camels, and only Camels, and the beer was bottled Budweiser and only bottled Budweiser, the first one guzzled. And the doctrine predates Zack’s by a decade. From 1993 to 1995 I was a local at Strangeways, at 2830 Wilson Boulevard in Clarendon, by the Courthouse Metro—not an Iota guy, not a Galaxy Hut guy, a Strangeways guy—and at Strangeways my drink was Newcastle Brown Ale, assigned by the room as surely as the Bud was assigned by Zack’s. Every room assigned its drink. The novel just wrote down the law.

Strangeways deserves its clause, being a prequel and not a second essay. It stood as Galaxy Hut’s near-twin on that stretch of Wilson; IOTA Club & Café was built next door at 2832 in 1993, and within a few years Strangeways had closed and IOTA had expanded into its physical space—IOTA, which lasted until 2017. And the two rooms sharing that wall were musical opposites: IOTA became a rockabilly and Southern rock room, importing independent Southern rock and rockabilly acts, while Strangeways was British to its bones—Britpop, new wave. So “not an Iota guy” was never a drinks preference; it was tribal, and it was musical, and when IOTA expanded through the wall it read as the other tribe taking the fort. Eve Zibart was covering that scene in the Washington Post as early as September 17, 1993, in a column called “Intelligent Life in the Galaxy.” I was never an Iota guy, and Iota literally swallowed my bar. Even the name arrived pre-loaded: I knew Strangeways from The Smiths—Strangeways, Here We Come—though I was studying at Norwich, in England, in those years. The reference reached me via Morrissey, not via the prison down the road that the album was named for. A British-prison name, carried by a Manchester band, hung on a Wilson Boulevard bar, decoded by an American local through a record. The cosmopolitan rotation again—it goes all the way down.

And it was the pressure valve. “A Bite to Eat at Zack’s” opens plain: “I went to Zack’s for a bite to eat because I was hungry and especially thirsty.” Hill Mole’s Washington is a courier’s city—and I had messengered those streets in 1989 and 1990, when you could still shoot into the Capitol cafeteria for grub and air conditioning between deliveries—watching post-9/11 security close over a town that used to be open door to door. The narrator is coming unglued under the strangeness of it, seeing patterns, feeling watched, drinking to get out of the apartment and out of himself. Zack’s is where he goes. Small and dark turns out to be a prescription.

The room itself, on a good night, was the Hill before the Hill got sorted. My crew in it—my posse, and yes, we said posse: it sounds stupid now, but in the 1990s and 2000s everybody called their group their posse, so it wasn’t cringe then, and whether it’s cringe now I leave as an open question—were Min Le, David Gelles, Oliver Uberti, Andrew Curry, and Sherif Abushadi, and the list is not closed, because a room like that is never fully recalled in one sitting, and the names that surface at 3 a.m. will be added here where they belong. Andrew Curry was the only one of them my own age; Min, David, Oliver, and Sherif were all nine or ten years younger than me, and the room never once made it a thing. Around us: lesbians and gay men, neighborhood locals, kids in their twenties and super old people, lodge brothers off a meeting, pool clubs in off their tour, all of it in one small dark room on Pennsylvania Avenue. Some nights were Gallaudet nights, when the deaf students had the place and the bar went quiet except for what I can only call the sea-lion sound of deaf people laughing—a whole room talking with its hands, and the only sound in it was joy. I have been in fancier rooms. I am not sure I have been in a better one.

Where the crew is now, as best as the room’s far end can report it: David Gelles is a bigwig at The New York Times. Oliver Uberti is a bigwig in infographics and illustration. Min Le makes children’s books and comic books. Andrew Curry was studying to become a therapist, and might be one by now. Sherif Abushadi is the open file—something with training centers, or a school; he may even live in Turkey now. He lives a magical life, and the record is content to leave it exactly there. If one of them finds this page by searching his own name some night—and men do—then the homing beacon worked, and the room has one more night in it.

And one absence, which I still find funny: Mark Harrison never came to Zack’s. Mark is my best friend, and later the two of us started a company together—but my entire 2000s, right up until that company, were basically Mark-free. The Zack’s years are a whole decade of my life my best friend isn’t in. He has the rest of the archive. The small dark room he never got.

And the posse never leaked, either. Through the whole Zack’s era there was no crossover between those men and the rest of my life—not the Lodge, not work, not anybody. A sealed world, running alongside the Mark-free decade, sealed the same way. The seal broke exactly once, years later, and barely: while I was dating Betsy, from 2013 to 2017, Andrew Curry would get invited to the Christmas get-togethers with her and that circle. Andrew—the only one of them my age—was also the only one who ever crossed over. The age-peer got through the door. Nobody else ever did.

And it went deeper than a guest list. I was completely compartmentalized—my words for that era, and the era’s governing fact. I never spoke of St. James, my church on the Hill, to anyone in the posse. I never spoke of being a Freemason—a Scottish Rite 32°, at that—to anyone in the posse, nor to anyone at New Media Strategies, where I worked in those years. I never talked about church. I never invited anybody to church. So picture the crossing honestly: a lodge brother walks out of a tyled room at 330 Pennsylvania and into a bar where nobody knows he is a Mason; a churchman drinks his Bud in a room that has never heard of St. James; the posse knows neither the lodge nor the church nor Mark nor the job nor any of it. The compartments were total, each with its own door. Zack’s was not an escape from that architecture. It was one of the sealed rooms—maybe the warmest one.

An author’s ruling, twenty years on, for anyone reading the novel as an archive: any bar scene in Hill Mole—any room where the narrator plays pool, drinks Budweiser, or meets his friends—is probably Zack’s as well, named or not. It was the default room. The unnamed bars are Zack’s without its name tag. And the ruling has evidence behind it: run the search yourself, across the whole aggregated text of the novel, and “pool” and “Bud” turn up only inside the Zack’s entries. The default room leaves fingerprints.

Even the walk home ran through it. “Taking Evasive Maneuvers,” four nights later: “At night walking back from Zack’s or the Metro, I am highly attuned to my environment.” He double-backs, vanishes into alleys, turns the table and follows the follower. Paranoid tradecraft on a Capitol Hill sidewalk, and the fixed point the route is measured from is the bar. Every spy needs a home station. His served burgers.

What the record kept

Here is the whole of Zack’s official trace: one line in a mid-2000s Capitol Hill Bunny Hop bar-crawl listing, where it appears in the Pennsylvania Avenue circuit between Finn Mac Cool’s and the Little Pub, with registration across from the Lodge at Hawk n’ Dove. A flyer. The city’s histories and its press kept nothing of Zack’s at all. What survives of the place, I archived myself: the novel, and “Memories of Zack’s from the Mid 2000s,” a page on my own site where I gathered the Zack’s cluster of entries together—A Bite to Eat at Zack’s; Zack’s is Small and Dark; Situational and Locational Drinking; The Chameleon About Me; Ceding My Food Choice; A Spidey Sense; Fucking Haole; City Rules of Engagement; Fire Can Lick Behind These Eyes—under an introduction that begins: “A lot had changes in DC over the last 30 years but I found a great bit of memories from the mid-2000s that I wrote around 2005 when I was going to Zack’s Bar to play pool, drink, and hang out with my friends a lot.” The page even carries a photograph captioned “Zack’s Former Location.” The archivist is the author. There was nobody else for the job.

Full disclosure, since this is an AI-search weblog even on its day off: that page is what Google’s AI Mode summarized when I asked the machine about myself. The machine already knows Zack’s. It learned it from my archive, and it improvises around the edges. Which means the beat and the bar meet on that page: everything I write here about AI search says the machine answers from what it can find, and what it can find about Zack’s is what one of its regulars bothered to keep. Citation is just memory with better distribution.

The rest of the row fared the same, only slower and better documented. Remingtons, the country-western gay bar at 637–639 Pennsylvania Avenue, closed on April 14, 2014, after more than thirty years; its building became a 7-Eleven and a Sprint store. Phase 1, on 8th Street, the longest-running lesbian bar in America, closed in 2016. The histories kept those two: Remingtons got its retrospective, Phase 1 got a preservation thesis. The Lodge endures across the street, exempted and chartered and minuted. And the Tune Inn—the one survivor—still stands at 331 with its 1947 name and its old license, still serving the avenue. By the mid-2010s the rest of the ecology of that stretch—lodge brothers crossing after the meeting, bar crawls, gay bars weathering the Marine Barracks across the street, a pool tour working the row—was gone.

Gone asymmetrically, though. Stand at 330 today and the Lodge is still there, elevator and all. Cross the avenue for a beer after the meeting and you are standing in the archaeology of the annex. The institution kept its building, its charter, its minutes, its archive. The bar kept nothing, because bars don’t. Bars are kept, or they aren’t.

The last relic

The written record kept the famous places. Zack’s got four entries in a spy novel nobody would mistake for history, a line on a flyer, a page its own regular built to hold them, and the people who were known in it from the neighborhood. This post makes five entries, more or less. If you want to visit the bar, two doors are left, and I built them both: the Zack’s entries gathered on the Memories page, and all 309 entries of Hill Mole in the archives at hillmole.com. It is small and dark in there, and the Bud is cold, and people know you.

And one thing made it out of the room. When Zack’s closed, the regulars each bought a house cue at a discount—the bar distributing itself, piece by piece, into the hands of the people who had kept it. Mine was a beautiful emerald-green breakdown, a two-piece cue, and I was so proud of it I could have framed it. Then came the first game I ever played with it, and I scratched. She has been Molly McScratch from that game to this one. She is still mine, in her case, in a storage unit in Arlington—the one relic of Zack’s known to survive. The Lodge keeps its minutes; the novel keeps the room; Molly keeps the game. Small and dark, all three. That’s the whole obituary. It’s more than most bars get. It’s less than it deserved.
