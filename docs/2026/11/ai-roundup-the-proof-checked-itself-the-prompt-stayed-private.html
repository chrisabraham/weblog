AI Roundup: The Proof Checked Itself, the Prompt Stayed Private

Friday's exhibits divide into two piles, and the piles are the story. In the first pile, machines did hard work and left the receipts where anyone can check them. DeepMind's agents cut the cost of quantum algorithms and proved each cut correct in a formal theorem prover, then open-sourced the library so strangers can run the proofs themselves. Perplexity gave developers a way to search the web as it stood on a chosen past date, stored copies and all, which is a receipt for what a page said before it changed. In the second pile, the platforms kept the receipts. Google announced a December core update in advance and described it in one careful sentence. OpenAI told advertisers, plainly, that they will never see the prompts or the intent behind their ChatGPT ads, only the totals. The frontier and the storefront are moving in opposite directions on the same question, and the question is the one this paper keeps returning to: what can be checked, and by whom?

DeepMind's Gemini agents cut quantum algorithm costs, and Lean checked the work

Google DeepMind and Google Quantum AI reported on November 23 that teams of Gemini 4 agents found cheaper quantum algorithms for several scientific problems, with each result verified in Lean, a formal theorem prover. The headline number is inertial confinement fusion: the computing cost of a key circuit fell 11.8 times, and the memory requirement dropped from 5,650 to 4,338 error-corrected qubits. Simulating a common light-sensitive molecule became eight times cheaper. For Cytochrome P450, the enzyme that breaks down most medicines in the human body, the improvement was 2.5 times, with memory down from 481 to 333 qubits. A routine called arcsin, used to load numbers into quantum states, got a 290-fold speedup, and one implementation of Grover's search got 2.57 times faster. DeepMind released the Lean library, the problem specifications, and the verified solutions as open source. The paper is on arXiv as 2611.09822.

The limits are printed with the results, and this paper prints them too. These circuits still need more resources than the first error-corrected quantum computers are expected to offer, humans chose the problems and supervised the work, and a preprint is a preprint until referees have had their turn. The method is the news. The circuits are too large to test on a classical computer or on today's small quantum processors, so the team proved correctness mathematically instead of running anything. Yesterday this paper carried Linus Torvalds' warning that a fix can add bugs of its own. DeepMind's answer to the same problem is a proof checker that accepts no argument, only a proof. Agents propose, at scale and at speed, and a machine that cannot be charmed verifies. That division of labor is the most honest architecture for AI research yet published, and every lab working on expensive, untestable claims now has to explain why its verification is weaker.

Perplexity's Time Machine lets agents search the web as it was

Perplexity announced Time Machine on November 25 for its Search API and Context API. A new parameter, snapshot_as_of, limits results to stored copies of pages as they stood at or before a chosen date, up to 365 days back, at no extra charge beyond standard API pricing. The announcement covers the developer APIs; Perplexity does not say the consumer app changes.

“Freeze the web with Perplexity Time Machine.”

A search engine with a memory changes what an agent can be asked to do. Today an agent researching a price change, a policy edit, or a quietly rewritten product page sees only the current version and has to take the past on faith. With a dated snapshot, the agent can cite what the page said in March against what it says now, and a human can check both. For Chris' client work the use is immediate: AI search audits live and die on citations, and citations move. A tool that can ask what an answer engine's sources looked like last quarter turns an argument about a traffic drop into an exhibit. The open question is coverage—whose stored copies, how complete, and how a site owner disputes a stale snapshot—and Perplexity has published the feature before publishing those answers. The feature itself is the right shape. The web needed a wayback machine built for machines, and now it has one with an API key.

Google finished November, then preannounced December before December started

Google's November 2026 core update finished rolling out on November 24. About an hour later, Google did something it has not done before: it preannounced the next one. The December 2026 core update sits on the Search Status Dashboard with a scope and no start date. Google says it focuses on how its systems understand and rank relevant content, including for longer and more complex questions, and on improving personalized results for people who have personalization turned on. It applies to all locations and languages. In the same stretch, Google published documentation explaining how AI Overviews and AI Mode choose the pages they link to.

Read the preannouncement as a calendar gift and nothing more. A business owner now knows a ranking change is coming in December, knows it targets long, complex questions—the exact shape of an AI Mode query—and knows personalization will matter more, which means two customers can ask the same question and get different answers with different citations. What Google has not given is a date, a signal list, or a way to test. The practical move is the boring one, and boring wins here: record this week's rankings and AI citations as the baseline, keep publishing pages that answer long questions completely and in plain language, and wait a full week after the rollout ends before judging anything. The sites that panic-edit in December will have no baseline and no verdict, only motion.

OpenAI told ChatGPT advertisers the prompts stay private, full stop

Asked by Adweek whether ChatGPT advertisers would get reports on the prompts or the inferred intent behind their ads, OpenAI's CTO of applications, Vijaye Raji, said they should not expect them, Search Engine Journal reported on November 26. The mechanics explain the answer. OpenAI's ad system uses a model it calls the orchestrator, which reads a conversation, writes its own search queries from the buying intent it finds there, and matches ads to those queries. Advertisers never see the queries their money matched. Ads Manager reports totals only: impressions, clicks, spend, click-through rate, average cost per click, average CPM, and conversions. Advertisers can supply context hints, and OpenAI states in its own guide that hints are not targeting rules and cannot enforce geography, schedules, or exclusions.

Every search marketer alive was trained on the search terms report—the list of what people actually typed, which is where campaigns were pruned and new products were discovered. That instrument does not exist in ChatGPT ads, and OpenAI has now said it is not coming. Part of the reason is real privacy: a ChatGPT conversation can hold medical worries, money trouble, and family business that no advertiser should ever read. Part of it is architecture: the matching query is written by OpenAI's model, so even a shared query would be the machine's paraphrase, not the customer's words. For a business buying these ads, the consequence is discipline. Landing pages, offers, and conversion tracking carry the whole load, because the conversation that sent the customer stays a black box. Measure what arrived. The why is not for sale.

The COLLUDE Act reached the Senate calendar, carrying Section 230 with it

The Senate Commerce Committee reported the COLLUDE Act, S.69, on November 20, and the bill now sits on the Senate Legislative Calendar as Calendar No. 724. No floor vote is scheduled. The bill would remove Section 230 liability protection from a provider that restricts access to political speech because of a governmental request, unless the request serves a legitimate law enforcement or national security purpose. It would also convert the protection from a shield that ends lawsuits early into an affirmative defense the provider must prove. The bill has one sponsor and no cosponsors. The Knight First Amendment Institute argues it could chill speech rather than protect it, and NetChoice and Public Knowledge each urged the committee to reject it.

This is the Lessig seat and the Godwin seat arguing over the same 26 words, and both chairs have a point worth printing. A government that can quietly ask platforms to take down political speech has found a way around the First Amendment, and naming that practice in a statute is a real act. A statute that punishes platforms for complying, judged after the fact in litigation they must now fund to the end, gives platforms a reason to leave disputed speech up and disputed speech down for the wrong reasons—fear of the lawsuit, not judgment about the speech. Calendar placement makes the bill eligible for a vote. It does not schedule one, and a single-sponsor bill with no cosponsors is a position paper until the floor says otherwise. This paper will print the vote if one comes. Until then the item travels as what it is: the year's clearest draft of how Congress currently wants to rewrite the rule the open internet was built on.

Forward calendar: Linux 7.4-rc5 is expected Sunday, November 29; Google's December core update has no start date yet; the COLLUDE Act awaits Senate floor time that has not been scheduled.
