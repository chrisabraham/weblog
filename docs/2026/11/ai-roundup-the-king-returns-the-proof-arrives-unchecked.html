AI Roundup: The King Returns, the Proof Arrives Unchecked

Tuesday graded three kinds of trust. NVIDIA's board reversed itself in twelve days and handed the company back to its founder, then spent $12.9 billion on the open-model commons. Anthropic's model produced a mathematical proof that a machine checked and no human has yet read. Google published, in plain documentation, the mechanics of how its AI answers pick the pages they link to—and the measurement firms filled in the part Google left out: the links are moving down the answer, and the local businesses rising fastest are the ones whose Business Profile points at the right website. Underneath, a lab at MIT found the brain doing what the multilingual models do, holding meaning in one shared code under twenty-one different languages. Authority is being re-delegated in every direction at once. The exhibits are unusually good today.

NVIDIA reinstated Jensen Huang, then agreed to buy Hugging Face for $12.9 billion

NVIDIA's board voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, CEO, and director, reported November 24. The reversal follows his November 12 resignation. An independent investigation, with input from senior United States government officials, concluded that Huang does not pose a national security risk. Shares jumped 31 percent on the news. A day later, NVIDIA signed a definitive agreement to buy Hugging Face, the dominant hub for open models and datasets, for $12.9 billion, with closing expected in the first half of 2027 subject to regulatory approval.

The reinstatement closes the strangest leadership episode in the AI buildout: the supplier every lab depends on lost its founder to an investigation and got him back inside two weeks, cleared. The Hugging Face deal is the larger fact. The hub is where open weights live, where small labs publish, and where the ecosystem keeps its shared shelf. Putting that shelf inside the dominant GPU vendor is an enclosure question in Lessig's exact sense—the architecture of open AI changes when its town square gets a landlord, whatever the landlord promises. Regulators cleared the man quickly. The purchase of the commons gets the longer look, and it should.

A Claude-written proof claims VP ≠ VNP, checked by Lean and by no human yet

Anthropic's formal-mathematics repository now carries a paper and a Lean 4 proof, written by an internal research Claude model, claiming that VP ≠ VNP over every field. VP and VNP are the algebraic analogues of P and NP: the proof says polynomial-size arithmetic circuits cannot compute the permanent or the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial. Anthropic is explicit about the limits. This does not settle P versus NP, and no human mathematician has checked the proof yet. The Lean build itself can be verified independently, by anyone, today.

“No human mathematician has checked the proof yet, though the Lean build can be verified independently.”

That sentence is the new shape of a frontier claim. For a century a proof arrived with a reader attached—someone who had followed the argument and staked a name on it. This one arrives with a checker instead: a theorem prover that accepts or rejects each step mechanically, indifferent to reputation. The question stays open in the honest way, labeled in the post and labeled here. A machine-checked proof that no person has read is either a milestone or a very elaborate typo, and the beautiful part is that anyone with a laptop can start finding out which. This paper will print the verdict when mathematicians render one.

Google documented how AI answers choose their links, and the first link is sliding down

Google published two documentation pages on November 24. A new Search Central page walks through how AI Overviews and AI Mode choose the pages they link to: ranked results and their snippets feed the model, related fan-out searches may add more pages, and the finished answer links to pages that back up what it says. Only indexed pages that can show a snippet qualify. A second page gives Google-Extended its own documentation, spelling out what the token covers—training of the foundation models behind Google's commercial AI products, and grounding in those products outside Search—and what it does not. Grounding in AI Overviews and AI Mode is a separate Search Console setting, a block does not keep a domain from being named or linked, and in AI Ranked's study only 3.8 percent of AI Overview source sites block the token at all. Peec AI supplied the measurement that makes the documentation matter: across more than 88 million chats, the share of AI Overviews putting their first inline link in the opening third fell from 76.8 percent on September 21 to 51.8 percent by November 20, with sharp drops around October 9 and October 30.

Read the three exhibits together and the citation economy gets concrete. Eligibility is mechanical: indexed, snippet-able, or invisible. Inclusion is competitive: the answer links to pages that support its sentences. Value is positional and falling: a citation that sat at the top of the answer in September now tends to sit a third lower, where fewer eyes reach it. The work for a business follows directly. Keep every money page indexable with a clean snippet, state the claim the answer needs in words the answer can quote, and track link position in AI Overviews as its own number, apart from cited-or-not. Yesterday's paper covered personalization splitting the answer by reader. Today the answer's own links are moving inside a single version. Both shifts reward the same discipline: be quotable early, in the page and in the answer alike.

The Business Profile website link tracked with 76 percent more AI impressions

Localo, a local-marketing platform, analyzed 2,134,260 AI Overview and AI Mode impressions from Search Console between May 18 and November 16. Average daily AI impressions for the local business sites in its network rose from 6,223 in the first week to 31,592 in the last, a 5.1-fold increase, while ordinary Search impressions grew 2 percent. The sharpest jump landed on October 30 and 31, across hundreds of unrelated businesses at once, which points to a change on Google's side. Impressions tied to the website set in a business's Google Business Profile grew 10.2 times between late October and mid-November, rising from 5 percent to 22 percent of the impressions Localo could classify. Among 1,066 matched sites, the ones the profile pointed to had a median 76 percent more AI impressions across two three-month periods. Sites whose profile pointed elsewhere had a median 9 percent fewer.

The labels belong on this one, and Localo prints them itself: the data is an association inside one company's network of mostly small sites, not proof that the link causes the visibility. The practical value survives the caveat, because the check costs five minutes. The website field in a Business Profile is set once, often years ago, often at an old domain or a social page. If Google's AI shows the profile and the profile hands the searcher a website, the field is a ranking asset whether or not the full 76 percent is causal. Open the profile, read the field, fix it if it is stale. That is the cheapest item in this paper and the one most likely to pay a client this week.

MIT found one shared neural code for meaning across 21 languages

Researchers in MIT's Fedorenko Lab, at the McGovern Institute for Brain Research, combined brain imaging from speakers of 21 languages—English, Vietnamese, Russian, Marathi, and Arabic among them—with the internal representations of multilingual language models. The encoding models they built predicted how the brain responds to sentences in a given language. The striking result: a model trained on brain responses in one language predicted brain activity for a different language it had never been trained on. Meaning, in the brain, appears to sit in a common code underneath the surface differences of tongue.

The frontier beat earns its keep on days like this. A multilingual model holds meaning in a shared geometry because that is the efficient way to build one, and the brain, measured independently, appears to do the same. Two systems that arrived at the same architecture from opposite directions—evolution and engineering—are evidence about the shape of meaning itself, not just about either system. The question the study leaves open is the good one, and this paper leaves it open with it: how much of that shared code is language, and how much is the world the language points at?

GPT-6 designed proteins that bound in the lab, with no biology model involved

OpenAI reported on November 24 that its general-purpose GPT-6 models can design protein structures and sequences using reasoning and ordinary code, without a specialized biology model in the loop. In laboratory tests, the designed proteins showed evidence of binding across five targets. OpenAI frames the result as an intrinsic capability of the frontier model: the same system that writes and reasons can be pointed at protein engineering and produce candidates that survive first contact with a bench.

Five binding targets is a beginning, labeled as OpenAI's own report, with peer review and replication ahead of it. The direction is what earns the slot. Specialized science models—built for proteins, for chemistry, for genomes—have carried the lab beat for years. A general model walking into that territory and binding on five targets suggests the specialist advantage is narrowing from the top down. Biocomputing was on this board's frontier list for exactly this collision: the general intelligence and the wet lab are becoming one beat, and the labs know it.
