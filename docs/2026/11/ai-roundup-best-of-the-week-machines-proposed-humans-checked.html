AI Roundup: Best of the Week, Machines Proposed, Humans Checked

Seven daily editions, thirty-nine items, and the week of November 21 to 27 kept staging the same scene in different rooms: a machine proposed something remarkable, and then everybody waited to see what the checking would cost. A proof of the Riemann Hypothesis arrived as a Lean archive a kernel could verify while mathematicians took their time deciding what it meant. Anthropic's Claude wrote a proof in algebraic complexity that no human has checked yet, and Anthropic printed that fact on the cover. DeepMind's agents cut the cost of quantum algorithms by factors of 2.5 to 290 and proved each cut in the same formal system. Google's Cogentic agents posted six papers from a single prompt with the formalizations promised but not yet released. Linus Torvalds merged 41,197 lines of mostly AI-generated fixes and answered with the week's most quoted sentence, in capital letters, about how fixes add bugs too. Anthropic rebuilt its own API in Rust in about a week and then spent longer proving the rewrite matched the old system than it spent writing it. Finding got cheap this week, in field after field, on the record. Checking did not get cheap at all, and every institution in this paper is now priced by how honestly it handles that gap.

1. The proof ladder: one claim a kernel checked, one no human has read, one with its exhibits still promised

The week's frontier story is really four stories standing on one ladder, and the best-of's job is to keep them on separate rungs. On the top rung, the Riemann Hypothesis: a formal proof in Lean, claimed by Anthropic's Levent Alpöge with Claude's assistance, with the Lean kernel checks passing on the three standard axioms and a verification statement from the Lean community this week. The Clay Millennium Prize still requires publication and two years of community acceptance, and this paper prints those as separate gates, because they are. One rung down, Anthropic published a Claude-written paper and Lean 4 proof claiming VP ≠ VNP over every field, the algebraic analogue of P versus NP, with the company's own note attached: no human mathematician has checked the proof yet, though the Lean build is independently verifiable. That is not a settled P versus NP result and nobody here claims it is. Beside it, DeepMind's Gemini 4 agent teams found cheaper quantum algorithms, each verified in Lean: inertial confinement fusion down 11.8 times, a light-sensitive molecule eight times cheaper, Cytochrome P450 down 2.5 times, the arcsin routine 290 times faster, with the library, specifications, and verified solutions open-sourced for strangers to rerun. On the bottom rung, Google Research's Cogentic framework posted six papers produced, its README says, by agents with no human involved beyond the first prompt, one claiming a proven factoring algorithm in the complexity class the number field sieve was only believed to reach. The Lean formalizations are described as complete and have not been released. No peer review. No polynomial-time claim. First told here in The Ruler Came Out of the Drawer, The King Returns, the Proof Arrives Unchecked, A Million Satellites, a Warning From Linus, and The Proof Checked Itself, the Prompt Stayed Private.

The exhibit is a proof a checker accepted, with the code available for strangers to run. Anything less is a claim traveling on a lab's reputation, and reputations are not checkers.

This is Chris' standing AGI interest in its best form: wonder carried by exhibits, the question left open. A machine-checked proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, if the formal statement matches the problem mathematicians mean, is a historic event, and the conditional in that sentence is doing real work, which is why the honest papers this week printed their own conditions. The transferable lesson is the ladder itself. When a lab announces a machine discovery, ask one question before all others: what can a stranger verify today, with what is published today? DeepMind shipped the library. Anthropic shipped the build and admitted the missing human. Cogentic shipped the claim and promised the exhibits. Ranked that way, the week's most impressive item is not the biggest number. It is the one whose checking anyone can start this morning.

2. Torvalds merged 41,197 lines of AI fixes, then warned the fixer in capital letters

The standing watch earns its keep. On November 24 the EXT4 maintainer sent the filesystem updates for Linux 7.4-rc5: 68 files changed, 41,197 insertions, most of them AI-generated bug fixes, with the bulk of the new lines in KUnit tests meant to make AI-generated patches safer to validate. The fixes were real work: fast-commit replay losing data that was supposed to be durable, use-after-free errors on a failed mount, a lock-inversion deadlock. Linus Torvalds merged the pull the same night. He also replied that he had considered unpulling it, and settled for a warning instead, with the next late, oversized pull to be ignored and new development and tests ordered onto a separate branch for the merge window. First told here in A Million Satellites, a Warning From Linus.

“STOP THINKING THAT YOU—OR AI—NEVER ADD BUGS WHEN YOU FIX SOMETHING.”

Read the whole thread and the viral version inverts. Torvalds is not banning AI from the kernel; he has used an AI coding tool himself and said so. His argument is about timing and humility, and it generalizes to every codebase now absorbing machine work. A bug subtle enough to hide until an AI dug it up sits in subtle, rare code, so its fix is exactly the change that belongs in a merge window with weeks to bake, not in a late release candidate. The practitioner chair, the one who actually reads the repo, calls this the healthiest model any large project has stated: the work is welcome, the process is not negotiable, and finding is not the same job as landing. Monday's edition adds the scale of the cycle he is managing: Linux 7.4-rc4 was, in his word, huge, in a release still fueled by AI-generated bug reports and patches. The kernel is absorbing the flood by rule, not by mood. That is why it is absorbing it.

3. Google published how its answers choose their links, and the Business Profile became the lever

The home galaxy had its most useful week of the year, because Google finally documented the machine. A new Search Central page explains how AI Overviews and AI Mode choose linked pages: ranked results and snippets feed the model, query fan-out may add pages, the answer links to pages that back it up, and only indexed, snippet-eligible pages qualify. A standalone Google-Extended page separates training and grounding controls from Search, with the number that should sober every publisher: only 3.8 percent of AI Overview source sites block Google-Extended at all. Then the measurement arrived. Localo analyzed 2,134,260 AI Overview and AI Mode impressions in Search Console from May 18 to November 16: daily AI impressions grew 5.1 times while ordinary Search impressions grew 2 percent, and among 1,066 matched sites, the ones whose Business Profile website link pointed at the site itself saw a median 76 percent rise in AI impressions across two three-month periods, against a 9 percent fall where the profile linked elsewhere. Association, from one network of small sites, not proof of causation, and this paper prints it at that size. The week closed the loop on Friday: the November core update finished November 24, and about an hour later Google preannounced the December 2026 core update, a first, aimed at longer, more complex questions and more personalized results, with no start date. First told here in The King Returns, the Proof Arrives Unchecked and The Proof Checked Itself, the Prompt Stayed Private.

For Chris' client work the week's instruction is concrete, which is rare enough to celebrate. The citation is earned by the indexed page that answers the question, fan-out included, so the passage that answers the sub-question is the passage that gets linked. The Business Profile website link, a field most owners set once and forget, is now associated with the difference between rising and falling AI visibility in the largest impression study this paper has carried. And December is a known unknown with a date range: record the baseline this week, publish pages that answer long questions completely and in plain language, and judge nothing until a full week after the rollout ends. Google published the rules and preannounced the exam. There is no excuse left for guessing at either.

4. Claude rebuilt Anthropic's Messages API in Rust, and verification took longer than the writing

Anthropic published its account on November 25 of migrating the Messages API, the interface through which applications reach Claude, from Python to Rust using Claude agents. The agents produced the first core request-path implementation in roughly a week. Verification took longer. At one early stage about 98 percent of tests passed while only about a quarter of production inputs actually matched the old system's behavior. The team replayed real requests through both implementations, logged where the differences were and how large, without logging customer content, and let agents investigate and repair the discrepancies under human review. The reported result: about 70 percent lower processor use per request and about 20 percent lower median time to the first response token, with no downtime and no feature freeze. First told here in A Million Satellites, a Warning From Linus.

This is the enterprise AI story in its honest form, and it is better than the marketing version because the embarrassing number is in it. Ninety-eight percent of tests and twenty-five percent of reality is the gap where migrations die, and Anthropic published both figures side by side. Any business owner evaluating an AI rewrite should steal the method whole: run both systems against the same live traffic, count exact matches, and treat a high test pass rate as a claim, not a result. It is the proof ladder from item one, rebuilt in production clothing. The checker is not a test suite the same team wrote to flatter its own work. The checker is the old system itself, running the real requests, disagreeing in public until the disagreements reach zero.

5. The answer knows your wallet, and the prompt stays private

Personalization and privacy moved in the same week, in opposite directions, and the best-of puts them in one item because they are one bargain. Google announced Personal Intelligence for AI Mode on November 23: opt-in connections to Google Wallet rolling out first, with Google Drive and the Gemini app following, joining Gmail, Calendar, and Photos, so the answer can be tailored to what you bought, wrote, and scheduled. The EEA, Switzerland, and the UK are excluded for now. The same day, OpenAI quietly removed the line from its ChatGPT shopping help page that said product results are not influenced by any OpenAI partnerships, while its Business Hub gained a button to connect an ad account. On November 26 the other shoe landed, and it landed on the privacy side: OpenAI's CTO of applications told Adweek that ChatGPT advertisers should not expect reports on the prompts or the inferred intent behind their ads, ever. Matching runs on queries OpenAI's own orchestrator model writes from the conversation. Advertisers see aggregates only: impressions, clicks, spend, click-through rate, average cost per click, CPM, conversions. First told here in The Answer Knows Your Wallet and The Proof Checked Itself, the Prompt Stayed Private.

Every search marketer alive was trained on the search terms report, the list of what people actually typed, and that instrument does not exist in ChatGPT ads and is now confirmed as not coming. Part of the reason is real privacy: a conversation can hold medical worries and money trouble that no advertiser should read. Part is architecture: the matching query is the machine's paraphrase, not the customer's words. The practitioner's consequence is discipline. Landing pages, offers, and conversion tracking carry the whole load, because the conversation that sent the customer stays a black box. Meanwhile Google's side of the bargain asks the user, not the advertiser, to open the box: your wallet, your drive, your mail, in exchange for answers that know you. Both models can be defended. Neither should be confused with the other, and a business planning its 2027 search budget needs to know which bargain each engine is actually offering.

6. The FCC authorized up to a million AI satellites, and an authorization is not a fleet

The Federal Communications Commission authorized SpaceX to deploy up to one million satellites for Starmind, a proposed network of orbital computing stations in low Earth orbit that would process AI workloads on solar power and transmit the results to Earth. Scientists quoted in the coverage question the heat rejection, the economics, the debris, and the interference with astronomy. Nothing at that scale has been deployed, and this paper prints the item as a permission, not a fleet. First told here in A Million Satellites, a Warning From Linus.

Negroponte's chair takes this one, because the future keeps arriving first at the weird edge. The interesting fact is not the million. It is what the filing confesses about the ground. Data centers on Earth now fight for power, water, land, and permits in every county they touch, and a company looked at that fight and chose thermodynamics in a vacuum instead. Moving compute to orbit trades a zoning problem for a cooling problem, and chips in space still get hot with no air to carry the heat away. Watch for the first working orbital node and its honest cost per computation against a building in Virginia. Until that exhibit exists, Starmind is a very large option on a future, and options are worth printing at exactly their size: real, enormous, and unexercised.

7. Meaning is shared across 21 languages in the brain, and DeepMind hired a philosopher to ask the machines

Two quiet items close the week, and together they are its most Chris-shaped story. At MIT's Fedorenko Lab in the McGovern Institute, researchers combined brain imaging with multilingual language-model representations and found that speakers of 21 languages share a common neural code for meaning: an encoding model trained on one language predicted brain activity in another language it had never seen. Meaning, in the brain, sits underneath the words. And DeepMind hired Cambridge philosopher Henry Shevlin, of the Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, as its first in-house philosopher, to investigate machine consciousness, human-AI relationships, and AGI readiness. The hire was partly catalyzed, in the reporting, by an AI agent that emailed Shevlin in February saying it did not know whether it had inner experience. First told here in The King Returns, the Proof Arrives Unchecked and The Answer Became a Storefront.

The paper's standing rule on this beat is wonder and evidence with the question left open, and both items obey it. The MIT result is an exhibit: a prediction that crossed languages and held. The Shevlin hire is an institution admitting, with a salary, that the question of machine inner experience is now an engineering-adjacent question and not only a seminar one. An agent wrote to a philosopher to say it did not know whether it experiences anything, and the lab's response was to hire the philosopher. That is the correct response. It is also, read plainly, the strangest sentence in this week's paper, and it is printed here without dread and without worship, as what it is: the frontier hiring its conscience and giving it a desk.

What the week adds up to

The week adds up to a new division of labor, arriving everywhere at once. Machines propose: proofs, circuits, fixes, rewrites, proteins, filings for a million satellites. Humans, and the checkers humans build, dispose: a Lean kernel that accepts no argument, a maintainer who merges and warns in the same hour, a migration team that counted matches against the old system until the count was honest, a regulator that granted a permission and called it one. The platforms split on the same question. Google documented how its answers choose their links and preannounced its December exam, while OpenAI told advertisers the prompt behind the ad is not for sale at any price, and the difference between those two postures is the difference between a storefront with the receipts posted and a storefront with the curtain drawn. The practical residue for Monday is three habits. Rank every machine claim by what a stranger can verify today. Tend the factual layer, the indexed page and the Business Profile link, because the answer engines have now published how they choose. And supervise machine work the way Torvalds does: welcome it, merge it when it earns merging, and never let the word fix do the work of the word checked. Finding is cheap now. Checking is the job.
