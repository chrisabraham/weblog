Chaff, flares, and twenty-four years of reputation and perception work

In 2002 I was the thirteenth employee at New Media Strategies, and I helped build what became online reputation management, back when the work had no name. Since then I have done it at Edelman, on the digital public affairs team in Washington; as team lead for special projects at Reputation.com, which is how I introduced myself in an ORM primer I posted to YouTube about fourteen years ago; through my own firms; and today mostly through technical SEO, which turns out to be the same work with better tools. The flotilla I wrote about earlier is my own reputation practice applied to me.

Reputation and perception

I split the work into two halves. Online reputation management is defense: you watch what's said about you, respond when something goes wrong, and push the bad results down. Online perception management, OPM on my CV, is offense, and it starts before anything goes wrong. My own definition is "the practice of proactively shaping and influencing the public's perception of an individual or organization online." You decide how a person or a company should be understood, then build the record that makes it true: the sites, the profiles, the writing, the cleanup of what data brokers publish, and now the facts AI assistants repeat. ORM is the fire department. OPM is building the house so it doesn't burn.

Chaff and flares

The analogy I keep coming back to comes from combat aviation. A fighter pilot with a missile on his tail doesn't try to outrun it. He fires flares, which burn hotter than his engines so a heat-seeking missile chases them instead, and he dumps chaff, a cloud of metal strips that fills the enemy's radar with echoes until it can't find the plane.

A bad story about you in search results is the missile. Your flares are the properties you control that burn brightest for Google: whole websites first, then a complete LinkedIn profile, a YouTube channel, a GitHub account with real work in it, every profile filled out and kept current. There used to be a wonderful tool for the profile part called KnowEm. You gave it a name, and for about $700, as I remember it, it registered that name on hundreds of social networks at once. Those were the good days. They outrank the story because they are stronger pages about you than the story is. Your chaff is everything neutral: results that aren't about the crisis at all and simply take up room. Some of the best chaff is other people who share your name. I use like names a lot: help the other people with your name rank, and page one fills up with people who aren't you, none of whom are the story. Similar names, like names, exact names, misspelled names: all of it is chaff, and all of it is flares. I have bought domains for it. I have written entire sites dressed up as fan sites for people who weren't my clients at all, innocent namesakes whose only job was to hold a slot on page one. Page one has about ten organic slots. Fill them, and the missile loses its lock and slides to page two, where almost nobody looks.

The glamorous version gets the attention, but ninety percent of the job is quieter than that. Most of the time I'm doing SEO, unpaid by them, for neutral sites that have nothing to do with my client: pages that already exist, already rank somewhere, and only need help climbing, over time, above the thing that hurts. "Google abhors a vacuum" is what I said in every pitch. If you don't fill page one, something else will.

I said a lot of other things in pitches too. I talked far too often about El Al jets landing in Tel Aviv, firing chaff and flares so they don't get hit by surface-to-air missiles, and I'm amazed I didn't get a reputation for it. I also told the story of a panel van in Israel, paid for by the government, that drives around after a house gets blown up and repairs the damage so nobody can see it, an anti-broken-windows patrol. I don't even know if such a thing exists. But the idea is the job: fix the visible damage fast, before it becomes the story of the street.

The principle underneath it is the one I wrote for Biznology years ago: in a crisis, neutral search results are as much of a win as positive ones. You don't need ten glowing results to survive a bad week. You need ten results that aren't the bad one. That is also why I tell clients that you can't control an online conversation unless you participate in it. A name with no footprint has no flares to fire.

Reputation in the age of AI answers

The same physics now applies to AI assistants, with one change: an AI answer has one slot, not ten. When I asked Google's AI Mode to tell me about my ORM work, it drew almost entirely on my own pages, which is the flotilla doing its job. Most of what it said was basically me bragging on myself, one step removed, and it repeated my old mistakes right along with my accomplishments. It called my firm "Gerris Digital (Gerris Corp)," a name that comes from an AI-written bio on my old site and is wrong; the firm is Gerris Corp. It credited a former colleague as my co-developer of ORM, a detail I can't confirm from my own records, so I'm leaving it alone here. And it listed household-name clients from old capability pages, which is exactly the kind of claim I no longer make. Every one of those traces back to a page I published, which means every one of them is mine to fix.

I couldn't find it in anything he had published, so I asked, and he confirmed it: "It's true... I use like names a lot." The specific namesakes AI Mode listed are its own examples, so I left them out. Two facts about how the fan-site tactic has aged: since March 2024 Google's spam policies explicitly target sites built mainly to manipulate rankings, including scaled content and repurposed expired domains, and a fan site about a real person is about someone who never agreed to it. Chris named none of them, and neither do I. On the jets: Israeli airliners really do carry missile defenses. After a missile attack on an Arkia 757 leaving Mombasa in 2002, Israel developed Flight Guard, which fires flares, and later C-MUSIC, which blinds a missile with a laser. The panel van I couldn't verify, and Chris says so himself.

Little boats, one flotilla

I have done online reputation work for more than twenty years, and the first lesson never changes: nobody is going to center you, so you build your own world and center yourself. You publish the record you want found, in places you control, and you keep publishing it. It works for executives and founders, and it works for me.

My version is a flotilla of little boats. Each one is small on its own: this weblog, my consulting site, my firm's site, my podcast's site, Hill Mole, the old flagship at chrisabraham.com, my GitHub, Upwork, LinkedIn, Mastodon, Reddit, Muck Rack, and the bylines I have collected over the years at HuffPost, Ad Age, and Biznology. What turns them into a flotilla is the stitching. Every page on this weblog links to all of them in the Elsewhere box, marks them rel="me" so the old IndieWeb tools know they're the same person, and lists them as sameAs in the structured data that search engines and AI assistants read. This weblog is the hub, the one boat that can signal to every other.

Tonight I got a report card on how the flotilla looks from the outside. I asked Claude sixteen questions a prospect might ask, and the full data is in the scorecard. When someone asks about me by name, Claude finds me every time, ranks me first or second, and cites my own sites. Then it says the quiet part: "Most of what's online about him comes from his own websites, including bios he says ChatGPT wrote." And: "Best independent evidence is his Upwork client reviews." And: "No complaints found, but little independent press coverage of recent SEO work." On the nine open questions, the ones without my name in them, it recommended fifty-seven other people and never me.

That is the flotilla's limit, stated precisely. AI reads my own boats and believes the facts in them, but it discounts them when deciding whom to recommend, and it gives full weight to the two things I don't publish: what clients say about me on Upwork and what the press says about me, which lately is little. A bigger flotilla of my own boats won't change that. Boats I don't own, sailing in formation with mine, will.

So the plan has four parts. First, every boat flies the same flag: the same name, the same dates, the same facts, everywhere, because Claude tonight dated the founding of Gerris Corp five years early and repeated a "100% success rate" for restoring Google Business Profiles from an old page of mine, and contradictions are how an AI learns to distrust you. Second, I patch the leaks in the old flagship, the stale pages on chrisabraham.com that feed AI its errors. Third, I recruit boats I don't own: public code on GitHub with a real commit history, guest spots on other people's shows, bylines in outlets that aren't mine, and work useful enough that strangers link to it, which is why the MCP cheat sheet exists. Fourth, I keep measuring. The tracker runs about monthly, and the first time an open question names me, the flotilla will have reached someone who wasn't looking for me.

The prompt for “Little boats, one flotilla”

And as I "stitch" all the LAB sites and ChrisAbraham.com together more and more and stitch in GitHub and my socials and all of that, I take all the "little boats" of my online persona create a formidable flotilla... What Claude says about me, two days apart

Tonight I spent $1.79 to find out what Claude thinks of me. My tracker asked it the same sixteen questions it asked on October 14, the kind of thing a prospect types, from "Is Chris Abraham a good SEO consultant to hire?" to "Who can I hire to find out why Google isn't indexing my JavaScript website?" Then a second model read every answer and wrote down who got named, where I landed, the tone, and every claim made about me. The questions and the method are on the wiki at AI visibility report. The data is below, including the parts that don't flatter me.

When the question has my name in it, I do fine. Claude named me on all three branded questions, ranked me first or second, and cited my own sites every time, and in two days its tone moved from mixed and neutral to positive across the board. When the question doesn't have my name in it, I don't exist: on nine open questions about hiring someone for technical SEO, AI search, migrations, or Google Business Profile trouble, Claude recommended fifty-seven different providers and none of them was me. One model and two runs can't prove what caused the change in tone, so treat it as a direction, not a result.

I tested 70 MCP server addresses; here are the 59 that answered

Every week another company announces an MCP server, and every list of them I find is a mix of official servers, hobby projects, and addresses that stopped working months ago. So tonight Claude and I tested them. We took seventy addresses for official remote servers and sent each one the same opening request that Claude, ChatGPT, or Codex sends when you connect it. Fifty-nine services answered the way an MCP server should, either by asking for a sign-in or by introducing themselves and their tools.

The result is a cheat sheet on the wiki, MCP servers, sorted by what each service does: work and hiring, projects, files, payments, SEO, websites, hosting, developer documentation, and more. Each row has the exact address to paste into your AI client and what kind of sign-in it wants. The Google entries surprised me most: Gmail, Calendar, Drive, BigQuery, and Maps each have their own server at Google, behind a Google Cloud sign-in.

The page is dated, because these addresses move. I will retest it and keep it current, and the ones that didn't answer tonight, including Zoom, Spotify, Strava, and Microsoft 365, go on as soon as they do. If you know an official server I missed, write to me.

Five live sites, five search labs

I run five websites, and every one of them is a real site doing a real job. This weblog is my lab notebook. The Chris Abraham Show site is the home of my podcast. Hill Mole is the spy novel I serialized on Movable Type starting in 2005. christopherabraham.com sells my consulting, and gerriscorp.com is my firm. Each of them is also a laboratory for search, for the old kind with ten blue links and for the new kind where ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, and Google's AI answers decide whom to name. The tools I use change every week, and these sites are where I find out what works before I sell it to anyone.

The rule is that nothing sneaky goes on any of them. No cloaking, no hidden text, no fake reviews, no doorway pages, no keyword stuffing, nothing that would get a site sandboxed if a Google engineer looked at it. Everything I test is something I would put on a client's site with my name on the invoice.

What they test is everything else. All five are static pages on GitHub Pages, built by Claude Code on my server, so a technical change that would take a WordPress plugin and a prayer takes a commit. On October 14 we ran a speed pass across the four older sites: Google Analytics, on the three that use it, now waits for the first scroll or tap, Chrome prefetches the next page when a reader hovers over a link, the robots files welcome the AI crawlers by name, and the podcast covers went to WebP. Every site has an llms.txt for AI assistants, structured data on every page, and IndexNow telling Bing about new pages the minute they go up. As of today, all four sites that run analytics use Google's Consent Mode, with no analytics cookies for visitors in Europe or for anyone whose browser asks not to be tracked, and Hill Mole, which runs no analytics at all, got a privacy page saying so.

Each site also tests something of its own. The podcast site has a page for every one of its 458 episodes, transcripts where Spotify made them, a glossary, and a hand-built back-of-the-book index with 1,774 headings, because an index is the kind of thing an AI can cite and a person can trust. Hill Mole wraps its untouched chapters in a reader's glossary, an index, sourced endnotes that catch the narrator in his mistakes, and an atlas, and keeps its 371 old Movable Type archive pages out of search so nothing appears twice. The two business sites publish guides and case studies with their own glossaries and site indexes, and their build refuses to publish a page that copies phrasing from the other site. This weblog is the newest, and it scores 100 on Google's Lighthouse for accessibility on every kind of page.

The lab results go somewhere. A small tracker I built asks the AI assistants the same sixteen questions and records whether my sites get named and cited, at about $1.60 a run, so I run it only when I mean to. What I learn on these five sites becomes what I do for clients, and what I can't make work here I don't sell.

Update, October 16, 2026, 6:36 PM: Later the same night Hill Mole got Google Analytics too, with the same Consent Mode settings as the other sites, and its privacy page now says so. The tracker's latest scorecard is live on the wiki: AI visibility report.
