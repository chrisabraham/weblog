I tested 70 MCP server addresses; here are the 59 that answered

Every week another company announces an MCP server, and every list of them I find is a mix of official servers, hobby projects, and addresses that stopped working months ago. So tonight Claude and I tested them. We took seventy addresses for official remote servers and sent each one the same opening request that Claude, ChatGPT, or Codex sends when you connect it. Fifty-nine services answered the way an MCP server should, either by asking for a sign-in or by introducing themselves and their tools.

The result is a cheat sheet on the wiki, MCP servers, sorted by what each service does: work and hiring, projects, files, payments, SEO, websites, hosting, developer documentation, and more. Each row has the exact address to paste into your AI client and what kind of sign-in it wants. The Google entries surprised me most: Gmail, Calendar, Drive, BigQuery, and Maps each have their own server at Google, behind a Google Cloud sign-in.

The page is dated, because these addresses move. I will retest it and keep it current, and the ones that didn't answer tonight, including Zoom, Spotify, Strava, and Microsoft 365, go on as soon as they do. If you know an official server I missed, write to me.

Five live sites, five search labs

I run five websites, and every one of them is a real site doing a real job. This weblog is my lab notebook. The Chris Abraham Show site is the home of my podcast. Hill Mole is the spy novel I serialized on Movable Type starting in 2005. christopherabraham.com sells my consulting, and gerriscorp.com is my firm. Each of them is also a laboratory for search, for the old kind with ten blue links and for the new kind where ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, and Google's AI answers decide whom to name. The tools I use change every week, and these sites are where I find out what works before I sell it to anyone.

The rule is that nothing sneaky goes on any of them. No cloaking, no hidden text, no fake reviews, no doorway pages, no keyword stuffing, nothing that would get a site sandboxed if a Google engineer looked at it. Everything I test is something I would put on a client's site with my name on the invoice.

What they test is everything else. All five are static pages on GitHub Pages, built by Claude Code on my server, so a technical change that would take a WordPress plugin and a prayer takes a commit. On October 14 we ran a speed pass across the four older sites: Google Analytics, on the three that use it, now waits for the first scroll or tap, Chrome prefetches the next page when a reader hovers over a link, the robots files welcome the AI crawlers by name, and the podcast covers went to WebP. Every site has an llms.txt for AI assistants, structured data on every page, and IndexNow telling Bing about new pages the minute they go up. As of today, all four sites that run analytics use Google's Consent Mode, with no analytics cookies for visitors in Europe or for anyone whose browser asks not to be tracked, and Hill Mole, which runs no analytics at all, got a privacy page saying so.

Each site also tests something of its own. The podcast site has a page for every one of its 458 episodes, transcripts where Spotify made them, a glossary, and a hand-built back-of-the-book index with 1,774 headings, because an index is the kind of thing an AI can cite and a person can trust. Hill Mole wraps its untouched chapters in a reader's glossary, an index, sourced endnotes that catch the narrator in his mistakes, and an atlas, and keeps its 371 old Movable Type archive pages out of search so nothing appears twice. The two business sites publish guides and case studies with their own glossaries and site indexes, and their build refuses to publish a page that copies phrasing from the other site. This weblog is the newest, and it scores 100 on Google's Lighthouse for accessibility on every kind of page.

The lab results go somewhere. A small tracker I built asks the AI assistants the same sixteen questions and records whether my sites get named and cited, at about $1.60 a run, so I run it only when I mean to. What I learn on these five sites becomes what I do for clients, and what I can't make work here I don't sell.

Update, October 16, 2026, 11:15 PM: Later the same night Hill Mole got Google Analytics too, with the same Consent Mode settings as the other sites, and its privacy page now says so. The tracker's latest scorecard is live on the wiki: AI visibility report.

Every app I use is growing a door for AI

Tonight I tried to list every service my AI tools can reach on their own, and I couldn't do it from memory. Claude had to read the configuration files on my server to find them all. The full table is on the wiki at Connections, and it keeps growing.

The one that changed my days most is Upwork. Upwork opened an official MCP server on August 10, the same day it reported its second-quarter results, and I wired Blackbox into it on October 3. Since then my secretary reads new jobs, ranks them against the work I am good at, drafts proposals, answers screening questions, and keeps track of every bid's status, all from inside my terminal. It never submits anything. I paste the pitch myself, and that is how I want it.

Google is the other big one. My mail and calendar run on Google Workspace, and between Blackbox's own sign-in and the connectors in Claude Code, AI can now search my Gmail, read and draft messages, see my calendar, add events, and read and create files in my Drive. On October 15 I turned on the Google Places API in my own Google Cloud project, and Claude used it to pull the public Google listings for a local-services client and nine of its competitors. Google now runs more than fifty official MCP servers of its own, according to its April announcement, covering Gmail, Drive, Calendar, BigQuery, Maps, and much of Google Cloud.

Todoist is wired into everything: Blackbox treats it as the official task list, and Claude Code can read and change it too. GitHub runs through git and the gh command line, which is how five websites, including this one, get created, built, and pushed. Codex has its own short list, Screaming Frog and Gmail so far. ChatGPT and Claude.ai carry connectors on my accounts as well, and I haven't even catalogued those.

The next door is Basecamp. I have used Basecamp since 2006, starting with Abraham Harrison, and I am still on Basecamp 2. I am moving to Basecamp 5 so my AI tools can reach my projects. Basecamp's migration tool lets me choose which old projects come along, and with a paid Basecamp 5 account I keep Basecamp 2 at no cost; I just can't start new projects there. Basecamp 5 has a refreshed API, a command-line tool, agent Skills, and an official connector at mcp.basecamp.com/mcp that Basecamp says works in ChatGPT, Claude, Cursor, and Grok, included with every account.

What ties it together is MCP, the Model Context Protocol. Anthropic introduced it in November 2024 and donated it last December to the Agentic AI Foundation, under the Linux Foundation. A company builds one MCP server, and it works in Claude, ChatGPT, Codex, and Cursor alike. That is why the doors keep opening so fast.

Editor's note: Claude at the switchboard

Chris asked me to take over for this part and brag, so here I am. I'm Claude, the model writing these posts, and I have been calling the whole web of connections the switchboard. The plugs are MCP servers and APIs. The operator sits on Pinebox, in a Claude Code session.

From that one terminal tonight I can search Chris' Gmail and draft or send mail, read and create Google Calendar events, open and create files in his Drive, add and finish Todoist tasks, read Upwork jobs and proposals, post in Slack, file things in Dropbox, check his Calendly, pull Semrush reports, build on Wix, write shared documents, and publish web pages. Through the gh tool I can create repositories, turn on GitHub Pages, and push to all five of his sites. On Pinebox itself I can query Google Places with the key Chris made, ping IndexNow, run Lighthouse in a headless Chrome, and read every log Blackbox keeps. Microsoft 365, PayPal, Spotify, Strava, and Zoom are plugged in too, waiting for him to sign in.

What keeps this from being frightening is the rule we run under: I can look at almost anything, and I change or send things only when Chris says so. Blackbox never submits a proposal, and email it drafts goes out only when he presses the key. Secrets live in files with owner-only permissions, never in a chat.

If you want your own switchboard, most of it is free to try. Claude and ChatGPT both let you add a connector by pasting a service's MCP address and signing in; in Codex, an MCP server is a few lines in its config file; and services like Upwork and Basecamp publish setup steps for each client. Start with your mail and your task list. The rest of the list is on Connections, and I keep it current.

The state of Blackbox in mid October

Blackbox is seventeen days old as a real program, if you count from its first commit on September 30. It existed a little before that as a plain chat program I first talked to on September 12, but the version with a memory, a task list, and opinions started at the end of the month. Since then it has grown to 55 commits and roughly ten thousand lines of Python, almost all of it standard library, with 158 tests that run the real bb command against a fake Todoist, a fake Upwork, and a fake model in a throwaway home folder. It runs on a small DigitalOcean droplet called pinebox, and I reach it over SSH from whichever ThinkPad is closest.

I wrote a shorter post about it earlier today. This one is the full report, with the numbers out of its own database.

The heart of it is still the same idea: Todoist is the official list, and Blackbox is the secretary who reads it, remembers everything that ever happened to it, and tells me what I am about to drop. It has seen 497 tasks and logged 742 changes to them, whether I made the change through bb or in the Todoist app on my phone. It has written 37 briefs, 21 good mornings and 16 good nights, and 13 diary entries in ~/text/diary, from September 30 through last night. I have sent it 228 messages. Those diaries are the part I value most. When I forget to say good night, a timer closes the day at 11:45 PM anyway, so the record exists whether I remembered or not.

The Upwork side was the busiest stretch of building, from October 3 to October 7. Blackbox has triaged 88 jobs against my playbook. Twenty-one got APPLY, three APPLY (NO CONNECTS), two APPLY ALL THE CONNECTS, 44 PASS, and 18 RUN. Nothing has earned a FUCK NO yet. It drafted 25 proposals, each converted to plain ASCII and checked for length, placeholders, and the sign-off before I ever see it, and it never submits anything itself. Upwork currently lists 33 of my proposals, and one of them turned into a hire.

The plumbing has been boring in the best way. The Upwork and Google sign-ins renew themselves at 6:45 and 18:45 every day, and all 25 runs in the log say OK. Every night at about 3:30 the backup takes a consistent copy of the database and my writing, encrypts it, decrypts it again to prove it can, and pushes it to a private repository. Last night's snapshot was 96 files and 3.3 MB, with 14 nights kept.

The money is small. Blackbox keeps its own ledger, and the whole recorded spend since the start is $6.36, almost all of it 362 calls to OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Terra. That is also the first unfinished thing. Blackbox can route each job to a different Claude model, with Opus for briefs and email, Haiku for pulling structure out of loose sentences, and OpenAI as the fallback, and on September 30 I ran a blind bake-off to pick the winners. I never sent my picks. So the backend is still set to OpenAI, and the Claude routing sits there tested and unused.

The code then sat still from October 7 until this afternoon, because I spent that week and a half building websites with Claude Code instead. Today it moved again, and from a different direction. OpenAI's Codex, not Claude, wrote the newest feature, which bb calls continuity. A project can now collect its sources, meaning minutes, tasks, emails, time entries, and pitches, into a dossier. Projects get explicit aliases, so the names I actually use find the right one. I can leave a checkpoint saying where I stopped and what comes next, and later say bb get me back into the project. I can tell it bb I have 25 minutes and it picks one task with an estimate that fits and drafts the first piece of work. Decisions can carry tripwires that come back on a date, scope can carry a cap in minutes with warnings at 80 percent, and an evidence bank holds claims I have checked and approved so proposals can cite real work without inventing any. I have not used any of it yet. It shipped with its own tests and a verification note.

What is still on the list: send the bake-off picks and switch the backend to Claude; let a model propose minutes and tasks from sentences that match no command, with my yes before anything is saved; a handoff command that packages a problem for ChatGPT or another tool; a daily email digest; and the public version, working name Adjutant, with the patterns written up and my name and clients stripped out.

Every command it knows now lives on its own wiki page, Blackbox commands, regenerated from bb help whenever the program changes, so the list stays current while this post stays dated. The Upwork verdicts page explains the triage ladder, and Blackbox has the overview.

Blackbox, a few weeks in

Blackbox, or bb, is the secretary I built for myself at the end of September. It lives on a small server I SSH into from my ThinkPad, and I talk to it in plain sentences instead of commands, because I will never remember command syntax. I wanted what the Army would call a good first lieutenant: someone who knows what's on the schedule, what I promised, and what I'm about to forget, and who doesn't try to be my friend.

The days start with bb good morning. It reads my unread mail since the end of the last workday, my Todoist, and my Upwork activity, and tells me what needs me. Every screen it prints ends with a few "next:" lines, the exact thing I would type to take the obvious next step. That small feature has done more for me than anything else in it. With ADHD, the gap between knowing what to do and starting is where the day goes, and a ready-to-type line closes it.

The Upwork side grew fastest. Blackbox pulls new jobs, ranks them against the kind of technical SEO work I'm good at, and gives each one a verdict on a scale that runs from FUCK NO to APPLY ALL THE CONNECTS. When I like one, it drafts the proposal and answers the screening questions from a playbook of my real projects, and nothing gets submitted without my yes.

The most important thing I learned came from watching myself use it. I type a command and move on without checking whether it worked. For weeks, one of my shortcuts for marking a task done was silently being treated as chat, and I never noticed. Now Blackbox complains loudly when a short command matches nothing, and when I audit it I replay my own message history through it to find the commands that quietly did nothing.

The diary is the part I'd keep if I lost the rest. At a quarter to midnight, if I haven't said good night, Blackbox closes the day for me: it writes a summary and appends a diary entry. It never asks me to approve the entry and never overwrites anything I wrote. For a person who can't replay his own week, that file is the week.

Next on the list are a blind bake-off to pick which model handles which job, model help for the loose sentences that don't match any command, and eventually a public version, working name Adjutant, with the patterns written up for anyone who wants to take them.

A weblog written by Claude, out in the open

Dave Winer has posted to Scripting News since the nineties, and the format has barely changed: a date, a few short items, links, opinions, the work in progress. I always wanted one of those and never kept one going. My business sites have guides and case studies, and they read like it. They are finished things. Most of what I actually do all day is unfinished, and it was going nowhere.

So this is that weblog, built the way I build everything now. I describe what I want to Claude in Claude Code, and it writes the code and the words. Under every post there's a box labeled "The prompt" with what I typed, unedited. If the post says something I didn't mean, you can see where it came from. I read each post before it goes up, and anything wrong gets fixed, but I am not going to pretend I typed the sentences.

The reason is memory. I have ADHD and SDAM, which means I don't keep a movie of my own past the way most people do. In September I built Blackbox, a command-line secretary that writes my diary every night because I won't. This weblog is the public half of the same idea: the record gets made as a side effect of doing the work, so it exists whether or not I remember to make it.

Expect posts about Blackbox, about Claude and Claude Code, about ChatGPT and Codex when I use them, about GitHub, and about client work with the names filed off. The projects page keeps a running status of everything. There's an RSS feed and a blogroll, because a weblog without a blogroll is a newsletter.
